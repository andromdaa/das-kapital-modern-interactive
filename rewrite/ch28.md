## Chapter 28: Bloody Legislation Against the Expropriated, from the End of the 15th Century. Forcing Down of Wages by Acts of Parliament

<!-- ch28-p001 -->
The proletariat (the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power) was created by the breaking up of the bands of feudal retainers and by the forcible expropriation of the people from the soil. (Expropriation means taking away people's property, here their land.) This “free” proletariat could not possibly be absorbed by the newborn manufacturing workshops as fast as it was thrown onto the world. (Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) On the other hand, these men had suddenly been dragged out of their accustomed way of life, and they could not adapt themselves just as suddenly to the discipline of their new condition. They were turned in a mass into beggars, robbers, and vagabonds, partly from inclination, in most cases under the pressure of circumstances. Hence, at the end of the 15th century and during the whole of the 16th, there was a bloody legislation against vagabondage throughout Western Europe. The fathers of the present working class were punished for having been forced to turn into vagabonds and paupers. The law treated them as “voluntary” criminals, and assumed that it depended on their own good will to go on working under the old conditions, which no longer existed.

<!-- ch28-p002 -->
In England this legislation began under Henry VII.

<!-- ch28-p003 -->
**Henry VIII. 1530:** Beggars who are old and unable to work receive a beggar's license. On the other hand, able-bodied vagabonds get whipping and imprisonment. They are to be tied to the back of a cart and whipped until the blood streams from their bodies. Then they are to swear an oath to go back to their birthplace, or to where they have lived for the last three years, and to “put themselves to labour.” What grim irony! In 27 Henry VIII. the earlier statute is repeated, but made harsher with new clauses. For a second arrest for vagabondage, the whipping is to be repeated and half the ear sliced off. But at the third relapse, the offender is to be executed as a hardened criminal and an enemy of the common good.

<!-- ch28-p004 -->
**Edward VI.:** A statute of the first year of his reign, 1547, orders that if anyone refuses to work, he shall be condemned as a slave to the person who has denounced him as an idler. The master shall feed his slave on bread and water, weak broth, and whatever scraps of meat he thinks fit. He has the right to force him to do any work, however disgusting, with whip and chains. If the slave is absent for two weeks, he is condemned to slavery for life and is to be branded on the forehead or back with the letter S. If he runs away three times, he is to be executed as a felon. The master can sell him, leave him in his will, or hire him out as a slave, just like any other personal chattel (movable property) or cattle. If the slaves attempt anything against the masters, they too are to be executed. Justices of the peace, when informed, are to hunt the rascals down. If it turns out that a vagabond has been idling about for three days, he is to be taken to his birthplace, branded on the breast with a red-hot iron with the letter V, and set to work, in chains, in the streets or at some other labor. If the vagabond gives a false birthplace, he is then to become the slave for life of that place, of its inhabitants, or of its corporation, and to be branded with an S. Everyone has the right to take away the children of the vagabonds and keep them as apprentices, the young men until their 24th year, the girls until their 20th. If they run away, they are to become the slaves of their masters up to that age, and the masters can put them in irons, whip them, etc., if they like. Every master may put an iron ring round the neck, arms, or legs of his slave, so as to know him more easily and be more certain of him.[^1] The last part of this statute provides that certain poor people may be employed by a place or by persons who are willing to give them food and drink and to find them work. This kind of parish slave was kept up in England until far into the 19th century under the name of “roundsmen.”

[^1]: The author of the “Essay on Trade, etc.,” 1770, says: “In the reign of Edward VI. indeed the English seem to have set, in good earnest, about encouraging manufactures and employing the poor. This we learn from a remarkable statute which runs thus: ‘That all vagrants shall be branded, &c.’” Cited above, p. 5.

<!-- ch28-p005 -->
**Elizabeth, 1572:** Unlicensed beggars over 14 years of age are to be severely flogged and branded on the left ear, unless someone will take them into service for two years. If they repeat the offense and are over 18, they are to be executed, unless someone will take them into service for two years. But for the third offense they are to be executed without mercy as felons. Similar statutes: 18 Elizabeth, c. 13, and another of 1597.[^2]

[^2]: Thomas More says in his “Utopia”: “Therfore that on [one] covetous and unsatiable cormaraunte [cormorant] and very plage of his native contrey maye compasse aboute and inclose many thousand akers of grounde together within one pale [fence] or hedge, the husbandman [farmer] be thrust owte of their owne, or els either by coneyne [collusion] and fraude, or by violent oppression they be put besydes it [put out of it], or by wrongs and iniuries thei be so weried that they be compelled to sell all: by one meanes, therfore, or by other, either by hooke or crooke they muste needes departe awaye, poore, selye [helpless], wretched soules, men, women, husbands, wiues, fatherlesse children, widowes, wofull mothers with their yonge babes, and their whole household smal in substance, and muche in numbre, as husbandrye requireth many handes. Awaye thei trudge, I say, owte of their knowen accustomed houses, fyndynge no place to reste in. All their housholde stuffe, which is very little woorthe, thoughe it might well abide the sale: yet beeynge sodainely thruste owte, they be constrayned to sell it for a thing of nought. And when they haue wandered abrode tyll that be spent, what cant they then els doe but steale, and then iustly pardy [truly] be hanged, or els go about beggyng. And yet then also they be caste in prison as vagaboundes, because they go aboute and worke not: whom no man wyl set a worke though thei neuer so willyngly profre themselues therto.” Of these poor fugitives, who, as Thomas More says, were forced to steal, “7,200 great and petty thieves were put to death,” in the reign of Henry VIII. (Holinshed, “Description of England,” Vol. 1, p. 186.) In Elizabeth's time, “rogues were trussed up [hanged] apace, and that there was not one year commonly wherein three or four hundred were not devoured and eaten up by the gallowes.” (Strype’s “Annals of the Reformation and Establishment of Religion and other Various Occurrences in the Church of England during Queen Elizabeth’s Happy Reign.” Second ed., 1725, Vol. 2.) According to this same Strype, in Somersetshire, in one year, 40 persons were executed, 35 robbers burnt in the hand, 37 whipped, and 183 discharged as “incorrigible vagabonds.” Nevertheless, he is of the opinion that this large number of prisoners does not include even a fifth of the actual criminals, thanks to the negligence of the justices and the foolish compassion of the people; and the other counties of England were no better off in this respect than Somersetshire, while some were even worse.

<!-- ch28-p006 -->
**James 1:** Anyone wandering about and begging is declared a rogue and a vagabond. Justices of the peace in petty sessions are authorized to have them publicly whipped, and to imprison them for 6 months for the first offense and for 2 years for the second. While in prison they are to be whipped as much and as often as the justices of the peace think fit... Incorrigible and dangerous rogues are to be branded with an R on the left shoulder and set to hard labor, and if they are caught begging again, they are to be executed without mercy. These statutes stayed legally binding until the beginning of the 18th century, and were only repealed by 12 Anne, c. 23.

<!-- ch28-p007 -->
There were similar laws in France, where by the middle of the 17th century a kingdom of vagabonds (truands) had been set up in Paris. Even at the beginning of Louis XVI.’s reign (Ordinance of July 13th, 1777), every man in good health from 16 to 60 years of age is to be sent to the galleys if he is without means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) and does not practice a trade. Of the same nature are the statute of Charles V. for the Netherlands (October, 1537), the first edict of the States and Towns of Holland (March 10, 1614), the “Plakaat” [proclamation] of the United Provinces (June 26, 1649), etc.

<!-- ch28-p008 -->
Thus the agricultural people were first forcibly expropriated from the soil, driven from their homes, and turned into vagabonds. Then they were whipped, branded, and tortured, by laws grotesquely terrible, into the discipline necessary for the wage system.

<!-- ch28-p009 -->
It is not enough that the conditions of labor are concentrated in a mass, in the shape of capital, at one pole of society, while at the other pole are grouped masses of men who have nothing to sell but their labor-power. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) Nor is it enough that they are compelled to sell it voluntarily. As capitalist production advances, it develops a working class which, by education, tradition, and habit, looks on the conditions of that mode of production as self-evident laws of Nature. Once the organization of the capitalist process of production is fully developed, it breaks down all resistance. The constant generation of a relative surplus population keeps the law of supply and demand of labor, and therefore keeps wages, in a rut that suits the wants of capital. (The relative surplus population is the workers made “surplus” to capital's needs for the time being, who serve as a reserve of available labor and press down on the employed. Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) The dull compulsion of economic relations completes the subjection of the worker to the capitalist. Direct force, outside economic conditions, is of course still used, but only in exceptional cases. In the ordinary run of things, the worker can be left to the “natural laws of production,” that is, to his dependence on capital, a dependence that springs from the conditions of production themselves and is guaranteed by them forever. It is otherwise during the historical birth of capitalist production. The bourgeoisie (the capitalist class), at its rise, wants and uses the power of the state to “regulate” wages, that is, to force them within the limits suitable for making surplus-value, to lengthen the working day, and to keep the worker himself in the normal degree of dependence. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor.) This is an essential element of the so-called primitive accumulation: the historical process that separated the producers from their means of production, above all by driving peasants off the land, and so created the conditions for capitalism.

<!-- ch28-p010 -->
The class of wage workers arose in the latter half of the 14th century. Then, and in the following century, it formed only a very small part of the population, and its position was well protected by the independent peasant proprietors in the country and by the guild organization in the town. In country and town, master and workers stood close together socially. The subordination of labor to capital was only formal, that is, the mode of production itself had as yet no specifically capitalist character. Variable capital greatly outweighed constant capital. (Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power; it reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies. Constant capital is the part spent on means of production; its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) Therefore the demand for wage labor grew rapidly with every accumulation of capital (turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows), while the supply of wage labor followed only slowly. A large part of the national product, which was later changed into a fund for capitalist accumulation, still went at that time into the worker's consumption fund.

<!-- ch28-p011 -->
Legislation on wage labor (aimed from the first at the exploitation of the worker and, as it went on, always equally hostile to him)[^3] was started in England by the Statute of Labourers of Edward III., 1349. The ordinance of 1350 in France, issued in the name of King John, corresponds to it. English and French legislation run parallel and are identical in content. Insofar as the labor statutes aim at forcibly lengthening the working day, I do not return to them, since this point was dealt with earlier (Chap. X., Section 5).

[^3]: “Whenever the legislature attempts to regulate the differences between masters and their workmen, its counsellors are always the masters,” says A. Smith. “L’esprit des lois, c’est la propriété” [The spirit of the laws is property], says Linguet.

<!-- ch28-p012 -->
The Statute of Labourers was passed at the urgent request of the House of Commons. A Tory says naively:

<!-- ch28-p013 -->
> “Formerly the poor demanded such *high* wages as to threaten industry and wealth. Next, their wages are so *low* as to threaten industry and wealth equally and perhaps more, but in another way.”[^4] A fixed scale of wages was laid down by law for town and country, for piece-work and day-work. The agricultural workers were to hire themselves out by the year, the town workers “in open market.” It was forbidden, under pain of imprisonment, to pay higher wages than those fixed by the statute, but taking higher wages was punished more severely than giving them. [So also in Sections 18 and 19 of the Statute of Apprentices of Elizabeth, ten days' imprisonment is decreed for the one who pays the higher wages, but twenty-one days for the one who receives them.] A statute of 1360 increased the penalties and authorized the employers to extort labor at the legal rate of wages by corporal punishment. All combinations, contracts, oaths, etc., by which masons and carpenters bound themselves to one another were declared null and void. Coalition of the workers is treated as a heinous crime from the 14th century to 1825, the year the laws against Trades’ Unions were repealed. The spirit of the Statute of Labourers of 1349 and of its offshoots shows clearly in the fact that the State does indeed dictate a maximum of wages, but on no account a minimum.

[^4]: “Sophisms of Free Trade.” By a Barrister. London, 1850, p. 206. He adds maliciously: “We were ready enough to interfere for the employer, can nothing now be done for the employed?”

In the 16th century, as we know, the condition of the workers had become much worse. The money wage rose, but not in proportion to the depreciation of money and the corresponding rise in the prices of commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use). So wages in reality fell. Nevertheless, the laws for keeping them down remained in force, together with the ear-clipping and branding of those “whom no one was willing to take into service.” The Statute of Apprentices, 5 Elizabeth, c. 3, empowered the justices of the peace to fix certain wages and to adjust them according to the time of year and the price of commodities. James I. extended these regulations of labor to weavers, spinners, and every possible category of worker as well.[^5] George II. extended the laws against coalitions of workers to the branches of manufacture. In the manufacturing period proper, the capitalist mode of production had become strong enough to make legal regulation of wages as impracticable as it was unnecessary. But the ruling classes did not want to be without the weapons of the old arsenal in case of need. Still, 8 George II. forbade journeymen tailors in and around London a day's wage higher than 2s. 7½d., except in cases of general mourning. Still, 13 George III., c. 68, handed the regulation of the wages of silk-weavers to the justices of the peace. Still, in 1706, it took two judgments of the higher courts to decide whether the orders of justices of the peace on wages also held good for non-agricultural workers. Still, in 1799, an act of Parliament ordered that the wages of the Scottish miners should continue to be regulated by a statute of Elizabeth and two Scottish acts of 1661 and 1671. How completely circumstances had changed in the meantime is proved by an event never heard of before in the English Lower House. In that place, where for more than 400 years laws had been made for the maximum beyond which wages absolutely must not rise, Whitbread in 1796 proposed a legal minimum wage for agricultural workers. Pitt opposed this, but confessed that the “condition of the poor was cruel.” Finally, in 1813, the laws for the regulation of wages were repealed. They were an absurd anomaly, since the capitalist regulated his factory by his own private legislation, and could use the poor-rates to make up the agricultural worker's wage to the indispensable minimum. The provisions of the labor statutes on contracts between employer and worker, on giving notice, and the like, allow only a civil action against an employer who breaks a contract, but on the contrary permit a criminal action against a worker who breaks one; and these provisions are still in full force at this hour (1873). The barbarous laws against Trades’ Unions fell in 1825 before the threatening attitude of the proletariat. Despite this, they fell only in part. Certain beautiful fragments of the old statute vanished only in 1859. Finally, the act of Parliament of June 29, 1871, made a pretense of removing the last traces of this class of legislation by giving Trades’ Unions legal recognition. But an act of Parliament of the same date (an act to amend the criminal law relating to violence, threats, and molestation) in fact re-established the former state of things in a new shape. By this Parliamentary sleight of hand, the means that the workers could use in a strike or lock-out were taken out of the laws common to all citizens and placed under exceptional penal legislation, whose interpretation fell to the employers themselves in their capacity as justices of the peace. Two years earlier, the same House of Commons and the same Mr. Gladstone, in the well-known straightforward fashion, had brought in a bill for the abolition of all exceptional penal legislation against the working class. But it was never allowed to go beyond the second reading, and the matter was dragged out in this way until at last the “great Liberal party,” by an alliance with the Tories, found the courage to turn against the very proletariat that had carried it into power. Not content with this treachery, the “great Liberal party” allowed the English judges, always obliging in the service of the ruling classes, to dig up again the earlier laws against “conspiracy” and to apply them to coalitions of workers. We see that the English Parliament gave up the laws against Strikes and Trades’ Unions only against its will and under the pressure of the masses, after it had itself, for 500 years, held, with shameless egoism, the position of a permanent Trades’ Union of the capitalists against the workers.

In Germany, especially after the Thirty Years' War, statutes for keeping down wages were general. “The want [lack] of servants and labourers was very troublesome to the landed proprietors in the depopulated districts. All villagers were forbidden to let rooms to single men and women; all the latter were to be reported to the authorities and cast into prison if they were unwilling to become servants, even if they were employed at any other work, such as sowing seeds for the peasants at a daily wage, or even buying and selling corn. (Imperial privileges and sanctions for Silesia, I., 25.) For a whole century in the decrees of the small German potentates a bitter cry goes up again and again about the wicked and impertinent rabble that will not reconcile itself to its hard lot, will not be content with the legal wage; the individual landed proprietors are forbidden to pay more than the State had fixed by a tariff. And yet the conditions of service were at times better after war than 100 years later; the farm servants of Silesia had, in 1652, meat twice a week, whilst even in our century, districts are known where they have it only three times a year. Further, wages after the war were higher than in the following century.” (G. Freytag.)

During the very first storms of the revolution, the French bourgeoisie dared to take away from the workers the right of association that they had only just won. By a decree of June 14, 1791, they declared every coalition of the workers to be “an attempt against liberty and the declaration of the rights of man,” punishable by a fine of 500 livres, together with loss of the rights of an active citizen for one year.[^6] By means of State compulsion, this law confined the struggle between capital and labor within limits comfortable for capital, and it has outlived revolutions and changes of dynasty. Even the Reign of Terror left it untouched. It was struck out of the Penal Code only quite recently. Nothing is more characteristic than the pretext for this bourgeois *coup d’état.* “Granting,” says Chapelier, the reporter of the Select Committee on this law, “that wages ought to be a little higher than they are, ... that they ought to be high enough for him that receives them, to be free from that state of absolute dependence due to the want [lack] of the necessaries of life, and which is almost that of slavery,” yet, Chapelier goes on, the workers must not be allowed to come to any understanding about their own interests, nor to act in common and so lessen their “absolute dependence, which is almost that of slavery;” because, if you please, in doing this they injure “the freedom of their cidevant [former] masters, the present entrepreneurs,” and because a coalition against the despotism of the one-time masters of the corporations (the old trade guilds) is – guess what! – a restoration of the corporations abolished by the French constitution.[^7]
