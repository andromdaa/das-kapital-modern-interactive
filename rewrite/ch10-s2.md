### Section 2: The Greed for Surplus-Labor. Manufacturer and Boyard

<!-- ch10-p014 -->
Capital did not invent surplus labor. Wherever one part of society holds a monopoly of the means of production, the worker, free or not free, must add extra working time to the working time needed to maintain himself. He must do so in order to produce the means of subsistence for the owners of the means of production[^7], whether this owner is the Athenian χαλος γαχαθος [well-to-do man], the Etruscan theocrat, the *civis Romanus* [Roman citizen], the Norman baron, the American slave-owner, the Wallachian Boyard, the modern landlord, or the capitalist.[^8] It is clear, however, that in any given economic formation of society where the use-value of the product predominates, and not its exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others), surplus labor will be limited by a given set of wants, which may be larger or smaller. Here no boundless thirst for surplus labor arises from the nature of production itself. So in antiquity over-work becomes horrible only where the aim is to obtain exchange-value in its specific, independent money form (the form of value in which one commodity, gold, has become the universal equivalent): in the production of gold and silver. Here, compulsory working to death is the recognized form of over-work. Just read Diodorus Siculus.[^9] Still, these are exceptions in antiquity. But as soon as peoples whose production still moves within the lower forms of slave labor, corvée labor (forced unpaid labor that peasants had to perform for a lord), etc., are drawn into the whirlpool of an international market dominated by the capitalist mode of production, and the sale of their products for export becomes their main interest, the civilized horrors of over-work are grafted onto the barbaric horrors of slavery, serfdom, etc. So the labor of Black people in the Southern States of the American Union kept something of a patriarchal character as long as production was aimed chiefly at immediate local consumption. But to the extent that the export of cotton became of vital interest to these states, the over-working of the Black worker, and sometimes the using up of his life in 7 years of labor, became a factor in a calculated and calculating system. It was no longer a question of getting a certain quantity of useful products out of him. It was now a question of the production of surplus labor itself. The same was true of the corvée, for example in the Danubian Principalities (now Roumania).

<!-- ch10-p015 -->
Comparing the greed for surplus labor in the Danubian Principalities with the same greed in English factories is of special interest, because in the corvée, surplus labor has an independent form that you can see and touch.

<!-- ch10-p016 -->
Suppose the working day consists of 6 hours of necessary labor and 6 hours of surplus labor. Then the free worker gives the capitalist 6 x 6, or 36, hours of surplus labor every week. It is the same as if he worked 3 days a week for himself and 3 days a week free of charge for the capitalist. But this does not show on the surface. Surplus labor and necessary labor flow into each other. So I can express the same relation by saying, for example, that the worker works 30 seconds of every minute for himself and 30 for the capitalist, etc. It is different with the corvée. The necessary labor that the Wallachian peasant does to maintain himself is clearly marked off from his surplus labor for the Boyard. He does the one on his own field, the other on the lord's estate. So the two parts of the labor-time exist independently, side by side. In the corvée, surplus labor is precisely marked off from necessary labor. This, however, makes no difference to the quantitative relation of surplus labor to necessary labor. Three days of surplus labor a week remain three days that bring the worker himself no equivalent, whether they are called corvée or wage labor. But in the capitalist, the greed for surplus labor shows itself in the striving for an unlimited extension of the working day; in the Boyard, more simply, in a direct hunt for days of corvée.[^10]

[^10]: What follows refers to the situation in the Rumanian provinces before the changes made since the Crimean war.

<!-- ch10-p017 -->
In the Danubian Principalities the corvée was mixed up with rents in kind and other trappings of bondage, but it was the most important tribute paid to the ruling class. Where this was so, the corvée rarely arose from serfdom; on the contrary, serfdom much more often arose from the corvée.[^11] This is what happened in the Roumanian provinces. Their original mode of production was based on common ownership of the soil, but not in the Slavonic or Indian form. The members of the community farmed part of the land separately, as freehold. Another part, the *ager publicus* [public land], they farmed in common. The products of this common labor served partly as a reserve fund against bad harvests and other accidents, and partly as a public store to cover the costs of war, religion, and other common expenses. In the course of time, military and church dignitaries seized, along with the common land, the labor spent on it. The labor of the free peasants on their common land was turned into corvée for the thieves of the common land. This corvée soon developed into a relation of servitude that existed in fact, though not in law, until Russia, the liberator of the world, made it legal under the pretense of abolishing serfdom. The code of the corvée that the Russian General Kisseleff proclaimed in 1831 was, of course, dictated by the Boyards themselves. So with one blow Russia conquered both the magnates of the Danubian provinces and the applause of liberal cretins all over Europe.

[^11]: This also holds for Germany, and especially for Prussia east of the Elbe. In the 15th century the German peasant was nearly everywhere a man who, while subject to certain rents paid in produce and labor, was otherwise at least practically free. The German colonists in Brandenburg, Pomerania, Silesia, and Eastern Prussia were even legally recognized as free men. The victory of the nobility in the peasants' war put an end to that. Not only were the conquered South German peasants enslaved again. From the middle of the 16th century the peasants of Eastern Prussia, Brandenburg, Pomerania, and Silesia, and soon after them the free peasants of Schleswig-Holstein, were reduced to the condition of serfs. (Maurer, Fronhöfe iv. vol., — Meitzen, “Der Boden des preussischen Staats” — Hanssen, “Leibeigenschaft in Schleswig-Holstein.” — *F. E.)*

<!-- ch10-p018 -->
This code of the corvée is called the “Règlement organique” [Organic Regulation]. According to it, every Wallachian peasant owes the so-called landlord, besides a mass of detailed payments in kind: *(1)*, 12 days of general labor; *(2)*, one day of field labor; *(3)*, one day of carrying wood. In all, 14 days a year. But with deep insight into political economy (the science of economics as it was then called), the working day is not taken in its ordinary sense. It is taken as the working day needed to produce an average daily product, and that average daily product is fixed so craftily that no Cyclops could finish it in 24 hours. In dry words, the Réglement itself declares, with true Russian irony, that by 12 working days one must understand the product of 36 days of manual labor; by 1 day of field labor, 3 days; and by 1 day of wood carrying, likewise three times as much. In all, 42 corvée days. To this had to be added the so-called jobagie, service owed to the lord on extraordinary occasions. Every village has to supply a fixed contingent to the jobagie each year, in proportion to the size of its population. This extra corvée is estimated at 14 days for each Wallachian peasant. So the prescribed corvée comes to 56 working days a year. But because of the harsh climate, the agricultural year in Wallachia has only 210 days. Of these, 40 for Sundays and holidays and 30 on average for bad weather, 70 days together, do not count. 140 working days remain. The ratio of the corvée to the necessary labor, 56/84 or 66 2/3 %, gives a much lower rate of surplus-value than the one that governs the labor of the English agricultural or factory worker. This, however, is only the corvée prescribed by law. And in a spirit even more “liberal” than the English Factory Acts, the “Règlement organique” has known how to make its own evasion easy. After turning 12 days into 56, it arranges the nominal day's work of each of the 56 corvée days so that part of it must spill over onto the next day. In one day, for example, an area of land must be weeded that needs twice as much time for this work, especially on maize plantations. For some kinds of agricultural labor, the legal day's work can be interpreted so that the day begins in May and ends in October. In Moldavia conditions are even harsher.

If the Règlement organique of the Danubian provinces was a positive expression of the greed for surplus labor which every one of its paragraphs made legal, the English Factory Acts are the negative expression of the same greed. These Acts curb capital's passion for draining labor-power without limit by forcibly limiting the working day through state regulations, made by a state ruled by capitalist and landlord. Apart from the working-class movement, which grew more threatening every day, the limiting of factory labor was dictated by the same necessity that spread guano (a fertilizer made of bird droppings) over the English fields. The same blind eagerness for plunder that in the one case exhausted the soil had, in the other, torn up the living force of the nation by the roots. Periodic epidemics speak as clearly on this point as the falling military standard in Germany and France.[^13]

The Factory Act of 1850, now in force (1867), allows an average working day of 10 hours. That is, for the first 5 days of the week, 12 hours from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., including ½ an hour for breakfast and an hour for dinner, which leaves 10½ working hours; and for Saturday 8 hours, from 6 a.m. to 2 p.m., of which ½ an hour is taken off for breakfast. That leaves 60 working hours: 10½ for each of the first 5 days, 7½ for the last.[^14]

[^14]: The history of the Factory Act of 1850 will be given in the course of this chapter.

<!-- ch10-p022 -->
Certain guardians of these laws are appointed: the Factory Inspectors, who report directly to the Home Secretary. Their reports are published every half-year, by order of Parliament. They provide regular, official statistics of the capitalist greed for surplus labor.

Crises, during which production is interrupted and the factories work “short time,” that is, only part of the week, naturally do not affect the tendency to extend the working day. The less business there is, the more profit has to be made on the business that is done. The less time is spent working, the more of that time has to be turned into surplus labor-time.

<!-- ch10-p030 -->

The following curious incident shows with what fanaticism, according to the manufacturers' evidence in courts of law, their hands set themselves against every interruption of factory labor. At the beginning of June, 1836, the magistrates of Dewsbury (Yorkshire) received information that the owners of 8 large mills near Batley had violated the Factory Acts. Some of these gentlemen were accused of having kept 5 boys between 12 and 15 years of age at work from 6 a.m. on Friday to 4 p.m. on the following Saturday, without allowing them any rest except for meals and one hour of sleep at midnight. And these children had to do this ceaseless labor of 30 hours in the “shoddyhole,” as the hole is called in which the woolen rags are pulled to pieces. There a dense atmosphere of dust, shreds, etc., forces even the adult worker to keep his mouth covered with handkerchiefs to protect his lungs! Instead of taking an oath — as quakers they were too scrupulously religious to take an oath — the accused gentlemen affirm that, in their great compassion for the unhappy children, they had allowed them four hours for sleep, but the obstinate children absolutely would not go to bed. The quaker gentlemen were fined £20. Dryden foresaw these gentry:

    Fox full fraught in seeming sanctity, That feared an oath, but like the devil would lie, That look’d like Lent, and had the holy leer, And durst not sin! before he said his prayer!”
