<!-- ch01-p067 -->
So real changes in the magnitude of value are reflected in their relative expression, that is, in the equation that expresses the magnitude of relative value, neither unambiguously nor completely. The relative value of a commodity may vary, although its value stays constant. Its relative value may stay constant, although its value varies. And finally, changes that happen at the same time in the magnitude of value and in its relative expression by no means necessarily match in amount.[^21]

[^21]: Vulgar economists (economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations) have exploited this mismatch between the magnitude of value and its relative expression, with their customary ingenuity. For example – “Once admit that A falls, because B, with which it is exchanged, rises, while no less labour is bestowed in the meantime on A, and your general principle of value falls to the ground... If he [Ricardo] allowed that when A rises in value relatively to B, B falls in value relatively to A, he cut away the ground on which he rested his grand proposition, that the value of a commodity is ever determined by the labour embodied in it, for if a change in the cost of A alters not only its own value in relation to B, for which it is exchanged, but also the value of B relatively to that of A, though no change has taken place in the quantity of labour to produce B, then not only the doctrine falls to the ground which asserts that the quantity of labour bestowed on an article regulates its value, but also that which affirms the cost of an article to regulate its value’ (J. Broadhurst: “Political Economy,” London, 1842, pp. 11 and 14.) Mr. Broadhurst might just as well say: consider the fractions 10/20, 10/50, 10/100, etc.; the number 10 stays unchanged, and yet its proportional magnitude, its size relative to the numbers 20, 50, 100 etc., keeps getting smaller. Therefore the great principle that the magnitude of a whole number, such as 10, is “regulated” by the number of times unity (the number one) is contained in it, falls to the ground. [The author explains in section 4 of this chapter, pp. 80-81, note 2 (note 33 of this document), what he means by “Vulgar Economy.” – Engels]

##### 3. The Equivalent form of value

<!-- ch01-p068 -->
We have seen that commodity A (the linen) expresses its value in the use-value of a commodity of a different kind (the coat), and by doing so it at the same time stamps the coat with a specific form of value, namely the form of the equivalent. The commodity linen shows that it has value through the fact that the coat is equated to it, without the coat having taken on a form of value different from its bodily form. The fact that the linen therefore has value is expressed by saying that the coat is directly exchangeable with it. Therefore, when we say that a commodity is in the equivalent form, we are expressing the fact that it is directly exchangeable with other commodities.

<!-- ch01-p069 -->
When one commodity, such as a coat, serves as the equivalent of another, such as linen, and coats as a result acquire the characteristic property of being directly exchangeable with linen, we are far from knowing in what proportion the two can be exchanged. Since the magnitude of the linen's value is given, that proportion depends on the value of the coat. The coat may serve as the equivalent and the linen as relative value, or the linen may serve as the equivalent and the coat as relative value. Either way, the magnitude of the coat's value is fixed by the labor-time necessary to produce it, independently of its form of value. But whenever the coat takes the position of equivalent in the equation of value, its value gets no quantitative expression. On the contrary, the commodity coat now figures only as a definite quantity of some article.

<!-- ch01-p070 -->
For instance, 40 yards of linen are worth – what? 2 coats. The commodity coat here plays the part of equivalent, and the use-value coat, as opposed to the linen, figures as an embodiment of value. Because of this, a definite number of coats is enough to express the definite quantity of value in the linen. Two coats may therefore express the quantity of value of 40 yards of linen, but they can never express the quantity of their own value. The fact is that in the equation of value, the equivalent figures only as a simple quantity of some article, of some use-value. A superficial look at this fact has misled Bailey, and many others too, both before and after him, into seeing nothing but a quantitative relation in the expression of value. The truth is that when a commodity acts as equivalent, no fixing of the amount of its value is expressed.

<!-- ch01-p071 -->
The first peculiarity that strikes us when we consider the form of the equivalent is this: use-value becomes the form in which its opposite, value, shows itself, the form of appearance of value.

<!-- ch01-p072 -->
The bodily form of the commodity becomes its form of value. But note well that this taking of one thing for another exists for any commodity B only when some other commodity A enters into a value relation with it, and then only within the limits of this relation. No commodity can stand in the relation of equivalent to itself, and so turn its own bodily shape into the expression of its own value. So every commodity is compelled to choose some other commodity as its equivalent, and to accept the use-value, that is, the bodily shape, of that other commodity as the form of its own value.

<!-- ch01-p073 -->
One of the measures that we apply to commodities as material things, as use-values, will illustrate this point. A sugar-loaf is a body, so it is heavy and has weight; but we can neither see nor touch this weight. We then take various pieces of iron whose weight has been determined beforehand. The iron, as iron, is no more the form of appearance of weight than the sugar-loaf is. Nevertheless, in order to express the sugar-loaf as so much weight, we put it into a weight-relation with the iron. In this relation, the iron acts as a body that represents nothing but weight. So a certain quantity of iron serves as the measure of the sugar's weight, and, in relation to the sugar-loaf, it represents weight embodied, the form of appearance of weight. The iron plays this part only within this relation, which the sugar, or any other body whose weight has to be determined, enters into with the iron. If they were not both heavy, they could not enter into this relation, and so the one could not serve as the expression of the other's weight. When we throw both into the scales, we actually see that as weight they are both the same, and that therefore, taken in the right proportions, they have the same weight. Just as the substance iron, as a measure of weight, represents weight alone in relation to the sugar-loaf, so, in our expression of value, the material object coat represents value alone in relation to the linen.

<!-- ch01-p074 -->
Here, however, the analogy ends. In expressing the weight of the sugar-loaf, the iron represents a natural property common to both bodies, namely their weight. But in expressing the value of the linen, the coat represents a non-natural property of both, something purely social, namely their value.

<!-- ch01-p075 -->
The relative form of value of a commodity – the linen, for example – expresses the value of that commodity as something wholly different from its substance and properties, as something coat-like, for instance. So we see that this expression itself points to some social relation lying at the bottom of it. With the equivalent form it is just the opposite. The very essence of this form is that the material commodity itself – the coat – just as it is, expresses value, and is given the form of value by Nature itself. Of course this holds only so long as the value relation exists in which the coat stands in the position of equivalent to the linen.[^22] However, the properties of a thing do not result from its relations to other things; they only show themselves in such relations. So the coat seems to be endowed by Nature with its equivalent form, its property of being directly exchangeable, just as much as with the property of being heavy, or the capacity to keep us warm. Hence the puzzling character of the equivalent form. It escapes the notice of the bourgeois political economist until this form, fully developed, confronts him in the shape of money. He then tries to explain away the mystical character of gold and silver by putting less dazzling commodities in their place, and by reciting, with ever renewed satisfaction, the catalogue of all possible commodities that at one time or another have played the part of equivalent. He has not the least suspicion that the simplest expression of value, such as 20 yds of linen = 1 coat, already sets us the riddle of the equivalent form to solve.

[^22]: Such expressions of relations in general, which Hegel calls reflex categories, form a very curious class. For instance, one man is king only because other men stand in the relation of subjects to him. They, on the contrary, imagine that they are subjects because he is king.

<!-- ch01-p076 -->
The body of the commodity that serves as the equivalent figures as the materialization of abstract human labor, and at the same time it is the product of some specific useful, concrete labor. So this concrete labor becomes the medium for expressing abstract human labor. If, on the one hand, the coat counts as nothing but the embodiment of abstract human labor, so, on the other hand, the tailoring actually embodied in it counts as nothing but the form in which that abstract human labor is realized. In the expression of the linen's value, the usefulness of the tailoring lies not in making clothes, but in making an object that we at once recognize to be Value, and therefore to be a congealed mass of labor. But it is labor that cannot be told apart from the labor realized in the value of the linen. To act as such a mirror of value, the labor of tailoring must reflect nothing except its own abstract quality of being human labor in general.

<!-- ch01-p077 -->
In tailoring, as well as in weaving, human labor-power is spent. Both, therefore, have the general property of being human labor, and so, in certain cases, such as the production of value, they may have to be considered under this aspect alone. There is nothing mysterious in this. But in the expression of value the tables are completely turned. For instance, how is the fact to be expressed that weaving creates the value of the linen, not by virtue of being weaving as such, but because of its general property of being human labor? Simply by setting against weaving that other particular form of concrete labor (in this instance tailoring) which produces the equivalent of the product of weaving. Just as the coat in its bodily form became a direct expression of value, so now tailoring, a concrete form of labor, appears as the direct and tangible embodiment of human labor in general.

<!-- ch01-p078 -->
So the second peculiarity of the equivalent form is that concrete labor becomes the form in which its opposite, abstract human labor, shows itself.

<!-- ch01-p079 -->
But this concrete labor, tailoring in our case, counts as undifferentiated human labor and is directly identified with it. Because of this, it also counts as identical with any other sort of labor, and therefore with the labor embodied in the linen. So, although like all other commodity-producing labor it is the labor of private individuals, it nevertheless counts at the same time as labor directly social in its character. This is the reason why it results in a product directly exchangeable with other commodities. We have, then, a third peculiarity of the equivalent form, namely that the labor of private individuals takes the form of its opposite, labor directly social in its form.

<!-- ch01-p080 -->
The two last peculiarities of the equivalent form will become easier to understand if we go back to the great thinker who was the first to analyze so many forms, whether of thought, of society, or of Nature, and among them the form of value too. I mean Aristotle.

<!-- ch01-p081 -->
In the first place, he states clearly that the money form of commodities is only the further development of the simple form of value – that is, of the expression of the value of one commodity in some other commodity taken at random. For he says:

<!-- ch01-p082 -->
> 5 beds = 1 house (κλῖναι πέντε ἀντὶ οἰκίας) [five beds for a house]

<!-- ch01-p083 -->
cannot be distinguished from

<!-- ch01-p084 -->
> 5 beds = so much money. (κλῖναι πέντε ἀντὶ ... ὅσου αἱ πέντε κλῖναι) [five beds for ... as much as the five beds]

<!-- ch01-p085 -->
He further sees that the value relation that gives rise to this expression makes it necessary for the house to be made qualitatively equal to the bed. He sees that without such an equalization, these two clearly different things could not be compared with each other as commensurable quantities, that is, as quantities measurable by a common standard. “Exchange,” he says, “cannot take place without equality, and equality not without commensurability". (οὔτ᾿ ἰσότης μὴ οὔσης συμμετρίας) [nor equality where there is no commensurability]. Here, however, he comes to a stop, and gives up analyzing the form of value any further. “It is, however, in reality, impossible (τῇ μὲν οὖν ἀληθείᾳ ἀδύνατον) [in truth, then, impossible], that such unlike things can be commensurable” – that is, qualitatively equal. Such an equalization can only be something foreign to their real nature, and so only “a makeshift for practical purposes.”

<!-- ch01-p086 -->
So Aristotle himself tells us what blocked his further analysis: it was the absence of any concept of value. What is that equal something, that common substance, which allows the value of the beds to be expressed by a house? Such a thing, in truth, cannot exist, says Aristotle. And why not? Compared with the beds, the house does represent something equal to them, insofar as it represents what is really equal in both the beds and the house. And that is – human labor.

<!-- ch01-p087 -->
There was, however, an important fact that prevented Aristotle from seeing that to attribute value to commodities is merely a way of expressing all labor as equal human labor, and therefore as labor of equal quality. Greek society was founded on slavery, and therefore had as its natural basis the inequality of human beings and of their labor-powers. The secret of the expression of value, namely that all kinds of labor are equal and equivalent because, and so far as, they are human labor in general, cannot be deciphered until the notion of human equality has already become as firmly fixed as a popular prejudice. This, however, is possible only in a society in which the great mass of the products of labor takes the form of commodities, and in which, therefore, the dominant relation between one person and another is that of owners of commodities. The brilliance of Aristotle's genius is shown by this alone: that he discovered a relation of equality in the expression of the value of commodities. Only the particular conditions of the society in which he lived prevented him from discovering what, “in truth,” lay at the bottom of this equality.

##### 4. The Elementary Form of value considered as a whole

<!-- ch01-p088 -->
The elementary form of value of a commodity is contained in the equation that expresses its value relation to another commodity of a different kind, or its exchange relation to that commodity. The value of commodity A is expressed qualitatively by the fact that commodity B is directly exchangeable with it. Its value is expressed quantitatively by the fact that a definite quantity of B is exchangeable with a definite quantity of A. In other words, the value of a commodity gets an independent and definite expression by taking the form of exchange-value. At the beginning of this chapter we said, in everyday language, that a commodity is both a use-value and an exchange-value. Strictly speaking, we were wrong. A commodity is a use-value, or useful object, and a value. It shows itself as the twofold thing it is as soon as its value takes on an independent form, namely the form of exchange-value. It never takes on this form when isolated, but only when it is placed in a value relation or exchange relation with another commodity of a different kind. Once we know this, that way of speaking does no harm; it simply serves as an abbreviation.

<!-- ch01-p089 -->
Our analysis has shown that the form or expression of the value of a commodity arises from the nature of value, and not that value and its magnitude arise from the way they are expressed as exchange-value. This, however, is the delusion both of the mercantilists and their recent revivers, Ferrier, Ganilh,[^23] and others, and of their opposites, the modern traveling salesmen of Free-trade, such as Bastiat. The mercantilists lay special stress on the qualitative side of the expression of value, and so on the equivalent form of commodities, which reaches its full perfection in money. The modern peddlers of Free-trade, on the other hand, who must get rid of their goods at any price, lay most stress on the quantitative side of the relative form of value. So for them neither value nor magnitude of value exists anywhere except in its expression through the exchange relation of commodities, that is, in the daily list of current prices. Macleod, who has taken it upon himself to dress up the confused ideas of Lombard Street (the center of London banking) in the most learned finery, is a successful cross between the superstitious mercantilists and the enlightened Free-trade traveling salesmen.

[^23]: F. L. A. Ferrier, sous-inspecteur des douanes [sub-inspector of customs], “Du gouvernement considéré dans ses rapports avec le commerce,” Paris, 1805; and Charles Ganilh, “Des Systèmes d’Economie Politique, – 2nd ed., Paris, 1821.

<!-- ch01-p090 -->
A close examination of the expression of the value of A in terms of B, contained in the equation that expresses the value relation of A to B, has shown us the following: within that relation, the bodily form of A figures only as a use-value, and the bodily form of B only as the form or aspect of value. So the opposition, or contrast, that exists inside each commodity between use-value and value is made visible on the outside when two commodities are placed in a particular relation to each other. In that relation, the commodity whose value is to be expressed figures directly as a mere use-value, while the commodity in which that value is to be expressed figures directly as mere exchange-value. Hence the elementary form of value of a commodity is the elementary form in which the contrast contained in that commodity, between use-value and value, becomes visible.

<!-- ch01-p091 -->
Every product of labor is a use-value in all states of society. But only at a definite historical epoch in a society's development does such a product become a commodity, namely at the epoch when the labor spent on producing a useful article comes to be expressed as one of the objective qualities of that article, that is, as its value. It therefore follows that the elementary form of value is also the earliest form in which a product of labor appears historically as a commodity, and that the gradual transformation of such products into commodities proceeds at the same pace as the development of the form of value.

<!-- ch01-p092 -->
At first sight we see the shortcomings of the elementary form of value: it is a mere germ, which must go through a series of metamorphoses before it can ripen into the price form.

<!-- ch01-p093 -->
The expression of the value of commodity A in terms of any other commodity B merely distinguishes the value of A from its use-value, and so places A merely in a relation of exchange with one single different commodity, B. But it is still far from expressing A's qualitative equality with all commodities, and its quantitative proportionality to them. To the elementary relative form of value of one commodity there corresponds the single equivalent form of one other commodity. Thus, in the relative expression of the linen's value, the coat takes the form of equivalent, or of being directly exchangeable, only in relation to a single commodity, the linen.

<!-- ch01-p094 -->
Nevertheless, the elementary form of value passes over easily into a more complete form. It is true that through the elementary form the value of a commodity A is expressed in terms of one, and only one, other commodity. But that one may be a commodity of any kind: coat, iron, grain, or anything else. So, depending on whether A is placed in relation with one or another of them, we get different elementary expressions of value for one and the same commodity.[^24] The number of such possible expressions is limited only by the number of different kinds of commodities distinct from it. So the isolated expression of A's value can be turned into a series, prolonged to any length, of the different elementary expressions of that value.

[^24]: In Homer, for instance, the value of an article is expressed in a series of different things. *Iliad*. VII. 472-475.
