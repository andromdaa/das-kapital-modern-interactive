##### 1. The Expanded Relative form of value

<!-- ch01-p097 -->
The value of a single commodity, the linen for example, is now expressed in terms of countless other elements of the world of commodities. Every other commodity now becomes a mirror of the linen’s value.[^25] So it is here that, for the first time, this value shows itself in its true light, as a congealed mass of undifferentiated human labor. For the labor that creates it now stands openly revealed as labor that ranks as equal with every other sort of human labor, whatever its form, whether tailoring, plowing, mining, etc., and so whether it is realized in coats, grain, iron, or gold. Through the form of its value, the linen now stands in a social relation, no longer with only one other kind of commodity, but with the whole world of commodities. As a commodity, it is a citizen of that world. At the same time, the endless series of value equations implies that, as regards the value of a commodity, it makes no difference in what particular form, or kind, of use-value it appears.

[^25]: For this reason we can speak of the coat value of the linen when its value is expressed in coats, or of its grain value when it is expressed in grain, and so on. Every such expression tells us that what appears in the use-values coat, grain, etc., is the value of the linen. “The value of any commodity denoting its relation in exchange, we may speak of it as ... corn [grain] value, cloth value, according to the commodity with which it is compared; and hence there are a thousand different kinds of value, as many kinds of value as there are commodities in existence, and all are equally real and equally nominal.” (“A Critical Dissertation on the Nature, Measures and Causes of Value: chiefly in reference to the writings of Mr. Ricardo and his followers.” By the author of “Essays on the Formation, &c., of Opinions.” London, 1825, p. 39.) S. Bailey, the author of this anonymous work, which caused a great stir in England in its day, fancied that by pointing out in this way the various relative expressions of one and the same value, he had proved that the concept of value could not be determined at all. However narrow his own views may have been, he did lay his finger on some serious defects in the Ricardian Theory. The proof is the hostility with which Ricardo’s followers attacked him. See, for example, the Westminster Review.

<!-- ch01-p098 -->
In the first form, 20 yds of linen = 1 coat, it might be pure accident, for all that appears otherwise, that these two commodities are exchangeable in definite quantities. In the second form, on the contrary, we see at once the background that determines this accidental appearance and is essentially different from it. The value of the linen stays the same in magnitude, whether it is expressed in coats, coffee, or iron, or in countless different commodities belonging to as many different owners. The accidental relation between two individual commodity-owners disappears. It becomes clear that it is not the exchange of commodities that regulates the magnitude of their value. On the contrary, it is the magnitude of their value that controls their exchange proportions.

##### 2. The particular Equivalent form

<!-- ch01-p099 -->
Each commodity, such as coat, tea, grain, iron, etc., figures in the expression of the linen’s value as an equivalent, and so as a thing that is value. The bodily form of each of these commodities now figures as a particular equivalent form, one out of many. In the same way, the many concrete, useful kinds of labor embodied in these different commodities now rank as so many different forms in which undifferentiated human labor is realized, or shows itself.

##### 3. Defects of the Total or Expanded form of value

<!-- ch01-p100 -->
First, the relative expression of value is incomplete, because the series that represents it never ends. The chain in which each equation of value is a link can at any moment be lengthened by each new kind of commodity that comes into existence and supplies the material for a fresh expression of value. Second, it is a many-colored mosaic of dissimilar and independent expressions of value. And lastly, if the relative value of each commodity in turn is expressed in this expanded form, as must be the case, we get for each of them a relative form of value that is different in every case and consists of an endless series of expressions of value. The defects of the expanded relative form of value are reflected in the corresponding equivalent form. The bodily form of each single commodity is one particular equivalent form among countless others. So, on the whole, we have nothing but fragmentary equivalent forms, each excluding the others. In the same way, the special, concrete, useful kind of labor embodied in each particular equivalent is presented only as a particular kind of labor, and so not as an exhaustive representative of human labor generally. Human labor generally does, indeed, find adequate expression in the totality of its many particular, concrete forms. But in that case its expression in an infinite series is always incomplete and lacks unity.

The expanded relative form of value, however, is nothing but the sum of the elementary relative expressions, or equations of the first kind, such as:

##### 1. The altered character of the form of value

<!-- ch01-p107 -->
All commodities now express their value (1) in an elementary form, because in a single commodity; (2) with unity, because in one and the same commodity. This form of value is elementary and the same for all, and therefore general.

<!-- ch01-p108 -->
Forms A and B were suited only to expressing the value of a commodity as something distinct from its use-value or material form.

<!-- ch01-p109 -->
The first form, A, supplies equations such as these: – 1 coat = 20 yards of linen, 10 lbs of tea = ½ a ton of iron. The value of the coat is equated to linen, the value of the tea to iron. But to be equated to linen, and again to iron, is to be as different as linen and iron are. Clearly, this form occurs in practice only at the very beginning, when the products of labor are turned into commodities by accidental and occasional exchanges.

<!-- ch01-p110 -->
The second form, B, distinguishes the value of a commodity from its use-value more adequately than the first form does. For there the value of the coat is set against the bodily form of the coat in every possible shape: it is equated to linen, to iron, to tea, in short to everything else, only not to itself, the coat. On the other hand, any general expression of value common to all is directly ruled out. For in the equation of value of each commodity, all other commodities now appear only in the form of equivalents. The expanded form of value first comes into actual existence as soon as a particular product of labor, such as cattle, is exchanged for various other commodities no longer as an exception, but habitually.

<!-- ch01-p111 -->
The third form, the last to be developed, expresses the values of the whole world of commodities in terms of a single commodity set apart for the purpose, namely the linen. So it represents their values to us by means of their equality with linen. By being equated to linen, the value of every commodity is now distinguished not only from its own use-value, but from all other use-values generally. By that very fact it is expressed as what is common to all commodities. Through this form, commodities are for the first time actually brought into relation with one another as values, or made to appear as exchange-values.

<!-- ch01-p112 -->
The two earlier forms express the value of each commodity either in terms of a single commodity of a different kind, or in a series of many such commodities. In both cases it is, so to speak, the special business of each single commodity to find an expression for its value, and it does this without the help of the others. In relation to it, these others play the passive parts of equivalents. The general form of value, C, results from the joint action of the whole world of commodities, and from that alone. A commodity can get a general expression of its value only if all other commodities, at the same time as it, express their values in the same equivalent; and every new commodity must follow suit. So it becomes clear that, since the existence of commodities as values is purely social, this social existence can be expressed only by the totality of their social relations, and so that the form of their value must be a socially recognized form.

<!-- ch01-p113 -->
Now that all commodities are equated to linen, they appear not only as qualitatively equal, as values in general, but also as values whose magnitudes can be compared. Because they express the magnitudes of their values in one and the same material, the linen, those magnitudes are also compared with each other. For instance, 10 lbs of tea = 20 yards of linen, and 40 lbs of coffee = 20 yards of linen. Therefore, 10 lbs of tea = 40 lbs of coffee. In other words, 1 lb of coffee contains only one-fourth as much substance of value – labor – as 1 lb of tea contains.

<!-- ch01-p114 -->
The general form of relative value, which takes in the whole world of commodities, turns the single commodity that is excluded from the rest and made to play the part of equivalent – here the linen – into the universal equivalent. The bodily form of the linen is now the form that the values of all commodities take in common. So it becomes directly exchangeable with every one of them. The substance linen becomes the visible incarnation, the social chrysalis state, of every kind of human labor. Weaving, which is the labor of certain private individuals producing a particular article, linen, therefore acquires a social character, the character of equality with all other kinds of labor. The countless equations that make up the general form of value equate, in turn, the labor embodied in the linen with the labor embodied in every other commodity. In this way they turn weaving into the general form in which undifferentiated human labor shows itself. In this manner the labor realized in the values of commodities is presented not only in its negative aspect, in which every concrete form and useful property of actual work is set aside; its own positive nature is also made to reveal itself expressly. The general form of value is the reduction of all kinds of actual labor to their common character of being human labor generally, of being the spending of human labor-power.

<!-- ch01-p115 -->
The general form of value, which represents all products of labor as mere congealed masses of undifferentiated human labor, shows by its very structure that it is the social summing-up of the world of commodities. So that form makes it beyond dispute that, in the world of commodities, the character that all labor has of being *human* labor is its specific social character.

##### 2. The Interdependent Development of the Relative Form of Value, and of the Equivalent Form

<!-- ch01-p116 -->
The degree of development of the relative form of value corresponds to that of the equivalent form. But we must remember that the development of the equivalent form is only the expression and result of the development of the relative form.

<!-- ch01-p117 -->
The primary or isolated relative form of value of one commodity turns some other commodity into an isolated equivalent. The expanded form of relative value, which is the expression of the value of one commodity in terms of all other commodities, gives those other commodities the character of particular equivalents of different kinds. And lastly, a particular kind of commodity acquires the character of universal equivalent, because all other commodities make it the material in which they uniformly express their value.

<!-- ch01-p118 -->
The opposition between the relative form of value and the equivalent form, the two poles of the form of value, develops along with that form itself.

<!-- ch01-p119 -->
The first form, 20 yds of linen = one coat, already contains this opposition, but does not yet fix it. Depending on whether we read this equation forwards or backwards, the linen and the coat play different parts. In one case the relative value of the linen is expressed in the coat; in the other, the relative value of the coat is expressed in the linen. So in this first form of value it is difficult to grasp the polar contrast.

<!-- ch01-p120 -->
Form B shows that only one single commodity at a time can fully expand its relative value, and that it gets this expanded form only because, and insofar as, all other commodities are equivalents with respect to it. Here we cannot reverse the equation, as we can the equation 20 yds of linen = 1 coat, without changing its general character and turning it from the expanded form of value into the general form of value.

<!-- ch01-p121 -->
Finally, form C gives the world of commodities a general social relative form of value, because, and insofar as, all commodities except one are thereby excluded from the equivalent form. So a single commodity, the linen, appears to have acquired the character of direct exchangeability with every other commodity because, and insofar as, this character is denied to every other commodity.[^26]

[^26]: It is by no means self-evident that this character of direct and universal exchangeability is, so to speak, a polar one, and as closely bound up with its opposite pole, the absence of direct exchangeability, as the positive pole of the magnet is with its negative counterpart. So one may imagine that all commodities can have this character stamped on them at the same time, just as one can imagine that all Catholics can be popes together. In the eyes of the petit bourgeois, for whom the production of commodities is the very height of human freedom and individual independence, it is of course highly desirable that the inconveniences resulting from this character of commodities, their not being directly exchangeable, should be removed. Proudhon’s socialism is a working out of this Philistine Utopia, a form of socialism which, as I have shown elsewhere, does not have even the merit of originality. Long before his time, Gray, Bray, and others attempted the task with much better success. But for all that, wisdom of this kind still flourishes in certain circles under the name of “science.” No school has ever played more tricks with the word science than the school of Proudhon, for “wo Begriffe fehlen, Da stellt zur rechten Zeit ein Wort sich ein.” [“Where concepts are lacking, a word turns up just at the right time,” Goethe’s, Faust, See Proudhon’s Philosophy of Poverty]

<!-- ch01-p122 -->
The commodity that figures as universal equivalent, on the other hand, is excluded from the relative form of value. If the linen, or any other commodity serving as universal equivalent, were at the same time to share in the relative form of value, it would have to serve as its own equivalent. We would then have 20 yds of linen = 20 yds of linen, and this tautology expresses neither value nor magnitude of value. To express the relative value of the universal equivalent, we must rather reverse form C. This equivalent has no relative form of value in common with other commodities; instead its value is expressed relatively by a never-ending series of other commodities. So the expanded form of relative value, or form B, now shows itself to be the specific form of relative value for the equivalent commodity.

##### 3. Transition from the General form of value to the Money form

<!-- ch01-p123 -->
The universal equivalent form is a form of value in general. So any commodity can take it on. On the other hand, if a commodity is found to have taken on the universal equivalent form (form C), this is only because, and insofar as, it has been excluded from all the other commodities as their equivalent, and excluded by their own act. And from the moment that this exclusion is finally restricted to one particular commodity, from that moment only, does the general form of relative value of the world of commodities gain real consistency and general social validity.

<!-- ch01-p124 -->
The particular commodity with whose bodily form the equivalent form is thus socially identified now becomes the money commodity, or serves as money. It becomes the special social function of that commodity, and so its social monopoly, to play the part of the universal equivalent within the world of commodities. Among the commodities that figure as particular equivalents of the linen in form B, and that in form C express their relative values in common in linen, one in particular has won this foremost place – namely, gold. In passing from form A to form B, and from form B to form C, the changes are fundamental. On the other hand, there is no difference between forms C and D, except that in form D gold has taken on the equivalent form in place of linen. Gold is in form D what linen was in form C – the universal equivalent. The progress consists in this alone: the character of direct and universal exchangeability – in other words, the universal equivalent form – has now, by social custom, become finally identified with the substance gold.

<!-- ch01-p127 -->
Gold is now money with reference to all other commodities only because it was previously, with reference to them, a simple commodity. Like all other commodities, it was also able to serve as an equivalent, either as a simple equivalent in isolated exchanges, or as a particular equivalent alongside others. Gradually it began to serve, within varying limits, as universal equivalent. As soon as it monopolizes this position in the expression of value for the world of commodities, it becomes the money commodity. Then, and not until then, does form D become distinct from form C, and the general form of value turn into the money form.

<!-- ch01-p128 -->
The elementary expression of the relative value of a single commodity, such as linen, in terms of the commodity, such as gold, that plays the part of money, is the price form of that commodity. So the price form of the linen is

20 yards of linen = 2 ounces of gold, or, if 2 ounces of gold when\
coined are £2, 20 yards of linen = £2.

The difficulty in forming a concept of the money form lies in clearly understanding the universal equivalent form, and, as a necessary consequence, the general form of value, form C. Form C can be derived from form B, the expanded form of value, whose essential component element, as we saw, is form A, 20 yards of linen = 1 coat or x commodity A = y commodity B. So the simple commodity form is the germ of the money form.
