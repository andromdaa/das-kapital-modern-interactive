### Section 4: Circumstances that, Independently of the Proportional Division of Surplus-Value into Capital and Revenue, Determine the Amount of Accumulation. Degree of Exploitation of Labor-Power. Productivity of Labor. Growing Difference in Amount Between Capital Employed and Capital Consumed. Magnitude of Capital Advanced

<!-- ch24-p074 -->
If the proportion in which surplus-value splits into capital and revenue is given, then the size of the capital accumulated clearly depends on the absolute size of the surplus-value. Suppose that 80 percent were capitalized and 20 percent eaten up. Then the accumulated capital will be £2,400 or £200, depending on whether the total surplus-value has amounted to £3,000 or £500. Hence all the circumstances that determine the mass of surplus-value also work to determine the size of the accumulation. We sum them up once again here, but only to the extent that they offer new points of view on accumulation.

<!-- ch24-p075 -->
It will be remembered that the rate of surplus-value depends, in the first place, on the degree of exploitation of labor-power. Political economy values this fact so highly that it sometimes treats the speeding-up of accumulation that comes from increased productivity of labor (how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time) as the same thing as its speeding-up through increased exploitation of the worker.[^35] In the chapters on the production of surplus-value we constantly assumed that wages are at least equal to the value of labor-power (the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work). In practice, however, the forcible reduction of wages below this value plays too important a part for us not to stop on it for a moment. Within certain limits, it in fact turns the worker’s necessary consumption fund into a fund for the accumulation of capital.

[^35]: “Ricardo says: ‘In different stages of society the accumulation of capital or of the means of employing’ (that is, exploiting) ‘labour is more or less rapid, and must in all cases depend on the productive powers of labour. The productive powers of labour are generally greatest where there is an abundance of fertile land.’ If, in the first sentence, the productive powers of labour mean the smallness of that aliquot part [fractional part] of any produce that goes to those whose manual labour produced it, the sentence is nearly identical, because the remaining aliquot part is the fund whence capital can, if the *owner pleases,* be accumulated. But then this does not generally happen, where there is most fertile land.” (“Observations on Certain Verbal Disputes, &c.” pp. 74, 75.)

If labour could be had without purchase, wages might be dispensed with.”[^36]

So zero cost for them is a limit in the mathematical sense: always out of reach, although we can always get closer and closer to it. The constant tendency of capital is to force the cost of labor back toward this zero. <!-- ch24-p087 -->
The so-called domestic industry has shown what part is played in our day by direct robbery from the worker’s necessary consumption fund in the formation of surplus-value, and therefore of capital’s accumulation fund. (Ch. xv., sect. 8, c.) Further facts on this subject will be given later.

<!-- ch24-p088 -->
In every branch of industry, the part of constant capital that consists of instruments of labor must be enough for a certain number of workers (a number fixed by the size of the business). Even so, it by no means always necessarily grows in the same proportion as the quantity of labor employed. Suppose that in a factory 100 workers working 8 hours a day yield 800 working hours. If the capitalist wants to raise this total by one half, he can employ 50 more workers. But then he must also advance more capital, not just for wages but for instruments of labor. He might, however, also let the 100 workers work 12 hours instead of 8, and then the instruments of labor already on hand would be enough. They would simply be used up faster. So additional labor, produced by a greater strain on labor-power, can increase the surplus-product and surplus-value (that is, the material of accumulation) without a matching increase in the constant part of capital.

<!-- ch24-p089 -->
In the extractive industries, such as mines, etc., the raw materials form no part of the capital advanced. Here the object of labor is not a product of earlier labor. Nature provides it free of charge, as with metals, minerals, coal, stone, etc. In these cases constant capital consists almost entirely of instruments of labor, which can very well absorb an increased quantity of labor (day and night shifts of workers, for example). Other things being equal, the mass and the value of the product will rise in direct proportion to the labor spent. As on the first day of production, the two original makers of products, man and Nature, now turned into the creators of the material elements of capital, still work together. Thanks to the elasticity of labor-power, the field of accumulation has widened without any previous enlargement of constant capital.

<!-- ch24-p090 -->
In agriculture, the land under cultivation cannot be increased without advancing more seed and manure. But once this advance is made, the purely mechanical working of the soil itself has a marvelous effect on the amount of the product. So a greater quantity of labor, done by the same number of workers as before, increases the fertility without requiring any new advance in instruments of labor. Once again it is the direct action of man on Nature that becomes an immediate source of greater accumulation, without any new capital stepping in.

<!-- ch24-p091 -->
Finally, in what is called manufacturing industry, every additional expenditure of labor presupposes a matching additional expenditure of raw materials, but not necessarily of instruments of labor. And since extractive industry and agriculture supply manufacturing industry with its raw materials and with those of its instruments of labor, the additional product that extractive industry and agriculture have created without an additional advance of capital also works in favor of manufacturing industry.

<!-- ch24-p092 -->
General result: by taking into itself the two primary creators of wealth, labor-power and the land, capital gains a power of expansion. This power lets it increase the elements of its accumulation beyond the limits apparently set by its own size, or by the value and the mass of the already-produced means of production in which it exists.

<!-- ch24-p093 -->
Another important factor in the accumulation of capital is the degree of productivity of social labor.

<!-- ch24-p094 -->
As the productive power of labor increases, so does the mass of products in which a certain value, and therefore a surplus-value of a given size, is embodied. If the rate of surplus-value stays the same, or even falls, as long as it falls more slowly than the productive power of labor rises, the mass of the surplus-product increases. If the division of this product into revenue and additional capital stays the same, the capitalist’s consumption may therefore increase without any decrease in the accumulation fund. The relative size of the accumulation fund may even increase at the expense of the consumption fund, while the cheapening of commodities puts at the capitalist’s disposal as many means of enjoyment as before, or even more. But, as we have seen, the growing productivity of labor goes hand in hand with the cheapening of the worker, and therefore with a higher rate of surplus-value, even when real wages are rising. Real wages never rise in proportion to the productive power of labor. So the same value in variable capital sets in motion more labor-power, and therefore more labor. The same value in constant capital is embodied in more means of production, that is, in more instruments of labor, materials of labor, and auxiliary materials. So it also supplies more elements for producing both use-value and value, and with them more absorbers of labor. Therefore, even if the value of the additional capital stays the same or even shrinks, accumulation still speeds up. Not only does the scale of reproduction grow materially, but the production of surplus-value grows faster than the value of the additional capital.

<!-- ch24-p095 -->
The development of the productive power of labor also acts back on the original capital already engaged in the process of production. Part of the functioning constant capital consists of instruments of labor, such as machinery, etc., which are not used up, and so not reproduced or replaced by new ones of the same kind, until after long periods of time. But every year part of these instruments of labor perishes or reaches the limit of its productive working life. So in that year it reaches the time for its periodic reproduction, its replacement by new ones of the same kind. If the productivity of labor has increased while these instruments of labor were being used up (and it develops continually with the unbroken advance of science and technology), more efficient and (considering their greater efficiency) cheaper machines, tools, apparatus, etc., replace the old ones. The old capital is reproduced in a more productive form, quite apart from the constant small improvements in the instruments of labor already in use. The other part of constant capital, raw material and auxiliary substances, is constantly reproduced in less than a year; those produced by agriculture, mostly once a year. So every introduction of improved methods acts almost at the same time on the new capital and on the capital already at work. Every advance in Chemistry does more than multiply the number of useful materials and the useful applications of those already known, and so widen capital’s field of investment as capital grows. At the same time it teaches how to throw the excrements of the processes of production and consumption back into the cycle of the process of reproduction. In this way, without any previous outlay of capital, it creates new material for capital. Like the increased exploitation of natural wealth by merely straining labor-power harder, science and technology give capital a power of expansion that does not depend on the given size of the capital actually functioning. At the same time they act on that part of the original capital which has reached its stage of renewal. In passing into its new shape, this part takes in, free of charge, the social progress made while its old shape was being used up. Of course, this development of productive power goes along with a partial depreciation of functioning capital. To the extent that this depreciation is sharply felt in competition, the burden falls on the worker, in whose increased exploitation the capitalist looks for compensation.

<!-- ch24-p096 -->
Labor passes on to its product the value of the means of production it uses up. On the other hand, the value and mass of the means of production set in motion by a given quantity of labor increase as the labor becomes more productive. Although the same quantity of labor always adds only the same sum of new value to its products, the old capital value that labor passes on to the products still increases as the productivity of labor grows.

<!-- ch24-p097 -->
For example, an English spinner and a Chinese spinner may work the same number of hours with the same intensity. Then in a week they will both create equal values. But in spite of this equality, there will be an immense difference between the value of the week’s product of the Englishman, who works with a mighty automatic machine, and that of the Chinese spinner, who has only a spinning-wheel. In the time the Chinese spinner spins one pound of cotton, the Englishman spins several hundreds of pounds. A sum of old values many hundred times as great swells the value of the Englishman’s product. In it those old values reappear in a new, useful form, and so can function again as capital.

<!-- ch24-p098 -->
> “In 1782,” as Frederick Engels teaches us, “all the wool crop in England of the three preceding years, lay untouched for want of labourers, and so it must have lain, if newly invented machinery had not come to its aid and spun it.”[^46]

[^46]: Friedrich Engels, “Lage der arbeitenden Klasse in England,” p. 20.

<!-- ch24-p099 -->
Labor embodied in the form of machinery did not, of course, directly call a single man into life. But it made it possible for a smaller number of workers, adding relatively less living labor, not only to consume the wool productively and put new value into it, but also to preserve its old value in the form of yarn, etc. At the same time, it caused and stimulated increased reproduction of wool. It is the natural property of living labor to pass on old value while it creates new value. Hence, as the efficiency, extent, and value of its means of production increase, and so with the accumulation that goes along with the development of its productive power, labor keeps up and perpetuates an ever-growing capital value in an ever-new form.”[^47] This natural power of labor appears as a built-in property of capital, in which labor is incorporated, just as the productive forces of social labor appear as inherent properties of capital, and as the capitalists’ constant appropriation of surplus labor appears as a constant self-expansion of capital.

[^47]: Classical economics, because of a faulty analysis of the labor process and of the process of creating value, has never properly grasped this important element of reproduction, as can be seen in Ricardo. He says, for example, that whatever the change in productive power, “a million men always produce in manufactures the same value.” This is accurate if the length and degree of intensity of their labor are given. But it does not prevent (and this Ricardo overlooks in certain conclusions he draws) a million men whose labor has different degrees of productivity from turning very different masses of means of production into products, and so preserving very different masses of value in their products. As a result, the values of the products they yield may vary considerably. Ricardo, it may be noted in passing, tried in vain to use that very example to make clear to J. B. Say the difference between use-value (which he here calls wealth or material riches) and exchange-value. Say answers: “Quant à la difficulté qu’élève Mr. Ricardo en disant que, par des procédés mieux entendus un million de personnes peuvent produire deux fois, trois fois autant de richesses, sans produire plus de valeurs, cette difficulté n’est pas une lorsque l’on considére, ainsi qu’on le doit, la production comme un échange dans lequel on donne les services productifs de son travail, de sa terre, et de ses capitaux, pour obtenir des produits. C’est par le moyen de ces services productifs, que nous acquérons tous les produits qui sont au monde. Or... nous sommes d’autant plus riches, nos services productifs ont d’autant plus de valeur qu’ils obtiennent dans l’échange appelé production une plus grande quantité de choses utiles.” [As for the difficulty Mr. Ricardo raises when he says that, by better-understood methods, a million people can produce two times, three times as much wealth without producing more values, this difficulty is none when one considers, as one should, production as an exchange in which one gives the productive services of one’s labor, one’s land, and one’s capital in order to obtain products. It is by means of these productive services that we acquire all the products that are in the world. Now ... we are the richer, our productive services have the more value, the greater the quantity of useful things they obtain in the exchange called production.] (J. B. Say, “Lettres à M. Malthus,” Paris, 1820, pp. 168, 169.) The “difficulté” [difficulty] – it exists for him, not for Ricardo – that Say means to clear up is this: Why does the exchange-value of use-values not increase when their quantity increases because of an increased productive power of labor? Answer: the difficulty is met by calling use-value exchange-value, if you please. Exchange-value is a thing that is connected one way or another with exchange. So if production is called an exchange of labor and means of production for the product, it is as clear as day that you get more exchange-value in proportion as production yields more use-value. In other words, the more use-values, for example stockings, a working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor) yields to the stocking-manufacturer, the richer he is in stockings. Suddenly, however, Say remembers that “with a greater quantity” of stockings their “price” (which of course has nothing to do with their exchange-value!) falls “parce que la concurrence les (les producteurs) oblige à donner les produits pour ce qu’ils leur coûtent... [because competition obliges them (the producers) to give up the products for what they cost them] But where does the profit come from, if the capitalist sells the commodities at cost-price? Never mind! Say declares that, as a result of increased productivity, everyone now receives two pairs of stockings in return for a given equivalent instead of one as before. The result he arrives at is precisely the proposition of Ricardo that he set out to disprove. After this mighty effort of thought, he triumphantly addresses Malthus in these words: “Telle est, monsieur, la doctrine bien liée, sans laquelle il est impossible, je le déclare, d’expliquer les plus grandes difficultés de l’économie politique, et notamment, comment il se peut qu’une nation soit plus riche lorsque ses produits diminuent de valeur, quoique la richesse soit de la valeur.” [Such, Sir, is the well-connected doctrine without which it is impossible, I declare, to explain the greatest difficulties of political economy, and in particular how it can be that a nation is richer when its products fall in value, although wealth is value.] (Cited above, p. 170.) An English economist remarks on the conjuring tricks of the same kind that appear in Say’s “Lettres”: “Those affected ways of talking make up in general that which M. Say is pleased to call his doctrine and which he earnestly urges Malthus to teach at Hertford, as it is already taught ‘dans plusieurs parties de l’Europe.’ He says, ‘Si vous trouvez une physionomie de paradoxe à toutes ces propositions, voyez les choses qu’elles expriment, et j’ose croire qu’elles vous paraîtront fort simples et fort raisonnables.’ [in several parts of Europe ... If you find all these propositions look like a paradox, look at the things they express, and I dare to believe that they will seem to you very simple and very reasonable] Doubtless, and in consequence of the same process, they will appear everything else, except original.” (“An Inquiry into those Principles Respecting the Nature of Demand, &c.,” pp. 116, 110.)

<!-- ch24-p100 -->
As capital grows, the difference between the capital employed and the capital consumed grows. In other words, there is an increase in the value and the material mass of the instruments of labor: buildings, machinery, drain-pipes, working cattle, and apparatus of every kind. These function for a longer or shorter time in processes of production that are constantly repeated, or serve to achieve particular useful effects, while they themselves wear out only gradually. So they lose their value only piece by piece, and so pass that value on to the product only bit by bit. To the same extent that these instruments of labor serve as makers of products without adding value to the product, that is, to the extent that they are wholly employed but only partly consumed, they perform, as we saw earlier, the same free service as the natural forces, water, steam, air, electricity, etc. This free service of past labor, when it is seized and filled with a soul by living labor, increases with the advancing stages of accumulation.

<!-- ch24-p101 -->
Past labor always disguises itself as capital. That is, the passive of the labor of A, B, C, etc., takes the form of the active of the non-worker X. Because of this, bourgeois and political economists are full of praise for the services of dead and gone labor, which, according to the Scottish genius MacCulloch, ought to receive a special remuneration in the shape of interest, profit, etc.[^48] So the powerful and ever-growing help that past labor gives to the living labor process, in the form of means of production, is credited to that form of past labor in which it is alienated from the worker himself as unpaid labor, that is, to its capitalist form. The practical agents of capitalist production and their petty, quibbling ideologists are as unable to think of the means of production as separate from the antagonistic social mask they wear today as a slave-owner is unable to think of the worker himself as distinct from his character as a slave.

[^48]: MacCulloch took out a patent on “wages of past labour” long before Senior did on “wages of abstinence.”

<!-- ch24-p102 -->
With a given degree of exploitation of labor-power, the mass of surplus-value produced is determined by the number of workers exploited at the same time. This number corresponds, although in varying proportions, to the size of the capital. So the more capital grows through successive accumulations, the more the sum of value grows that is divided into the consumption fund and the accumulation fund. The capitalist can therefore live a jollier life and at the same time show more “abstinence.” And finally, all the springs of production act with greater elasticity the more their scale extends with the mass of capital advanced.
