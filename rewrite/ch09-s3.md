### Section 3: Senior’s “Last Hour”

<!-- ch09-p037 -->
One fine morning in the year 1836, Nassau W. Senior was summoned from Oxford to Manchester. Senior may be called the leading wit among English economists, and he was well known both for his economic “science” and for his beautiful style. He was summoned to learn in Manchester the political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) that he taught in Oxford. The manufacturers chose him as their champion, not only against the newly passed Factory Act, but against the still more threatening agitation for a ten-hour day. With their usual practical sharpness, they had found out that the learned Professor “wanted [needed] a good deal of finishing;” it was this discovery that made them write to summon him. For his part, the Professor wrote up the lecture he had received from the Manchester manufacturers in a pamphlet, entitled: “Letters on the Factory Act, as it affects the cotton manufacture.” London, 1837. In it we find, among other things, the following edifying passage:

<!-- ch09-p038 -->
> “Under the present law, no mill in which persons under 18 years of age are employed, ... can be worked more than 11½ hours a day, that is, 12 hours for 5 days in the week, and nine on Saturday.

<!-- ch09-p039 -->
> “Now the following analysis (!) will show that in a mill so worked, the whole net profit is derived *from the last hour*. I will suppose a manufacturer to invest £100,000: – £80,000 in his mill and machinery, and £20,000 in raw material and wages. The annual return of that mill, supposing the capital to be turned [turned over] once a year, and gross profits to be 15 per cent., ought to be goods worth £115,000.... Of this £115,000, each of the twenty-three half-hours of work produces 5-115ths or one twenty-third. Of these 23-23rds (constituting the whole £115,000) twenty, that is to say £100,000 out of the £115,000, simply replace the capital; – one twenty-third (or £5,000 out of the £115,000) makes up for the deterioration of the mill and machinery. The remaining 2-23rds, that is, the last two of the twenty-three half-hours of every day, produce the net profit of 10 per cent. If, therefore (prices remaining the same), the factory could be kept at work thirteen hours instead of eleven and a half, with an addition of about £2,600 to the circulating capital, the net profit would be more than doubled. On the other hand, if the hours of working were reduced by one hour per day (prices remaining the same), the *net* profit would be destroyed – if they were reduced by one hour and a half, even the *gross* profit would be destroyed.”[^10]

[^10]: Senior, cited above, pp. 12, 13. We pass over such extraordinary notions as do not matter for our purpose. One example is the claim that manufacturers count as part of their profit, gross or net, the amount needed to make good the wear and tear of machinery, or in other words, to replace a part of the capital. In the same way, we pass over any question of whether his figures are accurate. Leonard Horner has shown in “A Letter to Mr. Senior,” etc., London, 1837, that they are worth no more than the so-called “Analysis.” Leonard Horner was one of the Factory Inquiry Commissioners in 1833, and Inspector, or rather Censor, of Factories until 1859. He did undying service to the English working class. He fought a lifelong battle, not only with the embittered manufacturers, but also with the Cabinet, which cared far more about the number of votes the employers cast in the Lower House than about the number of hours the “hands” worked in the mills.

    Apart from questions of principle, Senior’s statement is muddled. What he really meant to say was this: The manufacturer employs the worker for 11½ hours, or for 23 half-hours, a day. Like the working day, the working year may be thought of as consisting of 11½ hours or 23 half-hours, but each multiplied by the number of working days in the year. On this assumption, the 23 half-hours yield an annual product of £115,000; one half-hour yields 1/23 × £115,000; 20 half-hours yield 20/23 × £115,000 = £100,000, that is, they replace no more than the capital advanced. There remain 3 half-hours, which yield 1/23 × £115,000 = £5,000 or the gross profit. Of these 3 half-hours, one yields 1/23 × £115,000 = £5,000; that is, it makes up for the wear and tear of the machinery; the remaining 2 half-hours, that is, the last hour, yield 2/23 × £115,000 = £10,000 or the net profit. In the text, Senior turns the last 2/23 of the product into portions of the working day itself.

<!-- ch09-p040 -->
And the Professor calls this an “analysis!” Suppose he trusted the outcries of the manufacturers, and believed that the workers spend the best part of the day producing, that is, reproducing or replacing, the value of the buildings, machinery, cotton, coal, etc. In that case his analysis was unnecessary. His answer would simply have been: – Gentlemen! if you work your mills for 10 hours instead of 11½, then, other things being equal, the daily consumption of cotton, machinery, etc., will decrease in proportion. You gain just as much as you lose. In future your workers will spend one hour and a half less in reproducing or replacing the capital that has been advanced. – Suppose, on the other hand, that he did not believe them without further inquiry, but, as an expert in such matters, thought an analysis necessary. In that case he ought, in a question that concerns only the relation of net profit to the length of the working day, first of all to have asked the manufacturers to be careful not to lump together machinery, workshops, raw material, and labor. He ought to have asked them to be good enough to place the constant capital, invested in buildings, machinery, raw material, etc., on one side of the account, and the capital advanced in wages on the other side. If the Professor then found that, according to the manufacturers’ calculation, the worker reproduced or replaced his wages in 2 half-hours, then he should have continued his analysis like this:

<!-- ch09-p041 -->
According to your figures, the worker produces his wages in the last hour but one, and your surplus-value or net profit in the last hour. Now, since he produces equal values in equal periods, the product of the last hour but one must have the same value as that of the last hour. Further, he produces value only while he labors, and the amount of his labor is measured by his labor-time. This, you say, amounts to 11½ hours a day. He uses one portion of these 11½ hours in producing or replacing his wages, and the remaining portion in producing your net profit. Beyond this he does absolutely nothing. But since, on your assumption, his wages and the surplus-value he yields are of equal value, it is clear that he produces his wages in 5¾ hours, and your net profit in the other 5¾ hours. Again, since the value of the yarn produced in 2 hours is equal to the sum of the values of his wages and of your net profit, the measure of the value of this yarn must be 11½ working-hours. Of these, 5¾ hours measure the value of the yarn produced in the last hour but one, and 5¾ the value of the yarn produced in the last hour. Now we come to a tricky point, so pay attention! The last working-hour but one is, like the first, an ordinary working-hour, neither more nor less. How then can the spinner produce in one hour, in the shape of yarn, a value that embodies 5¾ hours’ labor? The truth is that he performs no such miracle. The use-value he produces in one hour is a definite quantity of yarn. (Use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties.) The value of this yarn is measured by 5¾ working-hours. Of these, 4¾ were already embodied in the means of production, in the cotton, the machinery, and so on, without any help from him; he adds only the remaining one hour. So since his wages are produced in 5¾ hours, and the yarn produced in one hour also contains 5¾ hours’ work, there is no witchcraft in the result that the value created by his 5¾ hours of spinning is equal to the value of the product spun in one hour. You are altogether on the wrong track if you think that he loses a single moment of his working day in reproducing or replacing the values of the cotton, the machinery, and so on. On the contrary, it is because his labor turns the cotton and spindles into yarn, because he spins, that the values of the cotton and spindles pass over to the yarn of their own accord. This result is due to the quality of his labor, not to its quantity. It is true that in one hour he will transfer more value to the yarn, in the shape of cotton, than in half an hour; but that is only because in one hour he spins up more cotton than in half an hour. So you see, your assertion that the worker produces the value of his wages in the last hour but one, and your net profit in the last hour, amounts to no more than this: in the yarn he produces in 2 working-hours, whether they are the first 2 or the last 2 hours of the working day, in that yarn there are incorporated 11½ working-hours, or just a whole day’s work, that is, two hours of his own work and 9½ hours of other people’s. And my assertion that he produces his wages in the first 5¾ hours, and your net profit in the last 5¾ hours, amounts only to this: you pay him for the former, but not for the latter. When I speak of payment of labor, instead of payment of labor-power, I am only talking your own slang. Now, gentlemen, if you compare the working time you pay for with the working time you do not pay for, you will find that they are to one another as half a day is to half a day. This gives a rate of 100%, and a very pretty percentage it is. Further, there is not the least doubt that if you make your “hands” toil for 13 hours instead of 11½, and, as may be expected of you, treat the work done in that extra hour and a half as pure surplus labor, then surplus labor will rise from 5¾ hours’ labor to 7¼ hours’ labor, and the rate of surplus-value from 100% to 126 2/23%. So you are far too hopeful in expecting that adding 1½ hours to the working day will raise the rate from 100% to 200% and more, in other words that it will be “more than doubled.” On the other hand ‒ man’s heart is a wonderful thing, especially when carried in the purse – you take too pessimistic a view when you fear that, if the hours of labor are cut from 11½ to 10, the whole of your net profit will go to the dogs. Not at all. If all other conditions remain the same, surplus labor will fall from 5¾ hours to 4¾ hours, a period that still gives a very profitable rate of surplus-value, namely 82 14/23%. But this dreadful “last hour,” about which you have invented more stories than the millenarians have about the day of judgment, is “all bosh.” If it goes, it will cost neither you your net profit, nor the boys and girls you employ their “purity of mind.”[^11] Whenever your “last hour” strikes in earnest, think of the Oxford Professor. And now, gentlemen, “farewell, and may we meet again in yonder better world, but not before.”

[^11]: If, on the one hand, Senior proved that the manufacturer’s net profit, the existence of the English cotton industry, and England’s command of the markets of the world depend on “the last working-hour,” then, on the other hand, Dr. Andrew Ure showed the following. If children and young persons under 18 years of age are turned out an hour sooner into the heartless and frivolous outside world, instead of being kept the full 12 hours in the warm and pure moral atmosphere of the factory, idleness and vice will rob them of all hope of salvation for their souls. Since 1848, the factory inspectors have never tired of taunting the employers with this “last,” this “fatal hour.” Thus Mr. Hovell in his report of the 21st May, 1855: “Had the following ingenious calculation (he quotes Senior) been correct, every cotton factory in the United Kingdom would have been working at a loss since the year 1850.” (Reports of the Insp. of Fact., for the half-year, ending 30th April, 1855, pp. 19, 20.) In the year 1848, after the 10 hours’ bill was passed, the employers of some flax spinning mills, scattered few and far between over the country on the borders of Dorset and Somerset, foisted a petition against the bill onto the shoulders of a few of their workers. One of the clauses of this petition reads: “Your petitioners, as parents, conceive that an additional hour of leisure will tend more to demoralise the children than otherwise, believing that idleness is the parent of vice.” On this the factory report of 31st Oct., 1848, says: The atmosphere of the flax mills, in which the children of these virtuous and tender parents work, is so loaded with dust and fibre from the raw material, that it is exceptionally unpleasant to stand even 10 minutes in the spinning rooms: for you are unable to do so without the most painful sensation, owing to the eyes, the ears, the nostrils, and mouth, being immediately filled by the clouds of flax dust from which there is no escape. The labour itself, owing to the feverish haste of the machinery, demands unceasing application of skill and movement, under the control of a watchfulness that never tires, and it seems somewhat hard, to let parents apply the term “idling” to their own children, who, after allowing for meal-times, are fettered for 10 whole hours to such an occupation, in such an atmosphere.... These children work longer than the labourers in the neighbouring villages.... Such cruel talk about “idleness and vice” ought to be branded as the purest cant [hypocritical talk], and the most shameless hypocrisy.... That portion of the public, who, about 12 years ago, were struck by the assurance with which, under the sanction of high authority, it was publicly and most earnestly proclaimed, that the whole net profit of the manufacturer flows from the labour of the last hour, and that, therefore, the reduction of the working day by one hour, would destroy his net profit, that portion of the public, we say, will hardly believe its own eyes, when it now finds, that the original discovery of the virtues of “the last hour” has since been so far improved, as to include morals as well as profit; so that, if the duration of the labour of children, is reduced to a full 10 hours, their morals, together with the net profits of their employers, will vanish, both being dependent on this last, this fatal hour. (See Repts., Insp. of Fact., for 31st Oct., 1848, p. 101.) The same report then gives some examples of the morality and virtue of these same pure-minded manufacturers: of the tricks, the dodges, the coaxing, the threats, and the falsifications they used, first, to force a few defenseless workers to sign petitions of this kind, and then to pass them off on Parliament as the petitions of a whole branch of industry, or a whole country. It is highly typical of the present state of so-called economic science that neither Senior himself, who later, to his honor be it said, energetically supported the factory legislation, nor his opponents, from first to last, have ever been able to explain the false conclusions of the “original discovery.” They appeal to actual experience, but the why and wherefore remains a mystery.

Senior invented the battle cry of the “last hour” in 1836.[^12] In the London *Economist* of the 15th April, 1848, James Wilson, an economic mandarin of high standing, raised the same cry again: this time against the 10 hours’ bill.

[^12]: All the same, the learned professor did get some benefit from his journey to Manchester. In the “Letters on the Factory Act,” he makes the whole net gains, including “profit” and “interests” and even “something more,” depend on a single unpaid hour’s work by the worker. One year earlier, in his “Outlines of Political Economy,” written for the instruction of Oxford students and cultivated Philistines, he had also “discovered, in opposition to Ricardo’s determination of value by labour, that profit is derived from the labour of the capitalist, and interest from his asceticism, in other words, from his abstinence.” The trick was an old one, but the word “abstinence” was new. Herr Roscher translates it rightly by “Enthaltung” [abstention]. Some of his countrymen, the Browns, Jones, and Robinsons of Germany, less well versed in Latin than he is, have, monk-like, rendered it by “Entsagung” (renunciation).
