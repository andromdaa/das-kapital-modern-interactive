### Section 8: Revolution Effected in Manufacture, Handicrafts, and Domestic Industry by Modern Industry

#### A. Overthrow of Cooperation Based on Handicraft and on the Division of Labor

<!-- ch15-p192 -->
We have seen how machinery does away with cooperation based on handicrafts, and with manufacture based on the division of handicraft labor. An example of the first kind is the mowing-machine: it replaces cooperation between mowers. A striking example of the second kind is the needle-making machine. According to Adam Smith, 10 men in his day, working in cooperation, made over 48,000 needles a day. A single needle-machine, on the other hand, makes 145,000 in a working day of 11 hours. One woman or one girl supervises four such machines, and so produces close to 600,000 needles in a day, and more than 3,000,000 in a week.[^166] When a single machine takes the place of cooperation or of manufacture, it may itself serve as the basis of an industry of a handicraft character. Still, such a return to handicrafts is only a transition to the factory system. As a rule, the factory system appears as soon as a mechanical motive power, such as steam or water, replaces human muscles for driving the machines. Here and there, but in any case only for a time, an industry may be carried on on a small scale by means of mechanical power. This is done by hiring steam-power, as in some of the Birmingham trades, or by using small caloric-engines, as in some branches of weaving.[^167] In the Coventry silk-weaving industry the experiment of “cottage factories” was tried. An engine-house was built in the center of a square surrounded by rows of cottages, and the engine was connected by shafts with the looms in the cottages. In all cases the power was hired at so much per loom. The rent was payable weekly, whether the looms worked or not. Each cottage held from 2 to 6 looms; some belonged to the weaver, some were bought on credit, some were hired. The struggle between these cottage factories and the factory proper lasted over 12 years. It ended with the complete ruin of the 300 cottage factories.[^168] Wherever the nature of the process did not involve production on a large scale, the new industries that have sprung up in the last few decades, such as envelope making, steel-pen making, etc., have as a general rule passed first through the handicraft stage and then through the manufacturing stage, as short phases of transition to the factory stage. The transition is very difficult where making the article by manufacture consists not of a series of graduated processes but of a great number of disconnected ones. This circumstance was a great obstacle to setting up steel-pen factories. Nevertheless, about 15 years ago, a machine was invented that automatically performed 6 separate operations at once. The first steel-pens were supplied by the handicraft system, in the year 1820, at £7 4s. the gross; in 1830 manufacture supplied them at 8s.; and today the factory system supplies them to the trade at from 2 to 6d. the gross.[^169]

[^166]: “Ch. Empl. Comm. III. Report, 1864,” p. 108, n. 447.

[^167]: In the United States this kind of restoration of handicrafts based on machinery is frequent. Therefore, when the inevitable transition to the factory system takes place, the concentration that follows will, compared with Europe and even with England, stride on in seven-league boots.

[^168]: See “Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1865,” p. 64.

[^169]: Mr. Gillott built the first large-scale steel-pen factory in Birmingham. As early as 1851 it produced over 180,000,000 pens a year, and consumed 120 tons of steel. Birmingham has the monopoly of this industry in the United Kingdom, and at present produces thousands of millions of steel-pens. According to the Census of 1861, the number of persons employed was 1,428, of whom 1,268 were females from 5 years of age upwards.

#### B. Reaction of the Factory System on Manufacture and Domestic Industries

<!-- ch15-p193 -->
Along with the development of the factory system, and of the revolution in agriculture that goes with it, production in all the other branches of industry not only expands but also changes its character. The factory system carries out the principle of analyzing the process of production into its constituent phases, and of solving the problems this poses by applying mechanics, chemistry, and the whole range of the natural sciences. This principle now becomes the determining principle everywhere. Hence machinery squeezes its way into the manufacturing industries, first for one detail process, then for another. In this way the solid crystal of their organization, based on the old division of labor, is dissolved and gives way to constant changes. Independently of this, a radical change takes place in the composition of the collective worker, a change in the persons working in combination. In contrast with the manufacturing period, the division of labor is from then on based, wherever possible, on employing women, children of all ages, and unskilled workers, in one word, on cheap labor, as it is characteristically called in England. This is the case not only with all large-scale production, whether it uses machinery or not, but also with the so-called domestic industry, whether carried on in the workers' houses or in small workshops. This modern so-called domestic industry has nothing in common, except the name, with the old-fashioned domestic industry. The existence of the old kind presupposes independent urban handicrafts, independent peasant farming, and above all a dwelling-house for the worker and his family. That old-fashioned industry has now been turned into an outside department of the factory, the manufacturing workshop, or the warehouse. Besides the factory workers, the manufacturing workers, and the craftsmen, whom it concentrates in large masses in one place and commands directly, capital also sets in motion another army by means of invisible threads: the workers in the domestic industries, who live in the large towns and are also scattered over the face of the country. An example: the shirt factory of Messrs. Tillie at Londonderry, which employs 1,000 factory workers in the factory itself, and 9,000 people spread up and down the country and working in their own houses.[^170]

[^170]: “Ch. Empl. Comm. II. Rep. 1864,” p. LXVIII., n. 415.

<!-- ch15-p194 -->
The exploitation of cheap and immature labor-power is carried out more shamelessly in modern manufacture than in the factory proper. This is because the technical foundation of the factory system, namely the replacement of muscular power by machines and the light character of the labor, is almost entirely absent in manufacture. At the same time, women and over-young children are exposed, in a most unconscionable way, to the influence of poisonous or harmful substances. This exploitation is more shameless in the so-called domestic industry than in manufacture. The reasons are these: the workers' power of resistance decreases as they are scattered; a whole series of plundering parasites push their way in between the employer and the worker; a domestic industry always has to compete either with the factory system or with manufacture in the same branch of production; poverty robs the worker of the conditions most essential to his labor, of space, light, and ventilation; employment becomes more and more irregular; and, finally, in these last refuges of the masses made “redundant” by modern industry and agriculture, competition for work reaches its maximum. Economy in the means of production was first carried out systematically in the factory system. There, from the very beginning, it went hand in hand with the most reckless squandering of labor-power and with robbery of the conditions normally needed for labor. This economy now shows its antagonistic and murderous side more and more in a given branch of industry, the less the social productive power of labor and the technical basis for a combination of processes are developed in that branch.

#### C. Modern Manufacture

<!-- ch15-p195 -->
I now go on to illustrate the principles laid down above with a few examples. In fact, the reader already knows numerous instances from the chapter on the working day. In the hardware manufacture of Birmingham and its neighborhood, 30,000 children and young persons, besides 10,000 women, are employed, mostly in very heavy work. They are to be seen there in the unhealthy brass-foundries, button factories, and enameling, galvanizing, and lacquering works.[^171] Because of the excessive labor of their workers, both adult and non-adult, certain London houses where newspapers and books are printed have earned the ill-omened name of “slaughterhouses.”[^172] Similar excesses occur in book-binding, where the victims are chiefly women, girls, and children. Young persons have to do heavy work in rope-walks (long sheds where rope is made) and night-work in salt mines, candle manufacturing workshops, and chemical works. Young people are worked to death turning the looms in silk weaving, where it is not carried on by machinery.[^173] One of the most shameful, the dirtiest, and the worst-paid kinds of labor, and one for which women and young girls are preferred, is the sorting of rags. It is well known that Great Britain, apart from its own immense store of rags, is the central market for the rag trade of the whole world. Rags flow in from Japan, from the most remote states of South America, and from the Canary Islands. But the chief sources of supply are Germany, France, Russia, Italy, Egypt, Turkey, Belgium, and Holland. They are used for manure, for stuffing bedding, for shoddy (cloth made from shredded rags), and as the raw material of paper. The rag-sorters are the medium through which small-pox and other infectious diseases spread, and they themselves are the first victims.[^174] A classic example of overwork, of hard and unsuitable labor, and of its brutalizing effects on the worker from childhood on, is found not only in coal-mining and among miners generally, but also in tile and brick making. In this industry, in England, the recently invented machinery is used only here and there. Between May and September the work lasts from 5 in the morning till 8 in the evening, and where the drying is done in the open air it often lasts from 4 in the morning till 9 in the evening. Work from 5 in the morning till 7 in the evening is considered “reduced” and “moderate.” Both boys and girls of 6, and even of 4, years of age are employed. They work the same number of hours as the adults, often longer. The work is hard, and the summer heat adds to the exhaustion. In a certain tile-field at Mosley, for example, a young woman 24 years of age used to make 2,000 tiles a day, with the help of 2 little girls who carried the clay for her and stacked the tiles. These girls carried 10 tons a day up the slippery sides of the clay pits, from a depth of 30 feet, and then for a distance of 210 feet.

#### D. Modern Domestic Industry

<!-- ch15-p205 -->
I now come to the so-called domestic industry. To get an idea of the horrors of this sphere, in which capital carries on its exploitation in the background of modern mechanical industry, one must go to the apparently quite idyllic trade of nail-making,[^178] carried on in a few remote villages of England. Here, however, it will be enough to give a few examples from those branches of the lace-making and straw-plaiting industries that are not yet carried on with the help of machinery, and that do not yet compete with branches carried on in factories or in manufacturing workshops.

[^178]: I mean here hammered nails, as distinguished from nails cut out and made by machinery. See “Child. Empl. Comm., Third Rep.,” pp. xi., xix., n. 125-130, p. 52, n. 11, p. 114, n. 487, p. 137, n. 674.

Of the 150,000 persons employed in England in the production of lace, about 10,000 come under the authority of the Factory Act, 1861. Almost all of the remaining 140,000 are women, young persons, and children of both sexes, though the male sex is weakly represented. The Factory Act of 1861 regulates the actual making of the lace, insofar as it is done by machinery, and this is the rule in England. The branches we are now about to examine, looking only at the workers who work at home and not at those who work in manufacturing workshops or warehouses, fall into two divisions, namely (1) finishing and (2) mending. Finishing gives the final touches to the machine-made lace, and includes numerous sub-divisions.

<!-- ch15-p210 -->
Lace finishing is done either in what are called “mistresses’ houses,” or by women in their own houses, with or without the help of their children. The women who keep the “mistresses’ houses” are themselves poor. The workroom is in a private house. The mistresses take orders from manufacturers or from warehousemen, and employ as many women, girls, and young children as the size of their rooms and the fluctuating demand of the business will allow. The number of workwomen employed in these workrooms varies from 20 to 40 in some, and from 10 to 20 in others. The average age at which the children start work is six years, but in many cases it is below five. The usual working hours are from 8 in the morning till eight in the evening, with 1½ hours for meals, which are taken at irregular intervals and often in the foul workrooms. When business is brisk, the labor often lasts from 8 or even 6 o’clock in the morning till 10, 11, or 12 o’clock at night. In English barracks the regulation space allotted to each soldier is 500-600 cubic feet, and in the military hospitals 1,200 cubic feet. But in those finishing sties there are only 67 to 100 cubic feet to each person. At the same time, gas-lights consume the oxygen of the air. To keep the lace clean, the children are often forced to take off their shoes, even in winter, although the floor is tiled or flagged.

> “It is not at all uncommon in Nottingham to find 14 to 20 children huddled together in a small room, of, perhaps, not more than 12 feet square, and employed for 15 hours out of the 24, at work that of itself is exhausting, from its weariness and monotony, and is besides carried on under every possible unwholesome condition.... Even the very youngest children work with a strained attention and a rapidity that is astonishing, hardly ever giving their fingers rest or glowering their motion. If a question be asked them, they never raise their eyes from their work from fear of losing a single moment.”

When women and their children work at home, which nowadays means in a hired room, often in an attic, things are, if possible, still worse. This sort of work is given out within a radius of 80 miles around Nottingham. When the children leave the warehouses at 9 or 10 o’clock at night, they are often given a bundle of lace to take home with them and finish. The Pharisee of a capitalist, represented by one of his servants, accompanies this act, of course, with the oily phrase: “That’s for mother.” Yet he knows well enough that the poor children must sit up and help.[^181]

[^181]: Cited above, pp. xxi., xxii.

<!-- ch15-p215 -->
Pillow lace-making is carried on in England chiefly in two agricultural districts. One is the Honiton lace district, which stretches from 20 to 30 miles along the south coast of Devonshire and includes a few places in North Devon. The other takes in a great part of the counties of Buckingham, Bedford, and Northampton, and also the neighboring parts of Oxfordshire and Huntingdonshire. The work is usually carried on in the cottages of the farm laborers. Many manufacturers employ more than 3,000 of these lace-makers, who are chiefly children and young persons, exclusively of the female sex. The state of things described for lace finishing is repeated here, except that instead of the “mistresses’ houses” we find what are called “lace-schools,” kept by poor women in their cottages. The children work in these schools from their fifth year, and often earlier, until their twelfth or fifteenth year. During the first year the very young ones work from four to eight hours, and later on from six in the morning till eight and ten o’clock at night.

<!-- ch15-p216 -->
> “The rooms are generally the ordinary living rooms of small cottages, the chimney stopped up to keep out draughts, the inmates kept warm by their own animal heat alone, and this frequently in winter. In other cases, these so-called school-rooms are like small store-rooms without fire-places.... The over-crowding in these dens and the consequent vitiation [fouling] of the air are often extreme. Added to this is the injurious effect of drains, privies [outdoor toilets], decomposing substances, and other filth usual in the purlieus [surroundings] of the smaller cottages.” With regard to space: “In one lace-school 18 girls and a mistress, 35 cubic feet to each person; in another, where the smell was unbearable, 18 persons and 24½ cubic feet per head. In this industry are to be found employed children of 2 and 2½ years.”[^182]

[^182]: Cited above, pp. xxix., xxx.

Where lace-making ends in the counties of Buckingham and Bedford, straw-plaiting begins, and it extends over a large part of Hertfordshire and the western and northern parts of Essex. In 1861 there were 40,043 persons employed in straw-plaiting and straw-hat making. Of these, 3,815 were males of all ages, and the rest females, of whom 14,913, including about 7,000 children, were under 20 years of age. In place of the lace-schools we find here the “straw-plait schools.” The children generally begin their instruction in straw-plaiting in their 4th year, often between their 3rd and 4th year. Education, of course, they get none. The children themselves call the elementary schools “natural schools,” to distinguish them from these blood-sucking institutions. In these they are kept at work simply to get through the task prescribed by their half-starved mothers, generally 30 yards a day. These same mothers often make them work at home after school is over, till 10, 11, and 12 o’clock at night. The straw cuts their mouths, with which they constantly moisten it, and their fingers. Dr. Ballard gives it as the general opinion of the whole body of medical officers in London that 300 cubic feet is the minimum proper space for each person in a bedroom or workroom. But in the straw-plait schools space is allotted more sparingly than in the lace-schools: “12 2/3, 17, 18½ and below 22 cubic feet for each person.”

<!-- ch15-p218 -->
> “The smaller of these numbers, says one of the commissioners, Mr. White, represents less space than the half of what a child would occupy if packed in a box measuring 3 feet in each direction.”
