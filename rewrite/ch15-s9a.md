### Section 9: The Factory Acts. Sanitary and Educational Clauses of the same. Their General Extension in England

<!-- ch15-p243 -->
Factory legislation is the first conscious and methodical reaction of society against the form that the process of production developed spontaneously. As we have seen, it is just as much the necessary product of modern industry as cotton yarn, self-actors, and the electric telegraph. Before we move on to how that legislation was extended in England, we shall look briefly at certain clauses in the Factory Acts that do not concern the hours of work.

<!-- ch15-p244 -->
Apart from their wording, which makes them easy for the capitalist to evade, the sanitary clauses are extremely meager. In fact, they are limited to provisions for whitewashing the walls, for ensuring cleanliness in a few other matters, for ventilation, and for protection against dangerous machinery. In the third book we shall come back to the fanatical opposition of the employers to those clauses that made them spend a little on appliances to protect the limbs of their workers. That opposition throws a fresh and glaring light on the Free-trade dogma, according to which, in a society of conflicting interests, each individual necessarily furthers the common good by seeking nothing but his own personal advantage! One example is enough. The reader knows that during the last 20 years the flax industry has expanded greatly, and that with this expansion the number of scutching mills in Ireland has increased. In 1864 there were 1,800 of these mills in that country. Regularly in autumn and winter, women and “young persons” are taken from field labor to feed flax into the rollers of the scutching mills. They are the wives, sons, and daughters of the neighboring small farmers, a class of people wholly unused to machinery. The accidents, in both number and kind, are without any parallel in the history of machinery. In one scutching mill, at Kildinan, near Cork, there were six fatal accidents and sixty mutilations between 1852 and 1856. Every one of them could have been prevented by the simplest appliances, at the cost of a few shillings. Dr. W. White, the certifying surgeon for factories at Downpatrick, states in his official report dated the 15th December, 1865:

<!-- ch15-p245 -->
> “The serious accidents at the scutching mills are of the most fearful nature. In many cases a quarter of the body is torn from the trunk, and either involves death, or a future of wretched incapacity and suffering. The increase of mills in the country will, of course, extend these dreadful results, and it will be a great boon if they are brought under the legislature. I am convinced that by proper supervision of scutching mills a vast sacrifice of life and limb would be averted.”[^214]

[^214]: Cited above, p. xv., n. 72. and following.

<!-- ch15-p246 -->
What could show the character of the capitalist mode of production better than the need to force on it, by Acts of Parliament, the simplest appliances for keeping things clean and healthy? In the potteries the Factory Act of 1864 “has whitewashed and cleansed upwards of 200 workshops, after a period of abstinence from any such cleaning, in many cases of 20 years, and in some, entirely,” (this is the “abstinence” of the capitalist!) “in which were employed 27,800 artisans, hitherto breathing through protracted days and often nights of labour, a mephitic [foul and poisonous] atmosphere, and which rendered an otherwise comparatively innocuous occupation, pregnant with disease and death. The Act has improved the ventilation very much.”[^215]

[^215]: “Rep. Insp. Fact., 31st October, 1865,” p. 127.

<!-- ch15-p247 -->
At the same time, this part of the Act shows strikingly that the capitalist mode of production, by its very nature, rules out all rational improvement beyond a certain point. It has been stated over and over again that the English doctors agree unanimously: where the work is continuous, 500 cubic feet is the very least space that should be allowed for each person. Now, if the Factory Acts, through their compulsory provisions, indirectly speed up the conversion of small workshops into factories, and so indirectly attack the property rights of the smaller capitalists and assure a monopoly to the great ones, then in the same way, if it were made compulsory to provide the proper space for each worker in every workshop, thousands of small employers would be expropriated directly, at a single stroke! The very root of the capitalist mode of production would be attacked, that is, the self-expansion of all capital, large or small, by means of the “free” purchase and consumption of labor-power. So factory legislation comes to a deadlock in front of these 500 cubic feet of breathing space. The sanitary officers, the industrial inquiry commissioners, the factory inspectors all harp, over and over again, on the need for those 500 cubic feet, and on the impossibility of wringing them out of capital. In fact, they are thereby declaring that consumption and other lung diseases among the workers are necessary conditions for the existence of capital.[^216]

[^216]: Experiments have found that with each breath of average intensity, a healthy average person uses up about 25 cubic inches of air, and takes about 20 breaths each minute. Hence the air each person breathes in during 24 hours is about 720,000 cubic inches, or 416 cubic feet. Clearly, however, air that has been breathed once cannot serve for the same process again until it has been purified in the great workshop of Nature. According to the experiments of Valentin and Brunner, it appears that a healthy man gives off about 1,300 cubic inches of carbonic acid per hour. This would mean about 8 ounces of solid carbon given off from the lungs in 24 hours. “Every man should have at least 800 cubic feet.” (Huxley.)

Paltry as the education clauses of the Act appear on the whole, they still proclaim elementary education to be an indispensable condition for employing children.[^217] Their success proved for the first time that it is possible to combine education and gymnastics[^218] with manual labor, and so also to combine manual labor with education and gymnastics. By questioning the schoolmasters, the factory inspectors soon found out that the factory children learned quite as much as the regular day pupils, and often more, although they received only half as much schooling.

[^217]: Under the English Factory Act, parents cannot send their children under 14 years of age into factories controlled by the Act unless they also let them receive elementary education at the same time. The manufacturer is responsible for compliance with the Act. “Factory education is compulsory, and it is a condition of labour.” (“Rep. Insp. Fact., 31st Oct., 1865,” p. 111.)

[^218]: On the very good results of combining gymnastics (and drill, in the case of boys) with compulsory education for factory children and pauper pupils, see the speech of N. W. Senior at the seventh annual congress of “The National Association for the Promotion of Social Science,” in “Report of Proceedings, &c.,” London 1863, pp. 63, 64, and also the “Rep. Insp. Fact., 31st Oct., 1865,” pp. 118, 119, 120, 126, and following.

<!-- ch15-p249 -->
> “This can be accounted for by the simple fact that, with only being at school for one half of the day, they are always fresh, and nearly always ready and willing to receive instruction. The system on which they work, half manual labour, and half school, renders each employment a rest and a relief to the other; consequently, both are far more congenial to the child, than would be the case were he kept constantly at one. It is quite clear that a boy who has been at school all the morning, cannot (in hot weather particularly) cope with one who comes fresh and bright from his work.”[^219]

[^219]: “Rep. Insp. Fact., 31st Oct., 1865,” p. 118. A silk manufacturer naively states to the Children’s Employment Commissioners: “I am quite sure that the true secret of producing efficient workpeople is to be found in uniting education and labour from a period of childhood. Of course the occupation must not be too severe, nor irksome, or unhealthy. But of the advantage of the union I have no doubt. I wish my own children could have some work as well as play to give variety to their schooling.” (“Ch. Empl. Comm. V. Rep.,” p. 82, n. 36.)

<!-- ch15-p250 -->
More on this point can be found in Senior’s speech at the Social Science Congress at Edinburgh in 1863. There he shows, among other things, how the monotonous and uselessly long school hours of upper-class and middle-class children uselessly add to the teacher’s labor, “while he not only fruitlessly but absolutely injuriously, wastes the time, health, and energy of the children.”[^220] As Robert Owen has shown us in detail, the germ of the education of the future sprouted from the factory system. That education will, for every child over a given age, combine productive labor with instruction and gymnastics. It will do this not only as one way of making production more efficient, but as the only way of producing fully developed human beings.

[^220]: Senior, cited above, p. 66. Modern industry, once it has reached a certain level, can also revolutionize people’s minds, through the revolution it brings about in the mode of production and in the social conditions of production. This is strikingly shown by comparing Senior’s speech in 1863 with his fierce attack on the Factory Act of 1833. It is also shown by comparing the views of the congress mentioned above with the fact that in certain country districts of England poor parents are forbidden, on pain of death by starvation, to educate their children. For example, Mr. Snell reports that it is a common occurrence in Somersetshire that, when a poor person claims parish relief, he is forced to take his children out of school. Mr. Wollarton, the clergyman at Feltham, also tells of cases where all relief was refused to certain families “because they were sending their children to school!”

Modern industry, as we have seen, uses technical means to sweep away the manufacturing division of labor, under which each man is bound hand and foot for life to a single detail-operation. At the same time, the capitalist form of that industry reproduces this same division of labor in a still more monstrous shape. In the factory proper, it does so by turning the worker into a living appendage of the machine. Everywhere outside the factory, it does so partly by the scattered use of machinery and machine workers,[^221] and partly by re-establishing the division of labor on a new basis, through the general introduction of the labor of women and children and of cheap unskilled labor.

[^221]: Wherever handicraft-machines driven by men compete, directly or indirectly, with more developed machines driven by mechanical power, a great change takes place for the worker who drives the machine. At first the steam-engine replaces this worker; afterwards he must replace the steam-engine. As a result, the strain and the amount of labor-power spent become monstrous, especially for the children who are condemned to this torture. Thus Mr. Longe, one of the commissioners, found boys from 10 to 15 years old in and around Coventry employed in driving the ribbon-looms, not to mention younger children who had to drive smaller machines. “It is extraordinarily fatiguing work. The boy is a mere substitute for steam power.” (“Ch. Empl. Comm. V, Rep. 1866;” p. 114, n. 6.) On the fatal consequences of “this system of slavery,” as the official report calls it, see the source cited above, p. 114 and following.

<!-- ch15-p252 -->
The antagonism between the manufacturing division of labor and the methods of modern industry makes itself forcibly felt. Among other ways, it shows itself in a frightful fact. A great part of the children employed in modern factories and manufacturing workshops are riveted from their earliest years to the simplest manipulations, and exploited for years, without being taught a single kind of work that would later make them of use, even in the same manufacturing workshop or factory. In the English letter-press printing trade, for example, there used to be a system, like the one in the old branches of manufacture and handicrafts, of moving the apprentices up from easy work to more and more difficult work. They went through a course of teaching until they were finished printers. Every one of them had to be able to read and write: their trade required it. All this was changed by the printing machine. It employs two kinds of workers. One kind is adults, tenters (machine minders). The other is boys, mostly from 11 to 17 years of age, whose sole business is either to spread the sheets of paper under the machine or to take the printed sheets from it. They perform this weary task, in London especially, for 14, 15, and 16 hours at a stretch, on several days in the week, and often for 36 hours, with only 2 hours’ rest for meals and sleep.[^222] A great part of them cannot read, and they are, as a rule, utter savages and very extraordinary creatures.

[^222]: Cited above, p. 3, n. 24.

<!-- ch15-p253 -->
> “To qualify them for the work which they have to do, they require no intellectual training; there is little room in it for skill, and less for judgment; their wages, though rather high for boys, do not increase proportionately as they grow up, and the majority of them cannot look for advancement to the better paid and more responsible post of machine minder, because while each machine has but one minder, it has at least two, and often four boys attached to it.”[^223]

[^223]: Cited above, P. 7, n. 60.

As soon as they get too old for such child’s work, that is, at about 17 at the latest, they are dismissed from the printing establishments. They become recruits for crime. Several attempts to find them work elsewhere came to nothing because of their ignorance and brutality, and their mental and physical degradation.

As with the division of labor inside the manufacturing workshops, so it is with the division of labor inside society. As long as handicraft and manufacture form the general groundwork of social production, the producer’s subjection to one branch alone, the breaking up of the many-sided variety of his work,[^224] is a necessary step in the development. On that groundwork, each separate branch of production finds through experience the form that suits it technically, slowly perfects that form, and, as soon as it has reached a given degree of maturity, quickly crystallizes it. Apart from new raw materials supplied by trade, the only thing that causes a change here and there is the gradual alteration of the instruments of labor. But their form, too, once experience has fixed it for good, turns to stone. This is proved by the fact that in many cases they were handed down in the same form from one generation to another for thousands of years. It is a telling feature that, even down into the eighteenth century, the different trades were called “mysteries” (mystères).[^225] No one but those duly initiated could get into their secrets. Modern industry tore away the veil that hid from men their own social process of production, and that turned the various, spontaneously divided branches of production into so many riddles, not only to outsiders but even to the initiated. Modern industry followed the principle of breaking each process down into its component movements, without any regard to whether a human hand could carry them out. This principle created the new modern science of technology. The varied, apparently unconnected, and petrified forms of the industrial processes now broke down into so many conscious and systematic applications of natural science to obtaining given useful effects. Technology also discovered the few main basic forms of motion that every productive action of the human body necessarily takes, however varied the instruments used. In just the same way, the science of mechanics sees in the most complicated machinery nothing but the continual repetition of the simple mechanical powers.

[^224]: “In some parts of the Highlands of Scotland, not many years ago, every peasant, according to the Statistical Account, made his own shoes of leather tanned by himself. Many a shepherd and cottar [cottager, farm tenant] too, with his wife and children, appeared at Church in clothes which had been touched by no hands but their own, since they were shorn from the sheep and sown in the flaxfield. In the preparation of these, it is added, scarcely a single article had been purchased, except the awl, needle, thimble, and a very few parts of the iron-work employed in the weaving. The dyes, too, were chiefly extracted by the women from trees, shrubs and herbs.” (Dugald Stewart’s “Works,” Hamilton’s Ed., Vol. viii., pp. 327-328.)

[^225]: In the celebrated “Livre des métiers” [Book of the Trades] of Etienne Boileau, we find the rule that a journeyman, on being admitted among the masters, had to swear “to love his brethren with brotherly love, to support them in their respective trades, not wilfully to betray the secrets of the trade, and besides, in the interests of all, not to recommend his own wares by calling the attention of the buyer to defects in the articles made by others.”

Modern industry never looks on the existing form of a process as final, or treats it as final. So the technical basis of that industry is revolutionary, while all earlier modes of production were essentially conservative.[^226] By means of machinery, chemical processes, and other methods, it is constantly changing not only the technical basis of production but also the functions of the worker and the social combinations of the labor process. In doing so it also revolutionizes, at the same time, the division of labor within society, and it keeps hurling masses of capital and of workers from one branch of production into another. So modern industry, by its very nature, requires variation of labor, fluidity of function, all-round mobility of the worker. But on the other hand, in its capitalist form, it reproduces the old division of labor with its fossilized specializations. We have seen how this absolute contradiction between the technical needs of modern industry and the social character built into its capitalist form does away with all fixity and security in the worker’s situation. We have seen how it constantly threatens, by taking away his instruments of labor, to snatch his means of subsistence out of his hands,[^227] and to make him superfluous by suppressing his detail-function. We have seen, too, how this antagonism vents its rage in creating that monstrosity, an industrial reserve army, kept in misery so as to be always at capital’s disposal. It vents its rage in the endless human sacrifices from among the working class, in the most reckless squandering of labor-power, and in the devastation caused by a social anarchy that turns every economic advance into a social disaster. This is the negative side. On the one hand, variation of work now imposes itself in the manner of an overpowering natural law, and with the blindly destructive action of a natural law that meets with resistance[^228] at every point. But on the other hand, modern industry, through its catastrophes, makes it necessary to recognize variation of work as a fundamental law of production, and with it the worker’s fitness for varied work, and with that the greatest possible development of his varied abilities. It becomes a question of life and death for society to adapt the mode of production to the normal working of this law. Indeed, modern industry compels society, on pain of death, to replace the detail worker of today with the fully developed individual. The detail worker of today is held in the grip of lifelong repetition of one and the same trivial operation, and so reduced to the mere fragment of a man. The fully developed individual is fit for a variety of labors and ready to face any change of production, and for him the different social functions he performs are just so many ways of giving free play to his own natural and acquired powers.

[^226]: “The bourgeoisie cannot exist without continually revolutionising the instruments of production, and thereby the relations of production and all the social relations. Conservation, in an unaltered form, of the old modes of production was on the contrary the first condition of existence for all earlier industrial classes. Constant revolution in production, uninterrupted disturbance of all social conditions, everlasting uncertainty and agitation, distinguish the bourgeois epoch from all earlier ones. All fixed, fast-frozen relations, with their train of ancient and venerable prejudices and opinions, are swept away, all new formed ones become antiquated before they can ossify. All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind.” (F. Engels und Karl Marx: “Manifest der Kommunistischen Partei.” London 1848, p. 5.)

[^227]: “You take my life When you do take the means whereby I live.” Shakespeare.

[^228]: A French worker, on his return from San-Francisco, writes as follows: “I never could have believed, that I was capable of working at the various occupations I was employed on in California. One step already taken spontaneously toward bringing about this revolution is the founding of technical and agricultural schools, and of “écoles d’enseignement professionnel” [schools of vocational education], in which the children of working men receive a little instruction in technology and in the practical handling of the various implements of labor. The Factory Act, that first and meager concession wrung from capital, goes no further than combining elementary education with work in the factory. Even so, there can be no doubt that when the working class comes to power, as it inevitably must, technical instruction, both theoretical and practical, will take its proper place in the working-class schools. There is also no doubt that such revolutionary ferments, whose final result is the abolition of the old division of labor, are diametrically opposed to the capitalist form of production and to the economic position of the worker that corresponds to that form. But the historical development of the antagonisms built into a given form of production is the only way in which that form of production can be dissolved and a new form established. “Ne sutor ultra crepidam” [Let the shoemaker not go beyond his shoe] – this *nec plus ultra* [very height] of handicraft wisdom became sheer nonsense from the moment the watchmaker Watt invented the steam-engine, the barber Arkwright the throstle, and the working jeweler Fulton the steamship.[^229]

[^229]: John Bellers, a true phenomenon in the history of political economy, saw very clearly at the end of the 17th century the need to abolish the present system of education and division of labor, which breed hypertrophy (overgrowth) and atrophy (wasting away) at the two opposite extremes of society. Among other things he says this: “An idle learning being little better than the learning of idleness.... Bodily labour, it’s a primitive institution of God.... Labour being as proper for the bodies’ health as eating is for its living; for what pains a man saves by ease, he will find in disease.... Labour adds oil to the lamp of life, when thinking inflames it.... A childish silly employ” (a warning, this, given by foresight, against the Basedows and their modern imitators) “leaves the children’s minds silly,” (“Proposals for Raising a Colledge of Industry of all Useful Trades and Husbandry.” London, 1696, pp. 12, 14, 18.)

As long as factory legislation is confined to regulating labor in factories, manufacturing workshops, etc., it is regarded as a mere interference with capital’s rights of exploitation. But when it comes to regulating the so-called “home-labour,”[^230] it is at once seen as a direct attack on the patria potestas, on parental authority. The tender-hearted English Parliament long pretended to shrink from taking this step. But the force of facts at last compelled it to admit that modern industry, by overturning the economic foundation of the traditional family and of the family labor that went with it, had also loosened all traditional family ties. The rights of the children had to be proclaimed. The final report of the Children’s Employment Commission of 1866 states:

[^230]: This kind of labor goes on mostly in small workshops, as we have seen in the lace-making and straw-plaiting trades, and as could be shown in more detail from the metal trades of Sheffield, Birmingham, etc.

<!-- ch15-p259 -->
> “It is unhappily, to a painful degree, apparent throughout the whole of the evidence, that against no persons do the children of both sexes so much require protection as against their parents.” The system of unlimited exploitation of children’s labor in general, and of the so-called home-labor in particular, is “maintained only because the parents are able, without check or control, to exercise this arbitrary and mischievous power over their young and tender offspring.... Parents must not possess the absolute power of making their children mere ‘machines to earn so much weekly wage....’ The children and young persons, therefore, in all such cases may justifiably claim from the legislature, as a natural right, that an exemption should be secured to them, from what destroys prematurely their physical strength, and lowers them in the scale of intellectual and moral beings.”[^231]

[^231]: “Ch. Empl. Comm., V. Rep.,” p. xxv., n. 162, and II. Rep., p. xxxviii., n, 285, 289, p. xxv., xxvi., n. 191.

However, it was not the misuse of parental authority that created the capitalist exploitation of children’s labor, whether direct or indirect. On the contrary, it was the capitalist mode of exploitation that swept away the economic basis of parental authority, and so made its exercise degenerate into a mischievous misuse of power. Under the capitalist system, the breaking up of the old family ties may seem terrible and disgusting. Nevertheless, modern industry gives an important part in the process of production, outside the domestic sphere, to women, to young persons, and to children of both sexes. In doing so it creates a new economic foundation for a higher form of the family and of the relations between the sexes. Of course, it is just as absurd to hold the Germanic-Christian form of the family to be absolute and final as it would be to give that character to the ancient Roman, the ancient Greek, or the Eastern forms, which, besides, taken together form a series in historical development. Moreover, it is obvious that the collective working group is made up of individuals of both sexes and all ages, and that this fact must necessarily, under suitable conditions, become a source of humane development. In its spontaneously developed, brutal, capitalist form, though, where the worker exists for the process of production and not the process of production for the worker, that fact is a pestilent source of corruption and slavery.[^232]

[^232]: “Factory labour may be as pure and as excellent as domestic labour, and perhaps more so.” (“Rep. Insp. of Fact., 31st October, 1865,” p. 129.)

It became necessary to generalize the Factory Acts, to transform them from an exceptional law covering mechanical spinning and weaving, those first creations of machinery, into a law affecting social production as a whole. As we have seen, this necessity arose from the way modern industry developed historically. In the wake of that industry, the traditional form of manufacture, of handicraft, and of domestic industry is entirely revolutionized. Manufacture keeps passing into the factory system, and handicrafts into manufacture. And lastly, the spheres of handicraft and of the domestic industries become, in what is, comparatively speaking, a wonderfully short time, dens of misery in which capitalist exploitation is given free rein for its wildest excesses. Two circumstances finally tip the scale. First, there is the constantly recurring experience that capital, as soon as it is subjected to legal control at one point, makes up for it all the more recklessly at other points.[^233] Second, there is the cry of the capitalists for equality in the conditions of competition, that is, for equal restraint on all exploitation of labor.[^234] On this point let us listen to two heart-broken cries. Messrs. Cooksley of Bristol, manufacturers of nails, chains, etc., introduced the regulations of the Factory Act into their business of their own accord.
