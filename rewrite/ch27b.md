The 18th century, however, did not yet recognize as fully as the 19th that national wealth and the poverty of the people are one and the same thing. Hence the very fierce debate in the economic literature of that time over the “enclosure of commons.” From the mass of material that lies before me, I give a few extracts that will throw a strong light on conditions at the time. “In several parishes of Hertfordshire,” writes one indignant person, “24 farms, numbering on the average 50-150 acres, have been melted up into three farms.”[^19] “In Northamptonshire and Leicestershire the enclosure of common lands has taken place on a very large scale, and most of the new lordships [estates], resulting from the enclosure, have been turned into pasturage, in consequence of which many lordships have not now 50 acres ploughed yearly, in which 1,500 were ploughed formerly. The ruins of former dwelling-houses, barns, stables, &c.,” are the only traces of the former inhabitants. “An hundred houses and families have in some open-field villages dwindled to eight or ten.... The landholders in most parishes that have been enclosed only 15 or 20 years, are very few in comparison of the numbers who occupied them in their open-field state. It is no uncommon thing for 4 or 5 wealthy graziers [livestock farmers] to engross [take over] a large enclosed lordship which was before in the hands of 20 or 30 farmers, and as many smaller tenants and proprietors. All these are hereby thrown out of their livings with their families and many other families who were chiefly employed and supported by them.”[^20] It was not only land lying waste, but often land that was cultivated in common, or held for a fixed rent paid to the community, that the neighboring landlords annexed on the pretext of enclosure. “I have here in view enclosures of open fields and lands already improved. It is acknowledged by even the writers in defence of enclosures that these diminished villages increase the monopolies of farms, raise the prices of provisions, and produce depopulation ... and even the enclosure of waste lands (as now carried on) bears hard on the poor, by depriving them of a part of their subsistence, and only goes towards increasing farms already too large.”[^21] “When,” says Dr. Price, “this land gets into the hands of a few great farmers, the consequence must be that the little farmers” (whom he earlier calls “a multitude of little proprietors and tenants, who maintain themselves and families by the produce of the ground they occupy by sheep kept on a common, by poultry, hogs, &c., and who therefore have little occasion to purchase any of the means of subsistence [the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live]”) “will be converted into a body of men who earn their subsistence by working for others, and who will be under a necessity of going to market for all they want.... There will, perhaps, be more labour, because there will be more compulsion to it.... Towns and manufactures will increase, because more will be driven to them in quest of places and employment. This is the way in which the engrossing [buying up] of farms naturally operates. And this is the way in which, for many years, it has been actually operating in this kingdom.”[^22] He sums up the effect of the enclosures like this: “Upon the whole, the circumstances of the lower ranks of men are altered in almost every respect for the worse. From little occupiers of land, they are reduced to the state of day-labourers and hirelings; and, at the same time, their subsistence in that state has become more difficult.”[^23] In fact, the seizure of the common lands, and the revolution in agriculture that went with it, hit the agricultural workers so hard that, even according to Eden, their wages began to fall below the minimum between 1765 and 1780, and began to be topped up by official poor-law relief. Their wages, he says, “were not more than enough for the absolute necessaries of life.”

In the 19th century, the very memory of the connection between the agricultural worker and the communal property had, of course, vanished. Finally, the last process of wholesale expropriation of the agricultural population from the soil is the so-called clearing of estates, that is, sweeping the people off them. All the English methods considered so far culminated in “clearing.” As we saw in the picture of present-day conditions given in an earlier chapter, where there are no more independent peasants to get rid of, the “clearing” of cottages begins. So the agricultural workers no longer find, on the very soil they cultivate, even the space they need for their own housing. But what “clearing of estates” really and properly means, we learn only in the promised land of modern romantic literature, the Highlands of Scotland. There the process stands out for its systematic character, for the size of the scale on which it is carried out at one blow (in Ireland landlords have gone so far as to sweep away several villages at once; in Scotland areas as large as German principalities are dealt with), and finally for the peculiar form of property under which the embezzled lands were held.

<!-- ch27-p015 -->
The Highland Celts were organized in clans, each of which owned the land on which it was settled. The representative of the clan, its chief or “great man,” was only the owner of this property in name, just as the Queen of England is the owner in name of all the nation's soil. When the English government succeeded in suppressing the internal wars of these “great men,” and their constant raids into the Lowland plains, the chiefs of the clans by no means gave up their time-honored trade as robbers; they only changed its form. On their own authority they turned their nominal right into a right of private property, and since this brought them into conflict with their clansmen, they resolved to drive the clansmen out by open force. “A king of England might as well claim to drive his subjects into the sea,” says Professor Newman.[^25] This revolution began in Scotland after the last rising of the followers of the Pretender (the exiled Stuart claimant to the British throne). Its first phases can be followed in the writings of Sir James Steuart[^26] and James Anderson.[^27] In the 18th century the Gaels who had been hunted out were forbidden to emigrate from the country, the aim being to drive them by force to Glasgow and other manufacturing towns.[^28] As an example of the method[^29] used in the 19th century, the “clearing” carried out by the Duchess of Sutherland will be enough here. This person, well schooled in economics, resolved, on taking over her government, to carry out a radical cure and to turn the whole country into a sheep pasture. Its population had already been reduced to 15,000 by earlier processes of the same kind. From 1814 to 1820 these 15,000 inhabitants, about 3,000 families, were systematically hunted and rooted out. All their villages were destroyed and burned, all their fields turned into pasture. British soldiers enforced this eviction and came to blows with the inhabitants. One old woman was burned to death in the flames of the hut she refused to leave. In this way this fine lady took for herself 794,000 acres of land that had belonged to the clan since time immemorial. She assigned the expelled inhabitants about 6,000 acres on the seashore – 2 acres per family. Until then these 6,000 acres had lain waste and brought in no income to their owners. The Duchess, in the nobility of her heart, actually went so far as to rent them, at an average rent of 2s. 6d. per acre, to the clansmen, who for centuries had shed their blood for her family. She divided the whole of the stolen clan land into 29 great sheep farms, each inhabited by a single family, mostly English farm-servants brought in from outside. In the year 1835 the 15,000 Gaels had already been replaced by 131,000 sheep. The remnant of the original inhabitants, flung onto the seashore, tried to live by catching fish. They became amphibious and lived, as an English author says, half on land and half on water, and, with all that, only half on both.[^30]

But finally, part of the sheep pastures are turned into deer preserves. Everyone knows that there are no real forests in England. The deer in the parks of the great are demure domestic cattle, as fat as London aldermen. So Scotland is the last refuge of the “noble passion.” “In the Highlands,” says Somers in 1848, “new forests are springing up like mushrooms. From east to west – from the neighbourhood of Aberdeen to the crags of Oban – you have now a continuous line of forests; while in other parts of the Highlands there are the new forests of Loch Archaig, Glengarry, Glenmoriston, &c. Sheep were introduced into glens which had been the seats of communities of small farmers; and the latter were driven to seek subsistence on coarser and more sterile tracks of soil. Deer-forests[^32] and the people cannot co-exist. One or other of the two must yield. Let the forests be increased in number and extent during the next quarter of a century, as they have been in the last, and the Gaels will perish from their native soil... The plundering of the church’s property, the fraudulent transfer of the State domains, the robbery of the common lands, the seizure of feudal and clan property and its transformation into modern private property under conditions of reckless terrorism: these were just so many idyllic methods of primitive accumulation (the historical process that separated the producers from their means of production, above all by driving peasants off the land, and so created the conditions for capitalism). They conquered the field for capitalist agriculture, made the soil part and parcel of capital, and created for the town industries the necessary supply of a “free” and outlawed proletariat.
