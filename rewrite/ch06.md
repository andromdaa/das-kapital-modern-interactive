## Chapter 6: The Buying and Selling of Labor-Power

<!-- ch06-p001 -->
The change of value (value being what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time) that happens to money meant to be turned into capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger) cannot take place in the money itself. In its function as a means of purchase and as a means of payment (money used to settle a debt after goods have changed hands on credit), money does no more than realize the price (the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity) of the commodity (a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use) that it buys or pays for. And as hard cash, it is value turned to stone, never varying.[^1] Just as little can the change arise in the second act of circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying), the resale of the commodity. That act does no more than change the article from its bodily form back again into its money form (the form of value in which one commodity, gold, has become the universal equivalent). The change must therefore take place in the commodity bought by the first act, M—C, but not in its value, for equivalents are exchanged, and the commodity is paid for at its full value. We are therefore forced to conclude that the change arises from the use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties) of the commodity as such, that is, from its consumption. To be able to extract value from the consumption of a commodity, our friend Moneybags must be lucky enough to find, within the sphere of circulation, in the market, a commodity whose use-value has the peculiar property of being a source of value, so that actually consuming it is itself an embodiment of labor, and therefore a creation of value. The owner of money does find such a special commodity on the market: the capacity for labor, or labor-power.

[^1]: “In the form of money ... capital is productive of no profit.” (Ricardo: “Princ. of Pol. Econ.,” p. 267.)

<!-- ch06-p002 -->
By labor-power, or capacity for labor, we are to understand the sum total of the mental and physical capabilities that exist in a human being, and that he exercises whenever he produces a use-value of any kind.

<!-- ch06-p003 -->
But for our owner of money to be able to find labor-power offered for sale as a commodity, various conditions must first be met. The exchange of commodities in itself implies no relations of dependence other than those that come from its own nature. On this assumption, labor-power can appear on the market as a commodity only if, and so far as, its owner, the individual whose labor-power it is, offers it for sale, or sells it, as a commodity. To be able to do this, he must have it at his disposal. He must be the free, unrestricted owner of his capacity for labor, that is, of his person.[^2] He and the owner of money meet in the market and deal with each other as people with equal rights, with this one difference alone: one is buyer, the other seller. Both, therefore, are equal in the eyes of the law. For this relation to continue, the owner of the labor-power must sell it only for a definite period. For if he were to sell it outright, all of it, once and for all, he would be selling himself. He would be turning himself from a free man into a slave, from an owner of a commodity into a commodity. He must always treat his labor-power as his own property, his own commodity. He can do this only by placing it at the buyer’s disposal temporarily, for a definite period of time. Only in this way can he avoid giving up his rights of ownership over it.[^3]

[^2]: In encyclopedias of classical antiquity we find such nonsense as this: that in the ancient world capital was fully developed, “except that the free labourer and a system of credit was wanting.” Mommsen too, in his “History of Rome,” makes one blunder after another on this point.

[^3]: Hence legislation in various countries fixes a maximum for labor contracts. Wherever free labor is the rule, the laws regulate how this contract is ended. In some States, particularly in Mexico (before the American Civil War, also in the territories taken from Mexico, and also, as a matter of fact, in the Danubian provinces until the revolution carried out by Kusa), slavery is hidden under the form of *peonage*. Through advances that are to be repaid in labor and are handed down from generation to generation, not only the individual worker but his family become, in fact, the property of other persons and their families. Juarez abolished *peonage*. The so-called Emperor Maximilian brought it back by a decree, which was fittingly denounced in the House of Representatives at Washington as a decree for re-introducing slavery into Mexico. “I may make over to another the use, for a limited time, of my particular bodily and mental aptitudes and capabilities; because in consequence of this restriction, they are impressed with a character of alienation with regard to me as a whole. But by the alienation of all my labour-time and the whole of my work, I should be converting the substance itself, in other words, my general activity and reality, my person, into the property of another.” (Hegel, “Philosophie des Rechts.” Berlin, 1840, p. 104, § 67.)

<!-- ch06-p004 -->
The second essential condition for the owner of money to find labor-power on the market as a commodity is this: the worker, instead of being in a position to sell commodities in which his labor is embodied, must be obliged to offer for sale, as a commodity, that very labor-power which exists only in his living self.

<!-- ch06-p005 -->
For a man to be able to sell commodities other than labor-power, he must of course have the means of production, such as raw material, tools, etc. No boots can be made without leather. He also needs the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live). Nobody, not even “a musician of the future”, can live on future products, or on use-values in an unfinished state. Ever since man first appeared on the world’s stage, he always has been, and still must be, a consumer, both before and while he is producing. In a society where all products take the form of commodities, these commodities must be sold after they have been produced; only after their sale can they serve to satisfy the needs of their producer. The time needed for their sale is added on top of the time needed for their production.

<!-- ch06-p006 -->
So, to turn his money into capital, the owner of money must meet in the market with the free worker, free in a double sense: free in that, as a free man, he can dispose of his labor-power as his own commodity, and free in that, on the other hand, he has no other commodity for sale and lacks everything needed to put his labor-power into action.

<!-- ch06-p007 -->
The owner of money has no interest in the question of why this free worker confronts him in the market, since he regards the labor market as a branch of the general market for commodities. And for the present, the question interests us just as little. We hold to the fact in theory, as he does in practice. One thing, however, is clear. Nature does not produce owners of money or commodities on one side, and on the other side men who possess nothing but their own labor-power. This relation has no natural basis, nor is its social basis one that is common to all historical periods. It is clearly the result of a past historical development, the product of many economic revolutions, of the extinction of a whole series of older forms of social production.

<!-- ch06-p008 -->
In the same way, the economic categories we have already discussed bear the stamp of history. Definite historical conditions are needed for a product to become a commodity. It must not be produced as the immediate means of subsistence of the producer himself. If we had gone further and asked under what circumstances all products, or even most of them, take the form of commodities, we would have found that this can happen only with production of a very specific kind: capitalist production. But such an inquiry would have been out of place in the analysis of commodities. The production and circulation of commodities can take place even though the great mass of the objects produced are meant for the immediate needs of their producers and are not turned into commodities, so that social production is still far from being dominated, in its length and breadth, by exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for). For products to appear as commodities, the social division of labor must have developed so far that the separation of use-value from exchange-value, a separation that first begins with barter, has already been completed. But such a degree of development is common to many forms of society, which in other respects show the most varied historical features. On the other hand, if we consider money, its existence implies a definite stage in the exchange of commodities. Money performs particular functions: as the mere equivalent of commodities, or as medium of circulation (money in its role of passing from hand to hand to carry out sales and purchases), or means of payment, as hoard (money withdrawn from circulation and kept as a store of value) or as world money (gold and silver used as money between nations). Depending on the extent of one function or the other, and on which of them relatively predominates, these functions point to very different stages in the process of social production. Yet we know from experience that a relatively primitive circulation of commodities is enough to produce all these forms. It is otherwise with capital. The historical conditions of its existence are by no means given with the mere circulation of money and commodities. Capital can spring into life only when the owner of the means of production and subsistence meets in the market with the free worker selling his labor-power. And this one historical condition contains a world’s history. So capital, from its first appearance, announces a new epoch in the process of social production.[^4]

[^4]: The capitalist epoch is therefore marked by this: labor-power, in the eyes of the worker himself, takes the form of a commodity that is his property, and so his labor becomes wage labor. On the other hand, it is only from this moment that the product of labor universally becomes a commodity.

<!-- ch06-p009 -->
We must now look more closely at this peculiar commodity, labor-power. Like all others, it has a value.[^5] How is that value determined?

[^5]: “The value or worth of a man, is as of all other things his price — that is to say, so much as would be given for the use of his power.” (Th. Hobbes: “Leviathan” in Works, Ed. Molesworth. London 1839-44, v. iii. p. 76.)

<!-- ch06-p010 -->
The value of labor-power is determined, as with every other commodity, by the labor-time needed to produce, and so also to reproduce, this special article. So far as it has value, it represents no more than a definite quantity of the average labor of society (simple average labor: the labor that an average person without special training can do; skilled labor counts as a multiple of it) embodied in it. Labor-power exists only as a capacity, or power, of the living individual. Its production therefore presupposes his existence. Given the individual, the production of labor-power consists in his reproducing himself, or maintaining himself. To maintain himself he needs a given quantity of the means of subsistence. So the labor-time needed to produce labor-power comes down to the labor-time needed to produce those means of subsistence. In other words, the value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to maintain the worker. Labor-power, however, becomes a reality only by being exercised; it sets itself in action only by working. But in this way a definite quantity of human muscle, nerve, brain, etc., is used up, and these must be restored. This increased expenditure demands a larger income.[^6] If the owner of labor-power works today, tomorrow he must again be able to repeat the same process in the same conditions of health and strength. So his means of subsistence must be enough to maintain him in his normal state as a working individual. His natural wants, such as food, clothing, fuel, and housing, vary with the climate and other physical conditions of his country. On the other hand, the number and extent of his so-called necessary wants, and also the ways of satisfying them, are themselves the product of historical development. They therefore depend to a great extent on a country’s degree of civilization, and more particularly on the conditions under which the class of free workers has been formed, and so on the habits and the degree of comfort in which it has been formed.[^7] So, unlike the case of other commodities, a historical and moral element enters into determining the value of labor-power. Nevertheless, in a given country, at a given period, the average quantity of the means of subsistence needed by the worker is known in practice.

[^6]: Hence the Roman Villicus, as overseer of the agricultural slaves, received “more meagre fare than working slaves, because his work was lighter.” (Th. Mommsen, Röm. Geschichte, 1856, p. 810.)

[^7]: Compare W. Th. Thornton: “Over-population and its Remedy,” London, 1846.

The owner of labor-power is mortal. If, then, he is to appear on the market continuously, as the continuous turning of money into capital assumes, the seller of labor-power must perpetuate himself, “in the way that every living individual perpetuates himself, by procreation.”[^8] The labor-power withdrawn from the market by wear and tear and by death must be continually replaced by at least an equal amount of fresh labor-power. So the sum of the means of subsistence needed to produce labor-power must include the means needed for the worker’s replacements, that is, his children, so that this race of peculiar commodity-owners may go on appearing in the market.[^9]

[^8]: Petty.

[^9]: “Its (labour’s) natural price ... consists in such a quantity of necessaries and comforts of life, as, from the nature of the climate, and the habits of the country, are necessary to support the labourer, and to enable him to rear such a family as may preserve, in the market, an undiminished supply of labour.” (R. Torrens: “An Essay on the External Corn Trade.” London 1815, p. 62.) Here the word “labour” is wrongly used for labor-power.

<!-- ch06-p012 -->
To modify the human organism so that it gains skill and handiness in a given branch of industry and becomes labor-power of a special kind, a special education or training is needed, and this in turn costs an equivalent in commodities of a greater or smaller amount. The amount varies with how complicated the labor-power is. The expenses of this education (extremely small in the case of ordinary labor-power) enter to that extent into the total value spent in producing it.

<!-- ch06-p013 -->
The value of labor-power comes down to the value of a definite quantity of the means of subsistence. It therefore varies with the value of these means, or with the quantity of labor needed to produce them.

<!-- ch06-p014 -->
Some of the means of subsistence, such as food and fuel, are consumed daily, and a fresh supply must be provided daily. Others, such as clothes and furniture, last for longer periods and need to be replaced only at longer intervals. One article must be bought or paid for daily, another weekly, another quarterly, and so on. But however the total of these outlays may be spread over the year, they must be covered by the average income, taking one day with another. If the total of the commodities required daily for the production of labor-power = A, those required weekly = B, those required quarterly = C, and so on, then the daily average of these commodities = (365A + 52B + 4C + &c) / 365. Suppose that 6 hours of social labor are embodied in this mass of commodities needed for the average day. Then half a day’s average social labor is embodied daily in labor-power; in other words, half a day’s labor is needed for the daily production of labor-power. This quantity of labor forms the value of a day’s labor-power, or the value of the labor-power reproduced daily. If half a day’s average social labor is embodied in three shillings, then three shillings is the price corresponding to the value of a day’s labor-power. So if its owner offers it for sale at three shillings a day, its selling price is equal to its value, and, according to our supposition, our friend Moneybags, who is set on turning his three shillings into capital, pays this value.

<!-- ch06-p015 -->
The minimum limit of the value of labor-power is determined by the value of the commodities without whose daily supply the worker cannot renew his vital energy, and so by the value of those means of subsistence that are physically indispensable. If the price of labor-power falls to this minimum, it falls below its value, since under such circumstances labor-power can be maintained and developed only in a crippled state. But the value of every commodity is determined by the labor-time needed to turn it out at normal quality.

<!-- ch06-p016 -->
It is a very cheap sort of sentimentality that declares this method of determining the value of labor-power, a method required by the very nature of the case, to be a brutal method, and that wails along with Rossi: “To comprehend capacity for labour (puissance de travail) at the same time that we make abstraction from the means of subsistence of the labourers during the process of production, is to comprehend a phantom (être de raison). When we speak of labour, or capacity for labour, we speak at the same time of the labourer and his means of subsistence, of labourer and wages.”[^10] When we speak of capacity for labor, we do not speak of labor, any more than we speak of digestion when we speak of capacity for digestion. Digestion requires something more than a good stomach. When we speak of capacity for labor, we do not leave the necessary means of subsistence out of account. On the contrary, their value is expressed in its value. If his capacity for labor remains unsold, the worker gets no benefit from it. Rather, he will feel it to be a cruel necessity imposed by nature that this capacity has cost a definite amount of the means of subsistence to produce, and that it will go on doing so to be reproduced. He will then agree with Sismondi: “that capacity for labour ... is nothing unless it is sold.”[^11]

[^10]: Rossi: “Cours d’Econ. Polit.,” Bruxelles, 1842, p. 370.

[^11]: Sismondi: “Nouv. Princ. etc.,” t. I, p. 112.

One consequence of the peculiar nature of labor-power as a commodity is that its use-value does not pass straight into the buyer’s hands when the contract between buyer and seller is concluded. Its value, like that of every other commodity, is already fixed before it goes into circulation, since a definite quantity of social labor has been spent on it. But its use-value consists in the later exercise of its force. The handing over (alienation) of labor-power and its actual appropriation by the buyer, its use as a use-value, are separated by an interval of time. But in cases where the formal handing over of a commodity’s use-value by sale does not happen at the same time as its actual delivery to the buyer, the buyer’s money usually functions as means of payment.[^12] In every country where the capitalist mode of production reigns, it is the custom not to pay for labor-power until it has been exercised for the period fixed by the contract, for example at the end of each week. In all cases, therefore, the worker advances the use-value of the labor-power to the capitalist: he lets the buyer consume it before he receives payment of its price. Everywhere, he gives credit to the capitalist. That this credit is no mere fiction is shown not only by the occasional loss of wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself) when the capitalist goes bankrupt,[^13] but also by a series of more lasting consequences.[^14] Nevertheless, whether money serves as a means of purchase or as a means of payment makes no difference to the nature of the exchange of commodities. The price of the labor-power is fixed by the contract, although it is not realized until later, like the rent of a house. The labor-power is sold, although it is only paid for later. So, for a clear understanding of the relation between the parties, it will be useful to assume for the time being that the owner of labor-power, each time he sells it, immediately receives the price agreed for it.

We now know how the value that the buyer pays to the owner of this peculiar commodity, labor-power, is determined. The use-value that the buyer gets in exchange shows itself only in the actual use, in the consumption, of the labor-power. The money-owner buys everything needed for this purpose, such as raw material, in the market, and pays its full value for it. The consumption of labor-power is at one and the same time the production of commodities and of surplus-value (the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it). The consumption of labor-power, as in the case of every other commodity, is completed outside the limits of the market, or of the sphere of circulation. So, accompanied by Mr. Moneybags and by the owner of labor-power, we take leave for a time of this noisy sphere, where everything takes place on the surface and in full view of everyone. We follow them both into the hidden abode of production, on whose threshold we are met by the words “No admittance except on business.” Here we shall see not only how capital produces, but how capital is produced. We shall at last force the secret of profit-making.

<!-- ch06-p019 -->
This sphere that we are leaving, within whose boundaries the sale and purchase of labor-power goes on, is in fact a very Eden of the innate rights of man. There alone rule Freedom, Equality, Property and Bentham. Freedom, because both buyer and seller of a commodity, say of labor-power, are bound only by their own free will. They make their contract as free agents, and the agreement they come to is only the form in which they give legal expression to their common will. Equality, because each enters into relation with the other as with a simple owner of commodities, and they exchange equivalent for equivalent. Property, because each disposes only of what is his own. And Bentham, because each looks only to himself. The only force that brings them together and puts them in relation with each other is the selfishness, the gain and the private interests of each. Each looks to himself only, and no one troubles himself about the rest. And just because they do so, they all, in accordance with the pre-established harmony of things, or under the guidance of an all-shrewd providence, work together to their mutual advantage, for the common good and in the interest of all.

<!-- ch06-p020 -->
On leaving this sphere of simple circulation, or of the exchange of commodities, which supplies the “Free-trader Vulgaris” with his views and ideas, and with the standard by which he judges a society based on capital and wages, we think we can see a change in the faces of our cast of characters. He who was the money-owner before now strides in front as capitalist; the owner of labor-power follows as his worker. The one has an air of importance, smirking, intent on business; the other is timid and holding back, like someone who is bringing his own hide to market and has nothing to expect but – a hiding.
