### Section 4: Different Forms of the Relative surplus population. The General Law of Capitalistic Accumulation

<!-- ch25-p054 -->
The relative surplus population exists in every possible form. Every worker belongs to it during the time when he is only partly employed or wholly unemployed. Leaving aside the great forms that recur periodically, which the changing phases of the industrial cycle stamp on it – now an acute form during the crisis, then again a chronic form during dull times – it always has three forms: the floating, the latent, and the stagnant.

<!-- ch25-p055 -->
In the centers of modern industry – factories, manufacturing workshops, ironworks, mines, etc. – the workers are sometimes pushed out, and sometimes drawn in again in greater masses. On the whole the number of those employed increases, although in a constantly decreasing proportion to the scale of production. Here the surplus population exists in the floating form.

<!-- ch25-p056 -->
In the automatic factories, as in all the great workshops, where machinery enters as a factor, or where only the modern division of labor is carried out, large numbers of boys are employed up to the age of maturity. Once they reach that age, only a very small number continue to find work in the same branches of industry, while the majority are regularly dismissed. This majority forms an element of the floating surplus population, and it grows as those branches of industry expand. Part of them emigrates, and in fact follows capital that has emigrated. One consequence is that the female population grows faster than the male, as England bears witness. The natural increase in the number of workers does not satisfy the needs of the accumulation of capital, and yet all the time it is in excess of them. That is a contradiction built into the movement of capital itself. Capital wants larger numbers of young workers and a smaller number of adults. This contradiction is no more glaring than that other one: there are complaints of a shortage of workers, while at the same time many thousands are out of work, because the division of labor chains them to a particular branch of industry.[^21]

[^21]: During the last six months of 1866, 80-90,000 working people in London were thrown out of work. Yet the Factory Report for that same half year says: “It does not appear absolutely true to say that demand will always produce supply just at the moment when it is needed. It has not done so with labour, for much machinery has been idle last year for want of hands.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1866,” p. 81.)

<!-- ch25-p057 -->
Besides, capital consumes labor-power so fast that the worker, by the middle of his life, has already more or less completely used himself up. He falls into the ranks of the supernumeraries, the extra workers no longer needed, or he is pushed down from a higher step on the ladder to a lower one. It is precisely among the workers of modern industry that we meet the shortest length of life. Dr. Lee, Medical Officer of Health for Manchester, stated

<!-- ch25-p058 -->
> “that the average age at death of the Manchester ... upper middle class was 38 years, while the average age at death of the labouring class was 17; while at Liverpool those figures were represented as 35 against 15. It thus appeared that the well-to-do classes had a lease of life which was more than double the value of that which fell to the lot of the less favoured citizens.”[^22]

[^22]: Opening address to the Sanitary Conference, Birmingham, January 15th, 1875, by J. Chamberlain, Mayor of the town, now (1883) President of the Board of Trade.

<!-- ch25-p059 -->
To fit these circumstances, the absolute increase of this section of the proletariat must take place under conditions that swell their numbers, even though the individual members are used up quickly. Hence the generations of workers must be renewed rapidly (this law does not hold for the other classes of the population). This social need is met by early marriages, which are a necessary consequence of the conditions in which the workers of modern industry live. It is also met by the premium that the exploitation of children places on their production.

<!-- ch25-p060 -->
As soon as capitalist production takes possession of agriculture, and in proportion to how far it does so, the demand for an agricultural working population falls absolutely, while the accumulation of the capital employed in agriculture advances. And unlike in non-agricultural industries, this pushing-out of workers is not made up for by a greater drawing-in. Part of the agricultural population is therefore constantly on the point of passing over into an urban or manufacturing proletariat, and is on the lookout for circumstances favorable to this change. (Manufacture is used here in the sense of all non-agricultural industries.)[^23] So this source of relative surplus population is constantly flowing. But the constant flow toward the towns presupposes that in the countryside itself there is a constant latent surplus population. How large it is becomes visible only when its outlet channels open exceptionally wide. The agricultural worker is therefore pushed down to the minimum of wages, and always stands with one foot already in the swamp of pauperism (the condition of the lowest layer of the surplus population, unable to find work and dependent on poor relief).

[^23]: The census for 1861 gives 781 towns for England and Wales, which “contained 10,960,998 inhabitants, while the villages and country parishes contained 9,105,226. In 1851, 580 towns were distinguished, and the population in them and in the surrounding country was nearly equal. But while in the subsequent ten years the population in the villages and the country increased half a million, the population in the 580 towns increased by a million and a half (1,554,067). The increase of the population of the country parishes is 6.5 per cent., and of the towns 17.3 per cent. The difference in the rates of increase is due to the migration from country to town. Three-fourths of the total increase of population has taken place in the towns.” (“Census. &c.,” pp. 11 and 12.)

<!-- ch25-p061 -->
The third category of the relative surplus population, the stagnant, forms part of the active labor army, but its employment is extremely irregular. So it provides capital with an endless reservoir of labor-power that is available for use. Its living conditions sink below the average normal level of the working class, and this at once makes it the broad basis of special branches of capitalist exploitation. Its marks are the maximum of working time and the minimum of wages. We have already come to know its chief form under the heading of “domestic industry.” It constantly recruits itself from the surplus forces of modern industry and agriculture, and especially from those decaying branches of industry where handicraft is giving way to manufacture, and manufacture to machinery. Its extent grows as the creation of a surplus population advances along with the extent and energy of accumulation. But at the same time it forms an element of the working class that reproduces and perpetuates itself, and it takes a proportionally greater part in the general increase of that class than the other elements do. In fact, not only the number of births and deaths, but the absolute size of families, stand in inverse proportion to the level of wages, and therefore to the amount of means of subsistence that the different categories of workers have at their disposal. This law of capitalist society would sound absurd to savages, or even to civilized colonists. It calls to mind the boundless reproduction of animals that are individually weak and constantly hunted down.[^24]

[^24]: “Poverty seems favourable to generation.” (A. Smith.) According to the gallant and witty Abbé Galiani, this is even an especially wise arrangement of God: “Iddio af che gli uomini che esercitano mestieri di prima utilità nascono abbondantemente.” (Galiani, cited above, p. 78.) [God brings it about that men who carry on trades of the first usefulness are born in abundance] “Misery up to the extreme point of famine and pestilence, instead of checking, tends to increase population.” (S. Laing, “National Distress,” 1844, p. 69.) After Laing has illustrated this with statistics, he goes on: “If the people were all in easy circumstances, the world would soon be depopulated.”

<!-- ch25-p062 -->
Finally, the lowest sediment of the relative surplus population lives in the sphere of pauperism. Leaving out vagabonds, criminals, and prostitutes – in a word, the “dangerous” classes – this layer of society consists of three categories. First, those able to work. One need only glance quickly at the statistics of English pauperism to see that the number of paupers rises with every crisis and falls with every revival of trade. Second, orphans and pauper children. These are candidates for the industrial reserve army, and in times of great prosperity, for example 1860, they are quickly enrolled in large numbers in the active army of workers. Third, the demoralized and ragged, and those unable to work. These are chiefly people who go under because the division of labor has left them unable to adapt; people who have passed the normal age of a worker; and the victims of industry, whose number grows with the growth of dangerous machinery, of mines, chemical works, etc. – the mutilated, the sickly, the widows, etc. Pauperism is the hospital of the active labor army and the dead weight of the industrial reserve army. Its production is included in that of the relative surplus population, and its necessity in theirs. Along with the surplus population, pauperism forms a condition of capitalist production, and of the capitalist development of wealth. It is part of the overhead costs of capitalist production; but capital knows how to shift most of these from its own shoulders onto those of the working class and the lower middle class.

<!-- ch25-p063 -->
The greater the social wealth, the capital at work, and the extent and energy of its growth – and therefore also the absolute mass of the proletariat and the productivity of its labor – the greater is the industrial reserve army. The same causes that develop the expansive power of capital also develop the labor-power at its disposal. So the relative mass of the industrial reserve army grows with the potential energy of wealth. But the greater this reserve army is in proportion to the active labor army, the greater is the mass of a fixed, settled surplus population, whose misery is in inverse ratio to the torment of its labor. Finally, the larger the Lazarus layers of the working class (named after the beggar Lazarus in the Bible) and the industrial reserve army, the greater is official pauperism. *This is the absolute general law of capitalist accumulation.* Like all other laws, it is modified in its working by many circumstances, but analyzing them does not concern us here.

<!-- ch25-p064 -->
We can now see plainly how foolish is the economic wisdom that preaches to the workers that they should adjust their numbers to the needs of capital. The mechanism of capitalist production and accumulation constantly carries out this adjustment itself. The first word of this adaptation is the creation of a relative surplus population, or industrial reserve army. Its last word is the misery of ever-widening layers of the active army of labor, and the dead weight of pauperism.

<!-- ch25-p065 -->
There is a law by which, thanks to the advance in the productivity of social labor, an ever-growing quantity of means of production may be set in motion by an ever-shrinking expenditure of human power. In a capitalist society – where the worker does not employ the means of production, but the means of production employ the worker – this law is completely turned upside down, and is expressed thus: the higher the productivity of labor, the greater is the pressure of the workers on the means of employment, and so the more precarious becomes their condition of existence, namely the sale of their own labor-power to increase another's wealth, or for the self-expansion of capital. The fact that the means of production, and the productivity of labor, increase faster than the productive population therefore expresses itself, in capitalist terms, in the inverted form that the working population always increases faster than the conditions under which capital can employ this increase for its own self-expansion.

<!-- ch25-p066 -->
We saw in Part IV., when we analyzed the production of relative surplus-value (surplus-value obtained by shortening necessary labor-time, by making workers' necessities cheaper through higher productivity, so that more of a working day of given length is surplus labor), that within the capitalist system all methods for raising the social productivity of labor are carried out at the cost of the individual worker. All means for developing production turn into means of domination over the producers and of exploitation of them. They mutilate the worker into a fragment of a man and degrade him to the level of an appendage of a machine. They destroy every remnant of charm in his work and turn it into hated toil. They estrange him from the intellectual powers of the labor process in the same proportion as science is built into it as an independent power. They distort the conditions under which he works, and during the labor process they subject him to a despotism all the more hateful for its pettiness. They turn his lifetime into working time, and drag his wife and child beneath the wheels of the Juggernaut of capital. But all methods for producing surplus-value are at the same time methods of accumulation, and every extension of accumulation becomes in turn a means for developing those methods. It follows therefore that as capital accumulates, the lot of the worker, be his pay high or low, must grow worse. Finally, the law that always keeps the relative surplus population, or industrial reserve army, in balance with the extent and energy of accumulation – this law chains the worker to capital more firmly than the wedges of Vulcan chained Prometheus to the rock. It establishes an accumulation of misery that corresponds to the accumulation of capital. Accumulation of wealth at one pole is therefore at the same time accumulation of misery, agony of toil slavery, ignorance, brutality, and mental degradation at the opposite pole, that is, on the side of the class that produces its own product in the form of capital.[^25] Political economists state this antagonistic character of capitalist accumulation in various forms, although they confuse it with phenomena that are certainly to some extent similar, but are nevertheless essentially different and belong to pre-capitalist modes of production.

[^25]: “De jour en jour il devient donc plus clair que les rapports de production dans lesquels se meut la bourgeoisie n’ont pas un caractère un, un caractère simple, mais un caractère de duplicité; que dans les mêmes rapports dans lesquels se produit la richesse, la misère se produit aussi; que dans les mêmes rapports dans lesquels il y a développement des forces productives, il y a une force productive de répression; que ces rapports ne produisent la richesse bourgeoise, c’est-à-dire la richesse de la classe bourgeoise, qu’en anéantissant continuellement la richesse des membres intégrants de cette classe et en produisant un prolétariat toujours croissant.” [So from day to day it becomes clearer that the relations of production in which the bourgeoisie moves do not have a single, simple character, but a double character; that in the very same relations in which wealth is produced, poverty is produced too; that in the very same relations in which productive forces develop, there is also a productive force of repression; that these relations produce bourgeois wealth, that is, the wealth of the bourgeois class, only by continually wiping out the wealth of the individual members of this class and by producing an ever-growing proletariat] (Karl Marx: “Misère de la Philosophie,” p. 116.)

<!-- ch25-p067 -->
The Venetian monk Ortes, one of the great economic writers of the 18th century, treats the antagonism of capitalist production as a general natural law of social wealth.

<!-- ch25-p068 -->
> “In the economy of a nation, advantages and evils always balance one another (il bene ed il male economico in una nazione sempre all, istessa misura [economic good and evil in a nation always at the same measure]): the abundance of wealth with some people, is always equal to the want of it with others (la copia dei beni in alcuni sempre eguale alia mancanza di essi in altri [the abundance of goods in some always equal to the lack of them in others]): the great riches of a small number are always accompanied by the absolute privation of the first necessaries of life for many others. The wealth of a nation corresponds with its population, and its misery corresponds with its wealth. Diligence in some compels idleness in others. The poor and idle are a necessary consequence of the rich and active,” etc.[^26]

[^26]: G. Ortes: “Delia Economia Nazionale libri sei, 1777,” in Custodi, Parte Moderna, t. xxi, pp. 6, 9, 22, 25, etc. Ortes says, cited above, p. 32: “In luoco di progettar sistemi inutili per la felicità de’popoli, mi limiterò a investigare la regione della loro infelicità.” [Instead of drawing up useless schemes for making peoples happy, I shall limit myself to looking into the reasons for their unhappiness]

<!-- ch25-p069 -->
About 10 years after Ortes, the Church of England parson Townsend glorified misery, in a thoroughly brutal way, as a necessary condition of wealth.

<!-- ch25-p070 -->
> “Legal constraint (to labour) is attended with too much trouble, violence, and noise, whereas hunger is not only a peaceable, silent, unremitted pressure, but as the most natural motive to industry and labour, it calls forth the most powerful exertions.”

<!-- ch25-p071 -->
So everything depends on making hunger permanent among the working class, and according to Townsend, the principle of population, which is especially active among the poor, takes care of that.

<!-- ch25-p072 -->
> “It seems to be a law of Nature that the poor should be to a certain degree improvident” [that is, so improvident as to be born *without* a silver spoon in the mouth], “that there may always be some to fulfil the most servile, the most sordid, and the most ignoble offices in the community. The stock of human happiness is thereby much increased, whilst the more delicate are not only relieved from drudgery ... but are left at liberty without interruption to pursue those callings which are suited to their various dispositions ... it” [the Poor Law] “tends to destroy the harmony and beauty, the symmetry and order of that system which God and Nature have established in the world.”[^27] Where the Venetian monk found in the fate that makes misery eternal the very reason for the existence of Christian charity, celibacy, monasteries, and holy houses, the Protestant prebendary (a clergyman paid from a cathedral's income) finds in it an excuse for condemning the laws under which the poor had a right to a miserable public relief.

[^27]: “A Dissertation on the Poor Laws. By a Well-wisher of Mankind. (The Rev. J. Townsend) 1786,” republished London 1817, pp. 15, 39, 41. This “delicate” parson, from whose work just quoted, as well as from his “Journey through Spain,” Malthus often copies whole pages, himself borrowed the greater part of his doctrine from Sir James Steuart, though he alters Steuart in the borrowing. For example, Steuart says: “Here, in slavery, was a forcible method of making mankind diligent,” [for the non-workers] ... “Men were then forced to work” [that is, to work free of charge for others], “because they were slaves of others; men are now forced to work” [that is, to work free of charge for non-workers] “because they are the slaves of their necessities.” But he does not conclude from this, as the fat holder of paid church posts does, that the wage worker must always go hungry. On the contrary, he wants to increase their wants and to make the growing number of their wants a spur to their labor for the “more delicate.”

<!-- ch25-p073 -->
> “The progress of social wealth,” says Storch, “begets this useful class of society ... which performs the most wearisome, the vilest, the most disgusting functions, which takes, in a word, on its shoulders all that is disagreeable and servile in life, and procures thus for other classes leisure, serenity of mind and conventional” [c’est bon! (that's a good one!)] “dignity of character.”[^28]

[^28]: Storch, cited above, t. iii, p. 223.

<!-- ch25-p074 -->
Storch asks himself what, then, the progress of this capitalist civilization, with its misery and its degradation of the masses, really consists of, compared with barbarism. He finds only one answer: security!

<!-- ch25-p075 -->
> “Thanks to the advance of industry and science,” says Sismondi, “every labourer can produce every day much more than his consumption requires. But at the same time, whilst his labour produces wealth, that wealth would, were he called on to consume it himself, make him less fit for labour.” According to him, “men” [that is, non-workers] “would probably prefer to do without all artistic perfection, and all the enjoyments that manufacturers procure for us, if it were necessary that all should buy them by constant toil like that of the labourer.... Exertion today is separated from its recompense; it is not the same man that first works, and then reposes; but it is because the one works that the other rests.... The indefinite multiplication of the productive powers of labour can then only have for result the increase of luxury and enjoyment of the idle rich.”[^29]

[^29]: Sismondi, cited above, pp. 79, 80, 85.
