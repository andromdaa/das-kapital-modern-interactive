## Chapter 17: Changes of Magnitude in the Price of Labor-Power and in Surplus-Value

<!-- ch17-p001 -->
The value of labor-power is determined by the value of the necessities of life that the average worker habitually requires. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) The quantity of these necessities is known at any given period in a given society, and so it can be treated as a constant magnitude. What changes is the value of this quantity. Besides this, two other factors help determine the value of labor-power. One is the cost of developing that power, and this cost varies with the mode of production. The other is its natural diversity: the difference between the labor-power of men and of women, of children and of adults. The use of these different sorts of labor-power, a use that is in its turn made necessary by the mode of production, makes a great difference in the cost of maintaining the worker's family, and in the value of the labor-power of the adult male. Both these factors, however, are left out of the following investigation.[^1]

[^1]: *Note in the 3rd German edition*. — The case considered at pages 321-324 is of course left out here. — *F. E.*

<!-- ch17-p002 -->
I assume (1) that commodities are sold at their value; (2) that the price of labor-power rises above its value now and then, but never sinks below it. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use. Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.)

<!-- ch17-p003 -->
On these assumptions, we have seen that the relative magnitudes of surplus-value and of the price of labor-power are determined by three circumstances. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) These are: (1) the length of the working day, or the extensive magnitude of labor (the working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor); (2) the normal intensity of labor, its intensive magnitude, by which a given quantity of labor is spent in a given time; (3) the productivity of labor, by which the same amount of labor yields, in a given time, a greater or smaller amount of product, depending on how far the conditions of production have developed. Clearly, very different combinations are possible. One of the three factors may be constant and two variable, or two constant and one variable, or, lastly, all three may vary at the same time. And the number of these combinations grows further because, when these factors vary at the same time, the amount and direction of their variations may differ. In what follows, only the chief combinations are considered.

### Section 1: Length of the Working day and Intensity of Labor Constant. Productiveness of Labor Variable

<!-- ch17-p004 -->
On these assumptions, the value of labor-power and the magnitude of surplus-value are determined by three laws.

<!-- ch17-p005 -->
*(1.)* A working day of given length always creates the same amount of value, no matter how the productivity of labor may vary, and with it the mass of the product and the price of each single commodity produced.

<!-- ch17-p006 -->
Say the value created by a working day of 12 hours is six shillings. The mass of articles produced varies with the productivity of labor. But the only result is that the value represented by six shillings is spread over a greater or smaller number of articles.

<!-- ch17-p007 -->
*(2.)* Surplus-value and the value of labor-power vary in opposite directions. A change in the productivity of labor, whether an increase or a decrease, causes a change in the opposite direction in the value of labor-power, and in the same direction in surplus-value.

<!-- ch17-p008 -->
The value created by a working day of 12 hours is a constant quantity, say six shillings. This constant quantity is the sum of the surplus-value plus the value of labor-power, which is the value the worker replaces with an equivalent. It is self-evident that if a constant quantity consists of two parts, neither of them can increase without the other decreasing. Let the two parts be equal at the start: 3 shillings value of labor-power, 3 shillings surplus-value. Then the value of labor-power cannot rise from three shillings to four without the surplus-value falling from three shillings to two. And the surplus-value cannot rise from three shillings to four without the value of labor-power falling from three shillings to two. So under these circumstances no change can take place in the absolute magnitude of either the surplus-value or the value of labor-power without a change, at the same time, in their relative magnitudes, that is, in their size relative to each other. It is impossible for them both to rise or both to fall at the same time.

<!-- ch17-p009 -->
Further, the value of labor-power cannot fall, and so surplus-value cannot rise, without a rise in the productivity of labor. For instance, in the case above, the value of labor-power cannot sink from three shillings to two unless an increase in the productivity of labor makes it possible to produce in 4 hours the same quantity of necessities that previously took 6 hours to produce. On the other hand, the value of labor-power cannot rise from three shillings to four without a decrease in the productivity of labor, so that eight hours become necessary to produce the same quantity of necessities that six hours used to be enough for. It follows that an increase in the productivity of labor causes a fall in the value of labor-power and a resulting rise in surplus-value. On the other hand, a decrease in that productivity causes a rise in the value of labor-power and a fall in surplus-value.

<!-- ch17-p010 -->
In formulating this law, Ricardo overlooked one circumstance. A change in the magnitude of the surplus-value or surplus labor causes a change in the opposite direction in the magnitude of the value of labor-power, or in the quantity of necessary labor. (Necessary labor is the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages. Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.) But it by no means follows that they vary in the same proportion. They do increase or decrease by the same quantity. But their proportional increase or decrease depends on their original magnitudes, before the change in the productivity of labor took place. Suppose the value of labor-power is 4 shillings, or the necessary labor-time 8 hours, and the surplus-value is 2 shillings, or the surplus labor 4 hours. Then suppose that, as a result of an increase in the productivity of labor, the value of labor-power falls to 3 shillings, or the necessary labor to 6 hours. The surplus-value will then rise to 3 shillings, or the surplus labor to 6 hours. The same quantity, 1 shilling or 2 hours, is added in one case and subtracted in the other. But the proportional change of magnitude is different in each case. The value of labor-power falls from 4 shillings to 3, that is, by 1/4 or 25%, while the surplus-value rises from 2 shillings to 3, that is, by 1/2 or 50%. So it follows that the proportional increase or decrease in surplus-value that results from a given change in the productivity of labor depends on the original magnitude of the part of the working day that is embodied in surplus-value. The smaller that part, the greater the proportional change; the greater that part, the smaller the proportional change.

<!-- ch17-p011 -->
*(3.)* An increase or decrease in surplus-value is always the result, and never the cause, of the corresponding decrease or increase in the value of labor-power.[^2]

[^2]: To this third law MacCulloch has made, among others, this absurd addition: that a rise in surplus-value, without a fall in the value of labor-power, can come about through the abolition of taxes payable by the capitalist. But abolishing such taxes makes no change whatever in the quantity of surplus-value that the capitalist squeezes out of the worker at first hand. It alters only the proportion in which that surplus-value is divided between himself and third persons. So it makes no change whatever in the relation between surplus-value and the value of labor-power. MacCulloch's exception therefore proves only that he has misunderstood the rule, a misfortune that happens to him as often in vulgarizing Ricardo as it happens to J. B. Say in vulgarizing Adam Smith.

<!-- ch17-p012 -->
The working day is constant in magnitude and is represented by a value of constant magnitude. To every change in the magnitude of surplus-value there corresponds an opposite change in the value of labor-power. And the value of labor-power cannot change except as a result of a change in the productivity of labor. Under these conditions, then, it clearly follows that every change of magnitude in surplus-value arises from an opposite change of magnitude in the value of labor-power. We have already seen that there can be no change of absolute magnitude in the value of labor-power and in surplus-value without a change in their relative magnitudes. So now it follows that no change in their relative magnitudes is possible without a previous change in the absolute magnitude of the value of labor-power.

<!-- ch17-p013 -->
According to the third law, a change in the magnitude of surplus-value presupposes a movement in the value of labor-power, and this movement is brought about by a change in the productivity of labor. The limit of this change is given by the altered value of labor-power. Nevertheless, even when circumstances allow the law to operate, secondary movements may occur. For example, suppose that, as a result of the increased productivity of labor, the value of labor-power falls from 4 shillings to 3, or the necessary labor-time from 8 hours to 6. The price of labor-power may then possibly fall no lower than 3s. 8d., 3s. 6d., or 3s. 2d., and so the surplus-value may rise no higher than 3s. 4d., 3s. 6d., or 3s. 10d. How far the price falls, with 3 shillings (the new value of labor-power) as its lowest limit, depends on the relative weight thrown into the scale by the pressure of capital on the one side and the resistance of the worker on the other. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.)

<!-- ch17-p014 -->
The value of labor-power is determined by the value of a given quantity of necessities. It is the value, not the mass, of these necessities that varies with the productivity of labor. It is possible, however, that an increase in productivity may let both the worker and the capitalist take for themselves a greater quantity of these necessities at the same time, without any change in the price of labor-power or in surplus-value. Suppose the value of labor-power is 3 shillings, and the necessary labor-time amounts to 6 hours, and the surplus-value is likewise 3 shillings, and the surplus labor 6 hours. If the productivity of labor were then doubled without altering the ratio of necessary labor to surplus labor, there would be no change of magnitude in surplus-value or in the price of labor-power. The only result would be that each of them would represent twice as many use-values as before (a use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties), these use-values being twice as cheap as before. Although labor-power would be unchanged in price, it would be above its value. If, however, the price of labor-power had fallen, not to 1s. 6d., the lowest point possible given its new value, but to 2s. 10d. or 2s. 6d., this lower price would still represent an increased mass of necessities. In this way, with an increasing productivity of labor, the price of labor-power can keep on falling, and yet this fall can go along with a constant growth in the mass of the worker's means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live). But even in such a case, the fall in the value of labor-power would cause a corresponding rise of surplus-value, and so the gulf between the worker's position and the capitalist's would keep widening.[^3]

[^3]: “When an alteration takes place in the productiveness of industry, and that either more or less is produced by a given quantity of labour and capital, the proportion of wages [the price paid for labor-power] may obviously vary, whilst the quantity, which that proportion represents, remains the same, or the quantity may vary, whilst the proportion remains the same.” (“Outlines of Political Economy, &c.,” p. 67.)

<!-- ch17-p015 -->
Ricardo was the first to formulate accurately the three laws stated above. But he falls into the following errors. (1) He takes the special conditions under which these laws hold good to be the general and only conditions of capitalist production. He knows of no change either in the length of the working day or in the intensity of labor, so for him there can be only one variable factor, namely the productivity of labor. (2) This error damages his analysis much more than (1): like the other economists, he has not investigated surplus-value as such, that is, independently of its particular forms, such as profit, rent, etc. So he mixes up the laws of the rate of surplus-value with the laws of the rate of profit. The rate of profit is, as we have already said, the ratio of the surplus-value to the total capital advanced. The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of the surplus-value to the variable part of that capital. Assume that a capital C of £500 is made up of raw material, instruments of labor, etc. (c), to the amount of £400, and of wages (v) to the amount of £100. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) Assume further that the surplus-value (s) = £100. Then we have rate of surplus-value s/v = £100/£100 = 100%. But the rate of profit s/c = £100/£500 = 20%. It is obvious, besides, that the rate of profit may depend on circumstances that in no way affect the rate of surplus-value. I shall show in Book III. that, with a given rate of surplus-value, we may have any number of rates of profit, and that various rates of surplus-value may, under given conditions, be expressed in a single rate of profit.

### Section 2: Working day Constant. Productiveness of Labor Constant. Intensity of Labor Variable

<!-- ch17-p016 -->
Increased intensity of labor means that more labor is spent in a given time. So a working day of more intense labor is embodied in more products than a day of less intense labor, the length of each day being the same. It is true that increased productivity of labor will also supply more products in a given working day. But in that case the value of each single product falls, because it costs less labor than before. In the case of greater intensity, that value stays unchanged, because each article costs the same labor as before. Here we have an increase in the number of products without a fall in their individual prices: as their number increases, so does the sum of their prices. But in the case of increased productivity, a given value is spread over a greater mass of products. So, with the length of the working day constant, a day's labor of increased intensity will be embodied in an increased value and, if the value of money stays unchanged, in more money. The value created varies with how far the intensity of labor departs from the normal intensity in that society. So a given working day no longer creates a constant value but a variable one. In a day of 12 hours of ordinary intensity, the value created is, say, 6 shillings, but with increased intensity the value created may be 7, 8, or more shillings. Clearly, if the value created by a day's labor increases from, say, 6 to 8 shillings, then the two parts into which this value is divided, namely the price of labor-power and surplus-value, may both increase at the same time, either equally or unequally. They may both increase at the same time from 3 shillings to 4. Here, the rise in the price of labor-power does not necessarily mean that the price has risen above the value of labor-power. On the contrary, the rise in price may go along with a fall in value. This happens whenever the rise in the price of labor-power does not make up for its increased wear and tear.

<!-- ch17-p017 -->
We know that, with temporary exceptions, a change in the productivity of labor causes no change in the value of labor-power, and so none in the magnitude of surplus-value, unless the products of the industries affected are articles that the workers habitually consume. In the present case this condition no longer applies. For when the change is either in the duration or in the intensity of labor, there is always a corresponding change in the magnitude of the value created, whatever the nature of the article in which that value is embodied.

<!-- ch17-p018 -->
If the intensity of labor were to increase at the same time and equally in every branch of industry, then the new, higher degree of intensity would become the normal degree for the society, and so would cease to be taken into account. But even then, the intensity of labor would still differ from country to country, and would modify how the law of value applies internationally. The more intense working day of one nation would be represented by a greater sum of money than the less intense day of another nation.[^4]

[^4]: “All things being equal, the English manufacturer can turn out a considerably larger amount of work in a given time than a foreign manufacturer, so much as to counterbalance the difference of the working days, between 60 hours a week here, and 72 or 80 elsewhere.” (Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1855, p. 65.) The surest possible means of reducing this qualitative difference between the English and the Continental working hour would be a law that shortened, quantitatively, the length of the working day in Continental factories.

### Section 3: Productiveness and Intensity of Labor Constant. Length of the Working day Variable

<!-- ch17-p019 -->
The working day may vary in two ways. It may be made either longer or shorter. From our present data, and within the limits of the assumptions made above, we obtain the following laws:

<!-- ch17-p020 -->
*(1.)* The working day creates a greater or smaller amount of value in proportion to its length. So it creates a variable, not a constant, quantity of value.

<!-- ch17-p021 -->
*(2.)* Every change in the relation between the magnitudes of surplus-value and of the value of labor-power arises from a change in the absolute magnitude of the surplus labor, and so of the surplus-value.

<!-- ch17-p022 -->
*(3.)* The absolute value of labor-power can change only as a result of the effect that the lengthening of surplus labor has on the wear and tear of labor-power. So every change in this absolute value is the effect, but never the cause, of a change in the magnitude of surplus-value.

<!-- ch17-p023 -->
We begin with the case in which the working day is shortened.

<!-- ch17-p024 -->
*(1.)* Under the conditions given above, a shortening of the working day leaves the value of labor-power, and with it the necessary labor-time, unaltered. It reduces the surplus labor and the surplus-value. Along with the absolute magnitude of the surplus-value, its relative magnitude also falls, that is*,* its magnitude relative to the value of labor-power, whose magnitude stays unaltered. Only by lowering the price of labor-power below its value could the capitalist protect himself from loss.

<!-- ch17-p025 -->
All the usual arguments against shortening the working day assume that it takes place under the conditions we have supposed here. But in reality the very opposite is the case: a change in the productivity and intensity of labor either comes before a shortening of the working day or immediately follows it.[^5]

[^5]: “There are compensating circumstances ... which the working of the Ten Hours' Act has brought to light.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct. 1848,” p. 7.)

<!-- ch17-p026 -->
*(2.)* Lengthening of the working day. Let the necessary labor-time be 6 hours, or the value of labor-power 3 shillings; let the surplus labor also be 6 hours, or the surplus-value 3 shillings. The whole working day then amounts to 12 hours and is embodied in a value of 6 shillings. Now, if the working day is lengthened by 2 hours and the price of labor-power stays unaltered, the surplus-value increases both absolutely and relatively. Although there is no absolute change in the value of labor-power, it suffers a relative fall. Under the conditions assumed in 1. there could be no change of relative magnitude in the value of labor-power without a change in its absolute magnitude. Here, on the contrary, the change of relative magnitude in the value of labor-power is the result of the change of absolute magnitude in surplus-value.

<!-- ch17-p027 -->
The value in which a day's labor is embodied increases with the length of that day. So clearly the surplus-value and the price of labor-power may both increase at the same time, by either equal or unequal quantities. This increase of both at the same time is therefore possible in two cases: one, an actual lengthening of the working day; the other, an increase in the intensity of labor without such lengthening.

<!-- ch17-p028 -->
When the working day is prolonged, the price of labor-power may fall below its value, although that price stays nominally unchanged or even rises. The value of a day's labor-power is, as will be remembered, estimated from its normal average duration, or from the normal length of life among the workers, and from the corresponding normal conversions of the body's organized matter into motion,[^6] in keeping with human nature. Up to a certain point, the increased wear and tear of labor-power that is inseparable from a lengthened working day may be made up for by higher wages. But beyond this point the wear and tear increases in geometrical progression, multiplying at every step, and every condition needed for the normal reproduction and functioning of labor-power is suppressed. The price of labor-power and the degree of its exploitation cease to be quantities that can be measured against each other.

[^6]: “The amount of labour which a man had undergone in the course of 24 hours might be approximately arrived at by an examination of the chemical changes which had taken place in his body, changed forms in matter indicating the anterior [earlier] exercise of dynamic force.” (Grove: “On the Correlation of Physical Forces.”)

### Section 4: Simultaneous Variations in the Duration, Productiveness, and Intensity of Labor

<!-- ch17-p029 -->
Clearly, a large number of combinations are possible here. Any two of the factors may vary while the third stays constant, or all three may vary at once. They may vary to the same or to different degrees, in the same or in opposite directions, so that the changes cancel each other out, either wholly or in part. Even so, analyzing every possible case is easy in view of the results given in I., II., and III. The effect of every possible combination can be found by treating each factor in turn as variable, and the other two as constant for the time being. So we shall take note, and briefly at that, of only two important cases.

#### A. Diminishing Productiveness of Labor with a Simultaneous Lengthening of the Working day

<!-- ch17-p030 -->
When we speak of a falling productivity of labor, we mean here a fall in those industries whose products determine the value of labor-power. An example is the fall that results from the soil becoming less fertile, and from the corresponding rise in the price of its products. Take the working day at 12 hours and the value it creates at 6 shillings, of which one half replaces the value of labor-power and the other forms the surplus-value. Suppose that, because the products of the soil have become dearer, the value of labor-power rises from 3 shillings to 4, and so the necessary labor-time from 6 hours to 8. If there is no change in the length of the working day, the surplus labor would fall from 6 hours to 4, and the surplus-value from 3 shillings to 2. If the day is lengthened by 2 hours, that is, from 12 hours to 14, the surplus labor stays at 6 hours and the surplus-value at 3 shillings[^*1], but the surplus-value decreases compared with the value of labor-power, as measured by the necessary labor-time. If the day is lengthened by 4 hours, namely from 12 hours to 16, the proportional magnitudes of surplus-value and value of labor-power, of surplus labor and necessary labor, stay unchanged. But the absolute magnitude of surplus-value rises from 3 shillings to 4, and that of the surplus labor from 6 hours to 8, an increase of 33 1/3%. So, with a falling productivity of labor and a simultaneous lengthening of the working day, the absolute magnitude of surplus-value may stay unaltered while its relative magnitude decreases. Its relative magnitude may stay unchanged while its absolute magnitude increases. And, provided the day is lengthened enough, both may increase.

[^*1]: Earlier English translations have “6 sh.” instead of 3 shillings. A reader pointed this error out to us; we have looked into it, checked it against the 1872 German Edition, and duly corrected an obvious error.

<!-- ch17-p031 -->
In the period between 1799 and 1815, the rising price of provisions in England led to a nominal rise in wages, although real wages, expressed in the necessities of life, fell. From this fact West and Ricardo drew the conclusion that the fall in the productivity of agricultural labor had brought about a fall in the rate of surplus-value. They made this assumed fact, which existed only in their imaginations, the starting-point of important investigations into the relative magnitudes of wages, profits, and rent. But in actual fact, surplus-value had at that time increased in both absolute and relative magnitude, thanks to the increased intensity of labor and to the lengthening of the working day. This was the period in which the right to prolong the hours of labor to an outrageous extent was established;[^7] the period that was especially marked by accelerated accumulation of capital here, by pauperism there.[^8] (Accumulation of capital means turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows. Pauperism is the condition of the lowest layer of the surplus population, unable to find work and dependent on poor relief.)

[^7]: “Corn and labour rarely march quite abreast; but there is an obvious limit, beyond which they cannot be separated. With regard to the unusual exertions made by the labouring classes in periods of dearness, which produce the fall of wages noticed in the evidence” (namely, before the Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry, 1814-15), “they are most meritorious in the individuals, and certainly favour the growth of capital. But no man of humanity could wish to see them constant and unremitted. They are most admirable as a temporary relief; but if they were constantly in action, effects of a similar kind would result from them, as from the population of a country being pushed to the very extreme limits of its food.” (Malthus: “Inquiry into the Nature and Progress of Rent,” London, 1815, p. 48, note.) All honor to Malthus for stressing the lengthening of the hours of labor, a fact he draws attention to elsewhere in his pamphlet as well, while Ricardo and others, in the face of the most notorious facts, make an unchanging length of the working day the foundation of all their investigations. But the conservative interests that Malthus served kept him from seeing something. An unlimited prolongation of the working day, combined with an extraordinary development of machinery and the exploitation of women and children, was bound to make a great part of the working class “supernumerary,” particularly as soon as the war should cease and England's monopoly in the markets of the world should come to an end. It was, of course, far more convenient, and far more in keeping with the interests of the ruling classes, whom Malthus worshipped like a true priest, to explain this “over-population” by the eternal laws of Nature, rather than by the historical laws of capitalist production.

[^8]: “A principal cause of the increase of capital, during the war, proceeded from the greater exertions, and perhaps the greater privations of the labouring classes, the most numerous in every society. More women and children were compelled by necessitous circumstances, to enter upon laborious occupations, and former workmen were, from the same cause, obliged to devote a greater portion of their time to increase production.” (Essays on Pol. Econ., in which are illustrated the principal causes of the present national distress. London, 1830, p. 248.)

#### B. Increasing Intensity and Productiveness of Labor with Simultaneous Shortening of the Working day

<!-- ch17-p032 -->
Increased productivity and greater intensity of labor both have the same effect. They both increase the mass of articles produced in a given time. So both shorten the part of the working day that the worker needs to produce his means of subsistence or their equivalent. The minimum length of the working day is fixed by this part, which is necessary but able to shrink. If the whole working day were to shrink to the length of this part, surplus labor would vanish, an outcome utterly impossible under the rule of capital. Only by abolishing the capitalist form of production could the working day be reduced to the necessary labor-time. But even in that case, necessary labor-time would expand its limits. On the one hand, this is because the notion of “means of subsistence” would expand considerably, and the worker would lay claim to an altogether different standard of life. On the other hand, it is because a part of what is now surplus labor would then count as necessary labor; I mean the labor of forming a fund for reserve and accumulation.

<!-- ch17-p033 -->
The more the productivity of labor increases, the more the working day can be shortened; and the more the working day is shortened, the more the intensity of labor can increase. From the point of view of society, productivity increases in the same ratio as the economy of labor. This economy, in its turn, includes not only economy in the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things), but also the avoidance of all useless labor. The capitalist mode of production on the one hand enforces economy in each individual business. On the other hand, through its anarchic system of competition, it gives rise to the most outrageous waste of labor-power and of society's means of production, not to mention the creation of a vast number of jobs that are at present indispensable, but in themselves superfluous.

<!-- ch17-p034 -->
Given the intensity and productivity of labor, the time that society must devote to material production is shorter, and so the time at its disposal for the free development, intellectual and social, of the individual is greater, in proportion as the work is more and more evenly divided among all the able-bodied members of society, and as any particular class is more and more deprived of the power to shift the natural burden of labor from its own shoulders onto those of another layer of society. In this direction, the shortening of the working day finally finds a limit in the generalization of labor. In capitalist society, free time is gained for one class by turning the whole lifetime of the masses into labor-time.
