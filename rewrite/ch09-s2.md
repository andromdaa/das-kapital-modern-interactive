### Section 2: The Representation of the Components of the Value of the Product by Corresponding Proportional Parts of the Product Itself

<!-- ch09-p021 -->
Let us now go back to the example that showed us how the capitalist turns money into capital.

<!-- ch09-p022 -->
The product of a working day of 12 hours is 20 lbs. of yarn, with a value of 30s. No less than 8/10ths of this value, or 24s., is due simply to the reappearance in the yarn of the value of the means of production (20 lbs. of cotton, value 20s., and spindle worn away, 4s.). So this part is constant capital. The remaining 2/10ths, or 6s., is the new value created during the spinning process. One half of this replaces the value of the day's labor-power, that is, the variable capital; the other half makes up a surplus-value of 3s. So the total value of the 20 lbs. of yarn is made up as follows:

<!-- ch09-p023 -->
30s. value of yarn = 24s. const. + 3s. var. + 3s. surpl.

<!-- ch09-p024 -->
Since the whole of this value is contained in the 20 lbs. of yarn produced, it follows that the various component parts of this value can be represented as contained in corresponding parts of the product, each in its own part.

<!-- ch09-p025 -->
If the value of 30s. is contained in 20 lbs. of yarn, then 8/10ths of this value, or the 24s. that make up its constant part, is contained in 8/10ths of the product, that is, in 16 lbs. of yarn. Of these, 13 1/3 lbs. represent the value of the raw material, the 20s. worth of cotton spun, and 2 2/3 lbs. represent the 4s. worth of spindle, etc., worn away in the process.

<!-- ch09-p026 -->
So the whole of the cotton used up in spinning the 20 lbs. of yarn is represented by 13 1/3 lbs. of yarn. It is true that, by weight, this amount of yarn contains no more than 13 1/3 lbs. of cotton, worth 13 1/3 shillings. But the additional value of 6 2/3 shillings contained in it is the equivalent of the cotton used up in spinning the remaining 6 2/3 lbs. of yarn. The effect is the same as if these 6 2/3 lbs. of yarn contained no cotton at all, and the whole 20 lbs. of cotton were packed into the 13 1/3 lbs. of yarn. On the other hand, this last-named weight of yarn does not contain a single atom either of the value of the auxiliary materials and implements, or of the value newly created in the process.

<!-- ch09-p027 -->
In the same way, the 2 2/3 lbs. of yarn in which the 4s., the rest of the constant capital, is embodied represent nothing but the value of the auxiliary materials and instruments of labor used up in producing the 20 lbs. of yarn.

<!-- ch09-p028 -->
So we have arrived at this result: although eight-tenths of the product, or 16 lbs. of yarn, is, as a useful article, just as much the work of the spinner's labor as the rest of the same product, yet, seen in this connection, it does not contain, and has not absorbed, any labor spent during the process of spinning. It is just as if the cotton had turned itself into yarn without help, as if the shape it had taken were mere trickery and deceit. For as soon as our capitalist sells it for 24s. and uses the money to replace his means of production, it becomes clear that these 16 lbs. of yarn are nothing more than so much cotton and spindle-waste in disguise.

<!-- ch09-p029 -->
On the other hand, the remaining 2/10ths of the product, or 4 lbs of yarn, represent nothing but the new value of 6s. created during the 12 hours' spinning process. All the value transferred to those 4 lbs from the raw material and instruments of labor used up was, so to speak, intercepted so that it could be built into the 16 lbs. spun first. In this case it is as if the spinner had spun 4 lbs. of yarn out of air, or as if he had spun them with the help of cotton and spindles that were a spontaneous gift of Nature and so transferred no value to the product.

<!-- ch09-p030 -->
Of these 4 lbs. of yarn, in which the whole of the value newly created during the process is condensed, one half represents the equivalent of the value of the labor used up, or the 3s. variable capital; the other half represents the 3s. surplus-value.

<!-- ch09-p031 -->
Since 12 working hours of the spinner are embodied in 6s., it follows that yarn with a value of 30s. must have 60 working hours embodied in it. And this amount of labor-time does in fact exist in the 20 lbs of yarn. For in 8/10ths, or 16 lbs, are materialized the 48 hours of labor that were spent on the means of production before the spinning process began; and in the remaining 2/10ths, or 4 lbs, are materialized the 12 hours' work done during the process itself.

<!-- ch09-p032 -->
On an earlier page we saw that the value of the yarn is equal to the sum of the new value created during the production of that yarn plus the value that already existed in the means of production.

<!-- ch09-p033 -->
It has now been shown how the various component parts of the value of the product, parts that differ from each other in function, may be represented by corresponding proportional parts of the product itself.

<!-- ch09-p034 -->
The product can be split up in this way into different parts. One part represents only the labor previously spent on the means of production, or the constant capital. Another represents only the necessary labor spent during the process of production, or the variable capital. A third and last part represents only the surplus labor spent during the same process, or the surplus-value. To do this is no less important than it is simple, as will be seen later on, when it is applied to complicated problems that have not been solved until now.

<!-- ch09-p035 -->
In the investigation above, we have treated the total product as the final result, ready for use, of a working day of 12 hours. But we can also follow this total product through all the stages of its production. In this way we shall arrive at the same result as before, if we represent the partial products turned out at the different stages as functionally different parts of the final or total product.

<!-- ch09-p036 -->
The spinner produces 20 lbs. of yarn in 12 hours, or 1⅔ lbs in 1 hour. So in 8 hours he produces 13⅔ lbs., a partial product equal in value to all the cotton that is spun in a whole day. In the same way, the partial product of the next period of 1 hour and 36 minutes is 2⅔ lbs. of yarn; this represents the value of the instruments of labor that are used up in 12 hours. In the following hour and 12 minutes, the spinner produces 2 lbs. of yarn worth 3 shillings, a value equal to the whole value he creates in his 6 hours' necessary labor. Finally, in the last hour and 12 minutes he produces another 2 lbs. of yarn, whose value is equal to the surplus-value created by his surplus labor during half a day. The English manufacturer uses this method of calculation in everyday practice. It shows, he will say, that in the first 8 hours, or ⅔ of the working day, he gets back the value of his cotton, and so on for the remaining hours. It is also a perfectly correct method. In fact it is the first method given above, with this difference: instead of being applied to space, in which the different parts of the finished product lie side by side, it deals with time, in which those parts are produced one after another. But it can also come with very barbarous notions, especially in the heads of people who are as interested, in practice, in the process of making value breed value as they are in misunderstanding that process in theory. Such people may get it into their heads that our spinner, for example, produces or replaces in the first 8 hours of his working day the *value* of the cotton; in the following hour and 36 minutes the *value* of the instruments of labor worn away; in the next hour and 12 minutes the *value* of the wages; and that he devotes only that well-known “last hour” to producing surplus-value for the manufacturer. In this way the poor spinner is made to perform a double miracle. He not only produces cotton, spindles, steam-engine, coal, oil, etc., at the same time as he spins with them, but also turns one working day into five. For in the example we are considering, producing the raw material and instruments of labor takes four working days of twelve hours each, and turning them into yarn takes another such day.
