### Section 6: The Struggle for a Normal Working Day. Compulsory Limitation by Law of the Working-Time. English Factory Acts, 1833

<!-- ch10-p135 -->
After capital had taken centuries to extend the working day to its normal maximum limit, and then beyond this to the limit of the natural day of 12 hours,[^98] the birth of machinery and modern industry in the last third of the 18th century was followed by a violent encroachment, like an avalanche in its intensity and extent. Every bound set by morals and nature, by age and sex, by day and night, was broken down. Even the ideas of day and night, which in the old statutes had a rustic simplicity, became so confused that as late as 1860 an English judge needed a truly Talmudic subtlety to explain “judicially” what was day and what was night.[^99] Capital celebrated its orgies.

[^98]: “It is certainly much to be regretted that any class of persons should toil 12 hours a day, which, including the time for their meals and for going to and returning from their work, amounts, in fact, to 14 of the 24 hours.... Without entering into the question of health, no one will hesitate, I think, to admit that, *in a moral point of view*, so entire an absorption of the time of the working-classes, without intermission, from the early age of 13, and in trades not subject to restriction, much younger, must be extremely prejudicial, and is an evil greatly to be deplored.... For the sake, therefore, of public morals, of bringing up an orderly population, and of giving the great body of the people a reasonable enjoyment of life, it is much to be desired that in all trades some portion of every working day should be reserved for rest and leisure.” (Leonard Horner in “Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Dec., 1841.”)

[^99]: See “Judgment of Mr. J. H. Otway, Belfast. Hilary Sessions, County Antrim, 1860.”

<!-- ch10-p136 -->
At first the working class was stunned by the noise and turmoil of the new system of production. As soon as it had recovered its senses to some extent, its resistance began, and it began first in England, the homeland of machinery. For 30 years, however, the concessions the workers won were purely nominal. Parliament passed 5 labor laws between 1802 and 1833, but it was shrewd enough not to vote a penny for carrying them out, for the officials needed, etc.[^100]

[^100]: It is very characteristic of the regime of Louis Philippe, the bourgeois king, that the one Factory Act passed during his reign, that of March 22nd, 1841, was never put into force. And this law dealt only with child labor. It fixed 8 hours a day for children between 8 and 12, 12 hours for children between 12 and 16, etc., with many exceptions that allow night-work even for children 8 years old. In a country where every mouse is under police administration, the supervision and enforcement of this law are left to the good will of the *amis du commerce* [friends of trade]. Only since 1853 has a paid government inspector been appointed, and that in one single department, the Departement du Nord. No less characteristic of the development of French society in general is the fact that Louis Philippe’s law stood alone among the all-embracing mass of French laws until the Revolution of 1848.

<!-- ch10-p137 -->
> These laws remained a dead letter. “The fact is, that prior to the Act of 1833, young persons and children were worked all night, all day, or both *ad libitum* [at will].”[^101]

[^101]: “Report of Insp. of Fact.” 30th April, 1860, p. 50.

<!-- ch10-p138 -->
A normal working day for modern industry dates only from the Factory Act of 1833, which covered cotton, wool, flax, and silk factories. Nothing is more characteristic of the spirit of capital than the history of the English Factory Acts from 1833 to 1864.

<!-- ch10-p139 -->
The Act of 1833 declares that the ordinary factory working day runs from half-past five in the morning to half-past eight in the evening. Within these limits, a period of 15 hours, it is lawful to employ young persons (that is, persons between 13 and 18 years of age) at any time of the day, provided that no individual young person works more than 12 hours in any one day, except in certain cases specially provided for. The 6th section of the Act provided: “That there shall be allowed in the course of every day not less than one and a half hours for meals to every such person restricted as hereinbefore provided.” The employment of children under 9 was forbidden, with exceptions mentioned later. The work of children between 9 and 13 was limited to 8 hours a day. Night-work, that is, according to this Act, work between 8:30 p.m. and 5:30 a.m., was forbidden for all persons between 9 and 18.

<!-- ch10-p140 -->
The law-makers were so far from wishing to infringe on the freedom of capital to exploit adult labor-power, or, as they called it, “the freedom of labour,” that they created a special system to prevent the Factory Acts from having so outrageous a consequence.

<!-- ch10-p141 -->
> “The great evil of the factory system [production in which a system of machines, driven by a central power source, does the work and workers attend the machines] as at present conducted,” says the first report of the Central Board of the Commission of June 28th 1833, “has appeared to us to be that it entails the necessity of continuing the labour of children to the utmost length of that of the adults. The only remedy for this evil, short of the limitation of the labour of adults which would, in our opinion, create an evil greater than that which is sought to be remedied, appears to be the plan of working double sets of children.”

<!-- ch10-p142 -->
... Under the name of System of Relays, this “plan” was therefore carried out. For example, from 5.30 a.m. until 1.30 in the afternoon one set of children between 9 and 13 was “put to” [set to work], and from 1.30 p.m. to 8.30 in the evening another set, etc.

<!-- ch10-p143 -->
To reward the manufacturers for having ignored, in the most barefaced way, all the Acts on children’s labor passed during the last twenty-two years, the pill was gilded for them still further. Parliament decreed that after March 1st, 1834, no child under 11, after March 1st 1835, no child under 12, and after March 1st, 1836, no child under 13 was to work more than eight hours in a factory. This “liberalism,” so full of consideration for “capital,” was all the more remarkable because Dr. Farre, Sir A. Carlisle, Sir B. Brodie, Sir C. Bell, Mr. Guthrie, etc., in a word, the most distinguished physicians and surgeons in London, had declared in their evidence before the House of Commons that delay was dangerous. Dr. Farre put it still more bluntly.

<!-- ch10-p144 -->
> “Legislation is necessary for the prevention of death, in any form in which it can be prematurely inflicted, and certainly this (i.e., the factory method) must be viewed as a most cruel mode of inflicting it.”

<!-- ch10-p145 -->
That same “reformed” Parliament, in its delicate consideration for the manufacturers, condemned children under 13, for years to come, to 72 hours of work per week in the Factory Hell. On the other hand, in the Emancipation Act, which also handed out freedom drop by drop, it forbade the planters, from the very start, to work any Black slave more than 45 hours a week.

<!-- ch10-p146 -->
But capital was in no way reconciled, and it now began a noisy agitation that went on for several years. The agitation turned mainly on the age of those who, under the name of children, were limited to 8 hours’ work and were subject to a certain amount of compulsory education. According to capitalistic anthropology, the age of childhood ended at 10, or at the very most at 11. The nearer the time came for the Factory Act to come into full force, the fatal year 1836, the more wildly the mob of manufacturers raged. In fact, they managed to intimidate the government so much that in 1835 it proposed to lower the limit of the age of childhood from 13 to 12. In the meantime the pressure from outside grew more threatening. The House of Commons lost its courage. It refused to throw children of 13 under the Juggernaut Car of capital for more than 8 hours a day, and the Act of 1833 came into full operation. It remained unchanged until June, 1844.

<!-- ch10-p147 -->
In the ten years during which the Act regulated factory work, first in part and then entirely, the official reports of the factory inspectors are full of complaints that it was impossible to put the Act into force. The law of 1833 left the lords of capital free, during the 15 hours from 5.30 a.m. to 8.30 p.m., to make every “young person” and every “child” begin, break off, resume, or end his 12 or 8 hours at any moment they liked. It also allowed them to give different persons different times for meals. So these gentlemen soon discovered a new “system of relays,” in which the labor-horses were not changed at fixed stations but were constantly re-harnessed at changing stations. We will not dwell longer on the beauty of this system, since we shall have to return to it later. But this much is clear at first glance: this system canceled the whole Factory Act, not only in its spirit but in its letter. With this complex bookkeeping for each individual child or young person, how could factory inspectors enforce the legally fixed work-time and the granting of the legal mealtimes? In a great many of the factories the old brutalities soon blossomed out again unpunished. In an interview with the Home Secretary (1844), the factory inspectors demonstrated that any control was impossible under the newly invented relay system.[^102] In the meantime, however, circumstances had greatly changed. The factory workers, especially since 1838, had made the Ten Hours’ Bill their economic election-cry, as they had made the Charter their political one. Even some of the manufacturers who had run their factories in line with the Act of 1833 flooded Parliament with written petitions about the immoral competition of their false brethren, whom greater impudence or luckier local circumstances enabled to break the law. Moreover, however much the individual manufacturer might give free rein to his old lust for gain, the spokesmen and political leaders of the manufacturing class ordered a change of front and of language toward the workers. They had entered the contest for the repeal of the Corn Laws (the tariffs on imported grain), and needed the workers to help them to victory. So they promised not only a double-sized loaf of bread but also the enactment of the Ten Hours’ Bill in the Free-trade millennium.[^103] All the less, then, did they dare to oppose a measure meant only to make the law of 1833 a reality. Threatened in their holiest interest, the rent of land, the Tories thundered with philanthropic indignation against the “nefarious practices”[^104] of their foes.

[^102]: “Rept. of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st October, 1849, p. 6

[^103]: “Rept. of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st October, 1848, p. 98.

[^104]: Leonard Horner uses the expression “nefarious practices” in his official reports. (“Report of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st October, 1859, p. 7.)

<!-- ch10-p148 -->
This was the origin of the additional Factory Act of June 7th, 1844. It came into effect on September 10th, 1844. It places a new category of workers under protection, namely women over 18. They were placed in every respect on the same footing as the young persons: their work time was limited to twelve hours, their night-labor was forbidden, etc. For the first time, legislation found itself compelled to control the labor of adults directly and officially. The Factory Report of 1844-1845 says with irony:

<!-- ch10-p149 -->
> “No instances have come to my knowledge of adult women having expressed any regret at their *rights* being thus far interfered with.”[^105] The working time of children under 13 was reduced to 6½ hours a day, and in certain circumstances to 7 hours.[^106]

[^105]: “Rept.,” etc., 30th Sept., 1844, p. 15.

[^106]: The Act allows children to be employed for 10 hours if they do not work day after day, but only on alternate days. On the whole, this clause remained a dead letter.

<!-- ch10-p150 -->
To get rid of the abuses of the “spurious relay system,” the law laid down, among others, the following important regulations: –

<!-- ch10-p151 -->
> “That the hours of work of children and young persons shall be reckoned from the time when any child or young person shall begin to work in the morning.”

<!-- ch10-p152 -->
So if A, for example, begins work at 8 in the morning and B at 10, B’s work-day must still end at the same hour as A’s. “The time shall be regulated by a public clock,” for example the nearest railway clock, by which the factory clock is to be set. The occupier is to hang up a “legible” printed notice stating the hours for the beginning and ending of work and the times allowed for the various meals. Children who begin work before 12 noon may not be employed again after 1 p.m. So the afternoon shift must consist of children other than those employed in the morning. Of the hour and a half for meal-times,

<!-- ch10-p153 -->
> “one hour thereof at the least shall be given before three of the clock in the afternoon ... and at the same period of the day. No child or young person shall be employed more than five hours before 1 p.m. without an interval for meal-time of at least 30 minutes. No child or young person [or female] shall be employed or allowed to remain in any room in which any manufacturing process is then [that is, at mealtimes] carried on,” etc.

<!-- ch10-p154 -->
We have seen that these minute details, which regulate with military uniformity, by the stroke of the clock, the times, limits, and pauses of work, were not at all the products of Parliamentary fancy. They developed gradually out of circumstances, as natural laws of the modern mode of production. Their formulation, official recognition, and proclamation by the State were the result of a long struggle between classes. One of their first consequences was that in practice the working day of the adult males in factories became subject to the same limits, since in most processes of production the cooperation of the children, young persons, and women is indispensable. On the whole, therefore, during the period from 1844 to 1847, the 12 hours’ working day became general and uniform in all branches of industry under the Factory Act.

<!-- ch10-p155 -->
The manufacturers, however, did not allow this “progress” without a compensating “retrogression.” At their instigation the House of Commons lowered the minimum age of exploitable children from 9 to 8. This was to ensure the additional supply of factory children that is due to capitalists according to divine and human law.[^107]

[^107]: “As a reduction in their hours of work would cause a larger number (of children) to be employed, it was thought that the additional supply of children from 8 to 9 years of age would meet the increased demand” (cited above, p. 13).

<!-- ch10-p156 -->
The years 1846-47 opened a new era in the economic history of England. In those years the Corn Laws were repealed, along with the duties on cotton and other raw materials. Free trade was proclaimed the guiding star of legislation. In a word, the millennium had arrived. On the other hand, in those same years the Chartist movement and the agitation for 10 hours reached their highest point. They found allies in the Tories, who were hungry for revenge. The army of Free-traders, oath-breakers with Bright and Cobden at their head, opposed the bill fanatically. Despite them, the Ten Hours’ Bill, fought for over so long, passed through Parliament.

<!-- ch10-p157 -->
The new Factory Act of June 8th, 1847, laid down that on July 1st, 1847, the working day of “young persons” (from 13 to 18) and of all females should be shortened, as a first step, to 11 hours, and that on May 1st, 1848, it should be limited once and for all to 10 hours. In other respects, the Act only amended and completed the Acts of 1833 and 1844.

<!-- ch10-p158 -->
Capital now launched a preliminary campaign to prevent the Act from coming into full force on May 1st, 1848. And the workers themselves, under the pretense that experience had taught them, were to help destroy their own work. The moment was cleverly chosen.

<!-- ch10-p159 -->
> “It must be remembered, too, that there has been more than two years of great suffering (in consequence of the terrible crisis of 1846-47) among the factory operatives, from many mills having worked short time, and many being altogether closed. A considerable number of the operatives must therefore be in very narrow circumstances; many, it is to be feared, in debt; so that it might fairly have been presumed that at the present time they would prefer working the longer time, in order to make up for past losses, perhaps to pay off debts, or get their furniture out of pawn, or replace that sold, or to get a new supply of clothes for themselves and their families.”[^108]

[^108]: Rep. of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st Oct., 1848, p. 16.

<!-- ch10-p160 -->
The manufacturers tried to make the natural effect of these circumstances worse by a general wage cut of 10%. This was done, so to speak, to celebrate the opening of the new Free-trade era. Then came a further cut of 8 1/3% as soon as the working day was shortened to 11 hours, and a cut of double that amount as soon as it was finally shortened to 10 hours. So wherever circumstances allowed it, wages were cut by at least 25%.[^109] Under conditions so favorably prepared, the agitation among the factory workers for the repeal of the Act of 1847 was begun. Neither lies, bribery, nor threats were spared in this attempt. But it was all in vain. As for the half-dozen petitions in which workers were made to complain of “their oppression by the Act,” the petitioners themselves declared under oral examination that their signatures had been extorted from them. “They felt themselves oppressed, but not exactly by the Factory Act.”[^110] But if the manufacturers did not succeed in making the workers speak as they wished, they themselves shrieked all the louder, in the press and in Parliament, in the name of the workers. They denounced the Factory Inspectors as a kind of revolutionary commissioners, like those of the French National Convention, ruthlessly sacrificing the unhappy factory workers to their humanitarian whim. This maneuver also failed. Factory Inspector Leonard Horner, in person and through his sub-inspectors, examined many witnesses in the factories of Lancashire. About 70% of the workers examined declared in favor of 10 hours, a much smaller percentage in favor of 11, and an altogether insignificant minority for the old 12 hours.[^111]

[^109]: “I found that men who had been getting 10s. a week, had had 1s. taken off for a reduction in the rate of 10 per cent, and 1s. 6d. off the remaining 9s. for the reduction in time, together 2s. 6d., and notwithstanding this, many of them said they would rather work 10 hours.” Cited above.

[^110]: “‘Though I signed it [the petition], I said at the time I was putting my hand to a wrong thing.’ ‘Then why did you put your hand to it?’ ‘Because I should have been turned off [dismissed] if I had refused.’ Whence it would appear that this petitioner felt himself ‘oppressed,’ but not exactly by the Factory Act.” Cited above, p. 102.

[^111]: p. 17, cited above. In Mr. Horner’s district 10,270 adult male workers were examined in this way in 181 factories. Their evidence is in the appendix to the Factory Reports for the half-year ending October 1848. These examinations also provide valuable material in other connections.

<!-- ch10-p161 -->
Another “friendly” dodge was to make the adult males work 12 to 15 hours, and then to trumpet this fact abroad as the best proof of what the proletariat desired in its heart of hearts. But the “ruthless” Factory Inspector Leonard Horner was again on the spot. The majority of the “over-times” (the men working the longer hours) declared:

<!-- ch10-p162 -->
> “They would much prefer working ten hours for less wages, but that they had no choice; that so many were out of employment (so many spinners getting very low wages by having to work as piecers, being unable to do better), that if they refused to work the longer time, others would immediately get their places, so that it was a question with them of agreeing to work the longer time, or of being thrown out of employment altogether.”[^112]

[^112]: Cited above. See the evidence collected by Leonard Horner himself, Nos. 69, 70, 71, 72, 92, 93, and that collected by Sub-Inspector A., Nos. 51, 52, 58, 59, 62, 70, of the Appendix. One manufacturer, too, tells the plain truth. See No. 14, and No. 265, cited above.

<!-- ch10-p163 -->
So the preliminary campaign of capital came to grief, and the Ten Hours’ Act came into force on May 1st, 1848. But meanwhile the fiasco of the Chartist party, whose leaders were imprisoned and whose organization was broken up, had shaken the English working class’s confidence in its own strength. Soon after this, the June insurrection in Paris and its bloody suppression united all factions of the ruling classes, in England as on the Continent: landlords and capitalists, stock-exchange wolves and shop-keepers, Protectionists and Free-traders, government and opposition, priests and freethinkers, young whores and old nuns. They united under the common cry for the salvation of Property, Religion, the Family and Society. Everywhere the working class was outlawed, placed under a ban, under a virtual law of suspects. The manufacturers no longer had any need to restrain themselves. They broke out in open revolt, not only against the Ten Hours’ Act, but against the whole of the legislation that since 1833 had aimed at restricting to some extent the “free” exploitation of labor-power. It was a pro-slavery rebellion in miniature, carried on for over two years with a cynical recklessness and a terrorist energy that came all the cheaper because the rebel capitalist risked nothing except the skin of his “hands.”

<!-- ch10-p164 -->
To understand what comes next, we must keep the following in mind. The Factory Acts of 1833, 1844, and 1847 were all three in force, insofar as one did not amend another. Not one of them limited the working day of the male worker over 18. And since 1833 the 15 hours from 5.30 a.m. to 8.30 p.m. had remained the legal “day,” within whose limits first the 12, and later the 10 hours’ labor of young persons and women had to be performed under the prescribed conditions.

<!-- ch10-p165 -->
The manufacturers began by dismissing, here and there, part of the young persons and women they employed, in many cases half of them, and then by bringing back the almost obsolete night-work for the adult males. The Ten Hours’ Act, they cried, leaves no other alternative.[^113]

[^113]: Reports, &c., for 31st October, 1848, pp. 133, 134.

<!-- ch10-p166 -->
Their second step concerned the legal pauses for meals. Let us hear the Factory Inspectors.

<!-- ch10-p167 -->
> “Since the restriction of the hours of work to ten, the factory occupiers maintain, although they have not yet practically gone the whole length, that supposing the hours of work to be from 9 a.m. to 7 p.m. they fulfil the provisions of the statutes by allowing an hour before 9 a.m. and half an hour after 7 p.m. [for meals]. In some cases they now allow an hour, or half an hour for dinner, insisting at the same time, that they are not bound to allow any part of the hour and a half in the course of the factory working day.”[^114] So the manufacturers maintained that the scrupulously strict provisions of the Act of 1844 on meal-times only gave the factory workers permission to eat and drink before coming into the factory and after leaving it, that is, at home. And why should the workers not eat their dinner before 9 in the morning? The crown lawyers, however, decided that the prescribed meal-times

[^114]: Reports, &c., for 30th April, 1848, p. 47.

<!-- ch10-p168 -->
> “must be in the interval during the working-hours, and that it will not be lawful to work for 10 hours continuously, from 9 a.m. to 7 p.m., without any interval.”[^115]

[^115]: Reports, &c., for 31st October, 1848, p. 130.

<!-- ch10-p169 -->
After these pleasant demonstrations, Capital opened its revolt with a step that agreed with the letter of the law of 1844, and was therefore legal.

<!-- ch10-p170 -->
The Act of 1844 certainly prohibited employing after 1 p.m. those children, from 8 to 13, who had been employed before noon. But it did not regulate in any way the 6½ hours’ work of the children whose work-time began at 12 midday or later. Children of 8, if they began work at noon, might be employed from 12 to 1, 1 hour; from 2 to 4 in the afternoon, 2 hours; from 5 to 8.30 in the evening, 3½ hours; in all, the legal 6½ hours. Or better still. To make their work coincide with that of the adult male workers up to 8.30 p.m., the manufacturers only had to give them no work till 2 in the afternoon; they could then keep them in the factory without a break till 8.30 in the evening.

<!-- ch10-p171 -->
> “And it is now expressly admitted that the practice exists in England from the desire of mill-owners to have their machinery at work for more than 10 hours a-day, to keep the children at work with male adults after all the young persons and women have left, and until 8.30 p.m. if the factory-owners choose.”[^116]

[^116]: Reports, &c., cited above, p. 142.

<!-- ch10-p172 -->
Workers and factory inspectors protested on grounds of health and morals, but Capital answered:

<!-- ch10-p173 -->
> “My deeds upon my head! I crave the law,\
> The penalty and forfeit of my bond.”

<!-- ch10-p174 -->
In fact, according to statistics laid before the House of Commons on July 26th, 1850, in spite of all protests, on July 15th, 1850, 3,742 children in 257 factories were subjected to this “practice.”[^117] Still, this was not enough. The lynx eye of Capital discovered that the Act of 1844 did not allow 5 hours’ work before mid-day without a pause of at least 30 minutes for refreshment, but prescribed nothing of the kind for work after mid-day. So it demanded and obtained the enjoyment not only of making children of 8 drudge without a break from 2 to 8.30 p.m., but also of making them go hungry during that time.

[^117]: Reports &c., for 31st October, 1850, pp. 5, 6.
