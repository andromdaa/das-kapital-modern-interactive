## Chapter 5: Contradictions in the General Formula of Capital

<!-- ch05-p001 -->
When money becomes capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger), circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying) takes a form that goes against all the laws we have investigated so far concerning the nature of commodities, value and money, and even concerning circulation itself. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use. Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) What sets this form apart from the simple circulation of commodities is that the two opposed processes, sale and purchase, come in reverse order. How can this purely formal difference between these processes change their character, as if by magic?

<!-- ch05-p002 -->
But that is not all. This reversal does not exist at all for two of the three persons who do business together. As a capitalist, I buy commodities from A and sell them again to B. But as a simple owner of commodities, I sell them to B and then buy new ones from A. A and B see no difference between the two sets of transactions. They are merely buyers or sellers. And each time I meet them, I am a mere owner of either money or commodities, a buyer or a seller. What is more, in both sets of transactions I face A only as a buyer and B only as a seller: the one only as money, the other only as commodities. I face neither of them as capital or as a capitalist, or as the representative of anything that is more than money or commodities, or that can produce any effect beyond what money and commodities can. For me, the purchase from A and the sale to B are parts of a series. But the connection between the two acts exists for me alone. A does not bother himself about my transaction with B, nor does B about my business with A. And if I offered to explain to them how meritorious I had been in reversing the order of the two acts, they would probably point out that I was mistaken about that order. They would say that the whole transaction did not begin with a purchase and end with a sale, but, on the contrary, began with a sale and ended with a purchase. In truth, my first act, the purchase, was a sale from A’s standpoint, and my second act, the sale, was a purchase from B’s standpoint. Not content with that, A and B would declare that the whole series was superfluous and nothing but Hokus Pokus, and that in future A would buy directly from B, and B sell directly to A. So the whole transaction would shrink to a single act: an isolated phase in the ordinary circulation of commodities, with no phase to complete it, a mere sale from A’s point of view and a mere purchase from B’s. Reversing the order, therefore, does not take us outside the sphere of the simple circulation of commodities. We must rather look to see whether there is anything in this simple circulation that allows the value entering circulation to expand, and so allows surplus-value to be created.

<!-- ch05-p003 -->
Let us take the process of circulation in a form in which it appears as a simple and direct exchange of commodities. This is always the case when two owners of commodities buy from each other, and on the settling day the amounts they owe each other are equal and cancel out. In this case the money is money of account. It serves to express the value of the commodities in their prices (a price is the money name of the value, that is, of the labor, embodied in a commodity), but it does not itself face them as hard cash. As far as use-values are concerned (a use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties), clearly both parties may gain some advantage. Both part with goods that, as use-values, are of no service to them, and receive others that they can use. And there may also be a further gain. A, who sells wine and buys grain, possibly produces more wine with a given labor-time than farmer B could. B, on the other hand, possibly produces more grain than wine-grower A could. So, for the same exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for), A may get more grain, and B more wine, than each would get without any exchange, by producing his own grain and wine. So with respect to use-value, there is good reason to say that “exchange is a transaction by which both sides gain.”[^1] It is different with exchange-value. “A man who has plenty of wine and no corn treats [negotiates] with a man who has plenty of corn and no wine; an exchange takes place between them of corn to the value of 50, for wine of the same value. This act produces no increase of exchange-value either for the one or the other; for each of them already possessed, before the exchange, a value equal to that which he acquired by means of that operation.”[^2] The result does not change if money is brought in between the commodities as a medium of circulation (money in its role of passing from hand to hand to carry out sales and purchases), so that the sale and the purchase become two separate acts.[^3] The value of a commodity is expressed in its price before the commodity goes into circulation. So that value is a precondition of circulation, not its result.[^4]

[^1]: “L’échange est une transaction admirable dans laquelle les deux contractants gagnent ‒ toujours (!)” [“Exchange is an admirable transaction in which the two contracting parties gain ‒ always (!)”] (Destutt de Tracy: “Traité de la Volonté et de ses effets.” Paris, 1826, p. 68.) This work later appeared as “Traité d’Econ. Polit.”

[^2]: “Mercier de la Rivière,” cited above, p. 544.

[^3]: “Que l’une de ces deux valeurs soit argent, ou qu’elles soient toutes deux marchandises usuelles, rien de plus indifférent en soi.” [“Whether one of these two values is money, or both are ordinary commodities, in itself nothing could matter less.”] (“Mercier de la Rivière,” cited above, p. 543.)

[^4]: “Ce ne sont pas les contractants qui prononcent sur la valeur; elle est décidée avant la convention.” [“It is not the parties to the contract who decide on the value; it is settled before the agreement.”] (Le Trosne, p. 906.)

<!-- ch05-p004 -->
Considered in the abstract, that is, apart from circumstances that do not follow directly from the laws of the simple circulation of commodities, an exchange contains nothing (if we leave aside the replacing of one use-value by another) but a metamorphosis, a mere change in the form of the commodity. The same exchange-value, that is, the same quantity of embodied social labor, stays in the hands of the owner of the commodity throughout: first in the shape of his own commodity, then in the form of the money he exchanged it for, and lastly in the shape of the commodity he buys with that money. This change of form does not imply a change in the magnitude of the value (the magnitude of value is how much value a commodity has, measured by the socially necessary labor-time needed to produce it). But the change that the value of the commodity goes through in this process is limited to a change in its money form. This form exists first as the price of the commodity offered for sale; then as an actual sum of money, which, however, was already expressed in the price; and lastly as the price of an equivalent commodity. Taken alone, this change of form no more implies a change in the quantity of value than changing a £5 note into sovereigns, half sovereigns and shillings does. So, to the extent that the circulation of commodities changes only the form of their values, and is free from disturbing influences, it must be the exchange of equivalents. Vulgar economics (economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations) knows little about the nature of value. Yet whenever it wishes to consider the phenomena of circulation in their pure form, it assumes that supply and demand are equal, which amounts to saying that their effect is nil. So if both buyer and seller may possibly gain something as regards the use-values exchanged, this is not the case as regards the exchange-values. Here we must rather say, “Where equality exists there can be no gain.”[^5] It is true that commodities may be sold at prices that deviate from their values. But these deviations must be regarded as breaches of the laws of the exchange of commodities,[^6] which in its normal state is an exchange of equivalents, and so is no method for increasing value.[^7]

[^5]: “Dove è egualità non è lucro.” [“Where there is equality there is no gain.”] (Galiani, “Della Moneta in Custodi, Parte Moderna,” t. iv., p. 244.)

[^6]: “L’échange devient désavantageux pour l’une des parties, lorsque quelque chose étrangère vient diminuer ou exagérer le prix; alors l’égalité est blessée, mais la lésion procède de cette cause et non de l’échange.” [“Exchange becomes unfavorable for one of the parties when something external comes to lower or raise the price; then equality is harmed, but the harm comes from that cause and not from the exchange.”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 904.)

[^7]: “L’échange est de sa nature un contrat d’égalité qui se fait de valeur pour valeur égale. Il n’est donc pas un moyen de s’enrichir, puisque l’on donne autant que l’on reçoit.” [“Exchange is by its nature a contract of equality, made value for equal value. So it is not a means of getting rich, since one gives as much as one receives.”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 903.)

<!-- ch05-p005 -->
So we see that behind every attempt to present the circulation of commodities as a source of surplus-value there lurks a mix-up, a confusing of use-value with exchange-value. For instance, Condillac says: “It is not true that on an exchange of commodities we give value for value. On the contrary, each of the two contracting parties in every case, gives a less for a greater value. ... If we really exchanged equal values, neither party could make a profit. And yet, they both gain, or ought to gain. Why? The value of a thing consists solely in its relation to our wants. What is more to the one is less to the other, and *vice versâ* [the other way round]. ... It is not to be assumed that we offer for sale articles required for our own consumption. ... We wish to part with a useless thing, in order to get one that we need; we want to give less for more. ... It was natural to think that, in an exchange, value was given for value, whenever each of the articles exchanged was of equal value with the same quantity of gold. ... But there is another point to be considered in our calculation. The question is, whether we both exchange something superfluous for something necessary.”[^8] We see in this passage how Condillac not only confuses use-value with exchange-value, but also, in a really childish way, assumes that in a society where the production of commodities is well developed, each producer produces his own means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), and throws into circulation only what is left over beyond his own needs.[^9] Still, Condillac’s argument is often used by modern economists, especially when the point is to show that the exchange of commodities in its developed form, trade, produces surplus-value. For instance: “Commerce ... adds value to products, for the same products in the hands of consumers, are worth more than in the hands of producers, and it may strictly be considered an act of production.”[^10] But commodities are not paid for twice over, once for their use-value and again for their value. And though the use-value of a commodity is more useful to the buyer than to the seller, its money form is more useful to the seller. Would he otherwise sell it? So we might just as well say that the buyer performs “strictly an act of production” by turning stockings, for example, into money.

[^8]: Condillac: “Le Commerce et le Gouvernement” (1776). Edit. Daire et Molinari in the “Mélanges d’Econ. Polit.” Paris, 1847, pp. 267, 291.

[^9]: So Le Trosne rightly answers his friend Condillac as follows: “Dans une ... société formée il n’y a pas de surabondant en aucun genre.” [“In a ... developed society nothing of any kind is superfluous.”] At the same time he jokingly remarks: “If both the persons who exchange receive more to an equal amount, and part with less to an equal amount, they both get the same.” It is because Condillac has not the faintest idea of the nature of exchange-value that Herr Professor Wilhelm Roscher has chosen him as a fit person to vouch for the soundness of his own childish notions. See Roscher’s “Die Grundlagen der Nationalökonomie, Dritte Auflage,” 1858.

[^10]: S. P. Newman: “Elements of Polit. Econ.” Andover and New York, 1835, p. 175.

<!-- ch05-p006 -->
If commodities, or commodities and money, of equal exchange-value, and so equivalents, are exchanged, clearly no one takes more value out of circulation than he throws into it. No surplus-value is created. And in its normal form, the circulation of commodities requires the exchange of equivalents. But in actual practice, the process does not keep its normal form. So let us assume an exchange of non-equivalents.

<!-- ch05-p007 -->
In any case, the only people who visit the market for commodities are owners of commodities, and the power these persons exercise over each other is nothing other than the power of their commodities. The material variety of these commodities is the material incentive to exchange. It makes buyers and sellers dependent on one another, because none of them possesses the object of his own wants, and each holds in his hand the object of another’s wants. Besides these material differences between their use-values, there is only one other difference between commodities: the difference between their bodily form and the form into which they are changed by sale, the difference between commodities and money. And so the owners of commodities can be told apart only as sellers, who own commodities, and buyers, who own money.

<!-- ch05-p008 -->
Suppose, then, that by some inexplicable privilege the seller is able to sell his commodities above their value, what is worth 100 for 110, so that the price is raised nominally by 10%. The seller therefore pockets a surplus-value of 10. But after he has sold, he becomes a buyer. A third owner of commodities now comes to him as a seller, and in this capacity he too enjoys the privilege of selling his commodities 10% too dear. Our friend gained 10 as a seller only to lose it again as a buyer.[^11] The net result is that all owners of commodities sell their goods to one another at 10% above their value, which comes to exactly the same as if they sold them at their true value. Such a general and nominal rise in prices has the same effect as if values were expressed in weight of silver instead of in weight of gold. The nominal prices of commodities would rise, but the real relation between their values would stay unchanged.

[^11]: “By the augmentation of the nominal value of the produce... sellers not enriched... since what they gain as sellers, they precisely expend in the quality of buyers.” (“The Essential Principles of the Wealth of Nations.” etc., London, 1797, p. 66.)

<!-- ch05-p009 -->
Let us make the opposite assumption: that the buyer has the privilege of buying commodities below their value. In this case we no longer need to remember that he will become a seller in his turn. He was a seller before he became a buyer; he had already lost 10% as a seller before he gained 10% as a buyer.[^12] Everything is just as it was.

[^12]: “Si l’on est forcé de donner pour 18 livres une quantité de telle production qui en valait 24, lorsqu’on employera ce même argent à acheter, on aura également pour 18 l. ce que l’on payait 24.” [“If one is forced to give for 18 livres a quantity of some product that was worth 24, then when one uses this same money to buy, one will likewise get for 18 livres what one used to pay 24 for.”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 897.)

<!-- ch05-p010 -->
So the creation of surplus-value, and therefore the turning of money into capital, can be explained neither by assuming that commodities are sold above their value, nor by assuming that they are bought below their value.[^13]

[^13]: “Chaque vendeur ne peut donc parvenir à renchérir habituellement ses marchandises, qu’en se soumettant aussi à payer habituellement plus cher les marchandises des autres vendeurs; et par la même raison, chaque consommateur ne peut payer habituellement moins cher ce qu’il achète, qu’en se soumettant aussi à une diminution semblable sur le prix des choses qu’il vend.” [“So each seller can habitually raise the price of his commodities only by submitting, in turn, to habitually paying more for the commodities of the other sellers; and for the same reason each consumer can habitually pay less for what he buys only by submitting, in turn, to a similar reduction in the price of the things he sells.”] (Mercier de la Rivière, cited above, p. 555.)

<!-- ch05-p011 -->
Bringing in irrelevant matters, in the manner of Col. Torrens, does nothing to simplify the problem: “Effectual demand consists in the power and inclination (!), on the part of consumers, to give for commodities, either by immediate or circuitous barter, some greater portion of ... capital than their production costs.”[^14] In relation to circulation, producers and consumers meet only as buyers and sellers. To claim that the surplus-value the producer gets comes from the fact that consumers pay more than their value for commodities is only to say, in other words: The owner of commodities has, as a seller, the privilege of selling too dear. The seller has himself produced the commodities, or represents their producer. But the buyer has just as much produced the commodities represented by his money, or represents their producer. The difference between them is that one buys and the other sells. The fact that the owner of commodities, under the name of producer, sells them above their value and, under the name of consumer, pays too much for them, does not carry us a single step further.[^15]

[^14]: Torrens. “An Essay on the Production of Wealth.” London, 1821, p. 349.

[^15]: “The idea of profits being paid by the consumers, is, assuredly, very absurd. Who are the consumers?” (G. Ramsay: “An Essay on the Distribution of Wealth.” Edinburgh, 1836, p. 183.)

<!-- ch05-p012 -->
So, to be consistent, those who uphold the delusion that surplus-value comes from a nominal rise in prices, or from the seller’s privilege of selling too dear, must assume that there is a class that only buys and does not sell, that is, only consumes and does not produce. From the standpoint we have reached so far, namely that of simple circulation, the existence of such a class cannot be explained. But let us look ahead. The money with which such a class is constantly buying must constantly flow into its pockets from the pockets of the commodity owners themselves, without any exchange, free of charge, by might or by right. To sell commodities above their value to such a class is only to pinch back part of the money previously handed over to it.[^16] In this way the towns of Asia Minor paid a yearly money tribute to ancient Rome. With this money Rome bought commodities from them, and paid too much for them. The provincials cheated the Romans, and so, in the course of trade, got back from their conquerors a part of the tribute. Yet for all that, it was the conquered who were really cheated. Their goods were still paid for with their own money. That is not the way to get rich or to create surplus-value.

[^16]: “When a man is in want of a demand, does Mr. Malthus recommend him to pay some other person to take off his goods?” This question was put by an angry disciple of Ricardo to Malthus, who, like his disciple Parson Chalmers, glorifies this class of simple buyers or consumers in economic terms. (See “An Inquiry into those Principles Respecting the Nature of Demand and the Necessity of Consumption, lately advocated by Mr. Malthus,” etc. London, 1821, p. 55.)

<!-- ch05-p013 -->
So let us keep within the limits of exchange, where sellers are also buyers, and buyers are also sellers. Perhaps our difficulty has come from treating the actors as personifications instead of as individuals.

<!-- ch05-p014 -->
A may be clever enough to get the better of B or C without their being able to pay him back in kind. A sells wine worth £40 to B and gets from him in exchange grain worth £50. A has turned his £40 into £50, has made more money out of less, and has turned his commodities into capital. Let us look at this a little more closely. Before the exchange we had £40 worth of wine in A’s hands and £50 worth of grain in B’s, a total value of £90. After the exchange we still have the same total value of £90. The value in circulation has not increased by one iota; it is only distributed differently between A and B. What is a loss of value to B is surplus-value to A; what is “minus” to one is “plus” to the other. The same change would have taken place if A, without the formality of an exchange, had simply stolen the £10 from B. Clearly, the sum of the values in circulation cannot be increased by any change in their distribution, any more than the quantity of precious metals in a country is increased by a Jew selling a Queen Anne’s farthing for a guinea. The capitalist class of any country, taken as a whole, cannot cheat itself.[^17]

[^17]: Destutt de Tracy held the opposite view, although, or perhaps because, he was a member of the Institute. He says industrial capitalists make profits because “they all sell for more than it has cost to produce. And to whom do they sell? In the first instance to one another.” (cited above, p. 239.)

<!-- ch05-p015 -->
Turn and twist as we may, then, the fact stays the same. If equivalents are exchanged, no surplus-value results; and if non-equivalents are exchanged, still no surplus-value results.[^18] Circulation, or the exchange of commodities, creates no value.[^19]

[^18]: “L’échange qui se fait de deux valeurs égales n’augmente ni ne diminue la masse des valeurs subsistantes dans la société. L’échange de deux valeurs inégales ... ne change rien non plus à la somme des valeurs sociales, bien qu’il ajoute à la fortune de l’un ce qu’il ôte de la fortune de l’autre.” [“The exchange of two equal values neither increases nor decreases the mass of values existing in society. Nor does the exchange of two unequal values ... change anything in the sum of social values, although it adds to the fortune of one person what it takes from the fortune of the other.”] (J. B. Say, cited above, t. II, pp. 443, 444.) Say, not in the least troubled by the consequences of this statement, borrows it almost word for word from the Physiocrats. The following example will show how Monsieur Say made use of the writings of the Physiocrats, which in his day were quite forgotten, in order to expand the “value” of his own. His most famous saying, “On n’achète des produits qu’avec des produits” [“Products are bought only with products.”] (cited above, t. II. p. 441.), reads as follows in the original physiocratic work: “Les productions ne se paient qu’avec des productions.” [“Products are paid for only with products.”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 899.)

[^19]: “Exchange confers no value at all upon products.” (F. Wayland: “The Elements of Political Economy.” Boston, 1843, p. 169.)

<!-- ch05-p016 -->
So the reason is now clear why, in analyzing the standard form of capital, the form in which it determines the economic organization of modern society, we left entirely out of account its most popular forms, which date, so to speak, from before the Flood: merchants’ capital and money-lenders’ capital.

<!-- ch05-p017 -->
The circuit M—C—M, buying in order to sell dearer, is seen most clearly in genuine merchants’ capital. But the movement takes place entirely within the sphere of circulation. However, since it is impossible to explain the turning of money into capital, the formation of surplus-value, by circulation alone, it would seem that merchants’ capital is impossible as long as equivalents are exchanged;[^20] and that it can therefore only arise from the double advantage gained, over both the selling and the buying producers, by the merchant who shoves himself in between them like a parasite. It is in this sense that Franklin says, “war is robbery, commerce is generally cheating.”[^21] If the turning of merchants’ money into capital is to be explained otherwise than by the producers simply being cheated, a long series of intermediate steps would be needed. At present, when the simple circulation of commodities is our only assumption, these steps are entirely missing.

[^20]: Under the rule of invariable equivalents, trade would be impossible. (G. Opdyke: “A Treatise on Polit. Economy.” New York, 1851, pp. 66-69.) “The difference between real value and exchange-value is based upon this fact, namely, that the value of a thing is different from the so-called equivalent given for it in trade, i.e., that this equivalent is no equivalent.” (F. Engels, cited above, p. 96).

[^21]: Benjamin Franklin: Works, Vol. II, edit. Sparks in “Positions to be examined concerning National Wealth,” p. 376.

<!-- ch05-p018 -->
> What we have said about merchants’ capital applies even more to money-lenders’ capital. In merchants’ capital, the two extremes, the money thrown onto the market and the increased money withdrawn from the market, are at least connected by a purchase and a sale, in other words by the movement of circulation. In money-lenders’ capital, the form M—C—M is reduced to the two extremes with nothing in between, M—M , money exchanged for more money. This form is incompatible with the nature of money, and so it remains inexplicable from the standpoint of the circulation of commodities. Hence Aristotle: “since chrematistic [the art of money-making] is a double science, one part belonging to commerce, the other to economic [household management], the latter being necessary and praiseworthy, the former based on circulation and with justice disapproved (for it is not based on Nature, but on mutual cheating), therefore the usurer is most rightly hated, because money itself is the source of his gain, and is not used for the purposes for which it was invented. For it originated for the exchange of commodities, but interest makes out of money, more money. Hence its name (τοκος interest and offspring). For the begotten are like those who beget them. But interest is money of money, so that of all modes of making a living, this is the most contrary to Nature.”[^22]

[^22]: Aristotle, cited above, c. 10.

<!-- ch05-p019 -->
In the course of our investigation we shall find that both merchants’ capital and interest-bearing capital are derived forms. At the same time it will become clear why these two forms appear in the course of history before the modern standard form of capital.

<!-- ch05-p020 -->
We have shown that surplus-value cannot be created by circulation, and so that, for it to be formed, something must take place in the background that is not visible in circulation itself.[^23] But can surplus-value possibly arise anywhere else than in circulation, which is the sum total of all the relations between commodity owners, insofar as these are determined by their commodities? Outside circulation, the commodity owner is in relation only with his own commodity. As far as value is concerned, that relation is limited to this: the commodity contains a quantity of his own labor, and that quantity is measured by a definite social standard. This quantity is expressed by the value of the commodity. And since the value is reckoned in money of account, this quantity is also expressed by the price, which we will suppose to be £10. But his labor is not represented both by the value of the commodity and by a surplus over that value: not by a price of 10 that is also a price of 11, not by a value that is greater than itself. The commodity owner can create value by his labor, but not self-expanding value. He can increase the value of his commodity by adding fresh labor, and so more value, to the value already there, for instance by making leather into boots. The same material now has more value, because it contains a greater quantity of labor. So the boots have more value than the leather, but the value of the leather remains what it was. It has not expanded itself; it has not added surplus-value to itself while the boots were being made. It is therefore impossible for a producer of commodities, outside the sphere of circulation and without coming into contact with other commodity owners, to expand value, and so to turn money or commodities into capital.

[^23]: “Profit, in the usual condition of the market, is not made by exchanging. Had it not existed before, neither could it after that transaction.” (Ramsay, cited above, p. 184.)

<!-- ch05-p021 -->
So it is impossible for capital to be produced by circulation, and it is just as impossible for it to arise apart from circulation. It must have its origin both in circulation and yet not in circulation.

<!-- ch05-p022 -->
So we have a double result.

<!-- ch05-p023 -->
The turning of money into capital has to be explained on the basis of the laws that govern the exchange of commodities, in such a way that the starting point is the exchange of equivalents.[^24] Our friend Moneybags, who is as yet only a capitalist in embryo, must buy his commodities at their value and sell them at their value, and yet at the end of the process he must withdraw more value from circulation than he threw into it at the start. His growth into a full-grown capitalist must take place both within the sphere of circulation and outside it. These are the conditions of the problem. *Hic Rhodus, hic salta!* [Here is Rhodes, jump here!][^25]

[^24]: From the investigation above, the reader will see that this statement only means this: the formation of capital must be possible even if the price of a commodity and its value are the same, because its formation cannot be put down to any deviation of the one from the other. If prices actually differ from values, we must first reduce prices to values. In other words, we must treat the difference as accidental, so that the phenomena can be observed in their pure form, and our observations are not disturbed by circumstances that have nothing to do with the process in question. We know, moreover, that this reduction is not a merely scientific procedure. The constant swings in prices, their rising and falling, balance each other out and reduce themselves to an average price, which is their hidden regulator. This average price is the guiding star of the merchant or the manufacturer in every undertaking that takes time. He knows that when a long period of time is taken, commodities are sold neither above nor below their average price, but at it. So if he thought about the matter at all, he would put the problem of the formation of capital this way: How can we explain the origin of capital on the assumption that prices are regulated by the average price, that is, ultimately by the value of the commodities? I say “ultimately,” because average prices do not directly coincide with the values of commodities, as Adam Smith, Ricardo, and others believe.

[^25]: “Hic Rhodus, hic saltus!” – Latin, usually translated: “Rhodes is here, here is where you jump!”

    It comes from the traditional Latin translation of the punch line of Aesop’s fable The Boastful Athlete, which has been mistranslated in several ways. In Greek, the saying reads:

    > “ιδού η ρόδος, ιδού και το πήδημα” [“Here is Rhodes, here too is the jump”]

    The story is that an athlete boasts that when he was in Rhodes he made a stupendous jump, and that there were witnesses who could back up his story. A bystander then remarked, ‘Alright! Let’s say this is Rhodes, demonstrate the jump here and now.’ The fable shows that people must be known by their deeds, not by the claims they make for themselves. In the context in which Hegel used it in the *Philosophy of Right*, this could be taken to mean that the philosophy of right must deal with the *actuality* of modern society, not with the theories and ideals that societies create for themselves, nor, as Hegel goes on to say, with trying to “teach the world what it ought to be.”

    Hegel gives the saying first in Greek, then in Latin (in the form “*Hic* Rhodus, *hic* saltus”), and he then says: “With little change, the above saying would read (in German): “*Hier* ist die Rose, *hier* tanze”: “Here is the rose, dance here”

    This is taken to be an allusion to the ‘rose in the cross’ of the Rosicrucians (who claimed to possess secret knowledge with which they could transform social life), implying that the material for understanding and changing society is found in society itself, not in some other-worldly theory, punning first on the Greek (*Rhodos* = Rhodes, *rhodon* = rose), then on the Latin (*saltus* = jump [noun], *salta* = dance [imperative]). [MIA Editors.]
