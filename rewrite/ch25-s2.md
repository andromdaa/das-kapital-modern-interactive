### Section 2: Relative Diminution of the Variable Part of Capital Simultaneously with the Progress of Accumulation and of the Concentration that Accompanies it

<!-- ch25-p016 -->
According to the economists themselves, what leads to a rise in wages is neither the actual extent of social wealth nor the size of the capital already at work. It is only the constant growth of accumulation, and the speed of that growth. (Adam Smith, Book I., chapter 8.) So far, we have considered only one special phase of this process: the phase in which capital increases while the technical composition of capital stays constant. But the process goes beyond this phase.

<!-- ch25-p017 -->
Once the general basis of the capitalist system is given, a point is reached in the course of accumulation at which the development of the productivity of social labor (how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time) becomes the most powerful lever of accumulation.

<!-- ch25-p018 -->
> “The same cause,” says Adam Smith, “which raises the wages of labour, the increase of stock [capital], tends to increase its productive powers, and to make a smaller quantity of labour produce a greater quantity of work.”[^11]

[^11]: Adam Smith, “Enquiry into the Nature of ...”, Volume I.

<!-- ch25-p019 -->
Leave aside natural conditions, such as the fertility of the soil, etc. Leave aside, too, the skill of independent and isolated producers, which shows itself in the quality of their products rather than in their quantity. Apart from these, the degree of productivity of labor in a given society is expressed in the relative amount of means of production that one worker turns into products during a given time, with the same exertion of labor-power. The mass of means of production that he transforms in this way grows with the productivity of his labor. But these means of production play a double part. The increase of some is a consequence of the rising productivity of labor; the increase of others is a condition of it. For example, with the division of labor in manufacture, and with the use of machinery, more raw material is worked up in the same time. (Division of labor here means the splitting of the making of one product into many separate tasks, each done by a specialized worker. Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) So a greater mass of raw material and auxiliary materials enters into the labor process, the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things. That is the consequence of the rising productivity of labor. On the other hand, the mass of machinery, beasts of burden, mineral fertilizers, drain-pipes, etc., is a condition of the rising productivity of labor. So too are the means of production concentrated in buildings, furnaces, means of transport, etc. But whether condition or consequence, the growing amount of the means of production, compared with the labor-power incorporated into them, is an expression of the growing productivity of labor. So the increase of this productivity shows itself in a decrease of the mass of labor in proportion to the mass of means of production it sets in motion, or in a decrease of the subjective factor of the labor process compared with the objective factor.

<!-- ch25-p020 -->
This change in the technical composition of capital, this growth in the mass of means of production compared with the mass of the labor-power that brings them to life, is reflected in turn in its value composition: the constant part of capital increases at the expense of its variable part. For example, at first 50 percent of a capital may be laid out in means of production, and 50 percent in labor-power; later on, with the development of the productivity of labor, 80 percent in means of production and 20 percent in labor-power, and so on. This law of the progressive increase of constant capital in proportion to variable capital is confirmed at every step (as already shown) by comparative analysis of the prices of commodities, whether we compare different economic epochs or different nations in the same epoch. One element of price represents only the value of the means of production, that is, the constant part of capital consumed. Its relative size is in direct proportion to the advance of accumulation. The other element of price pays for labor (the variable part of capital). Its relative size is in inverse proportion to the advance of accumulation.

<!-- ch25-p021 -->
However, this decrease in the variable part of capital compared with the constant part, or the altered value composition of capital, shows only approximately the change in the composition of its material parts. Suppose, for example, that the capital-value employed today in spinning is 7/8 constant and 1/8 variable, while at the beginning of the 18th century it was ½ constant and ½ variable. On the other hand, the mass of raw material, instruments of labor, etc., that a certain quantity of spinning labor consumes productively today is many hundred times greater than at the beginning of the 18th century. The reason is simply that, with the rising productivity of labor, not only does the mass of the means of production it consumes increase, but their value compared with their mass falls. So their value rises absolutely, but not in proportion to their mass. The growth of the difference between constant and variable capital is therefore much smaller than the growth of the difference between two masses: the mass of means of production into which the constant capital is converted, and the mass of labor-power into which the variable capital is converted. The first difference increases with the second, but to a smaller degree.

<!-- ch25-p022 -->
But if the progress of accumulation reduces the relative size of the variable part of capital, it by no means, in doing so, rules out the possibility of a rise in its absolute size. Suppose that a capital-value is at first divided into 50 percent constant and 50 percent variable capital, and later into 80 percent constant and 20 percent variable. If in the meantime the original capital, say £6,000, has grown to £18,000, its variable part has also grown. It was £3,000; it is now £3,600. But where before an increase of capital by 20 percent would have been enough to raise the demand for labor by 20 percent, now this same rise requires the original capital to be tripled.

<!-- ch25-p023 -->
In Part IV it was shown how the development of the productivity of social labor presupposes cooperation on a large scale (many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist's command); how only on this condition can division and combination of labor be organized, and the means of production be economized by concentration on a vast scale; how instruments of labor that by their very nature can only be used in common, such as a system of machinery, can be called into being; how huge natural forces can be pressed into the service of production; and how the process of production can be transformed into a technological application of science. Take the basis of the production of commodities, where the means of production are the property of private persons, and where the craftsman therefore either produces commodities in isolation from and independently of others, or sells his labor-power as a commodity because he lacks the means to work independently. On this basis, cooperation on a large scale can come about only through the growth of individual capitals, only in proportion as the means of social production and the means of subsistence are turned into the private property of capitalists. The basis of the production of commodities can allow production on a large scale only in the capitalist form. So a certain accumulation of capital in the hands of individual producers of commodities is the necessary precondition of the specifically capitalist mode of production. We therefore had to assume that this happens during the transition from handicraft to capitalist industry. It may be called primitive accumulation, because it is the historical basis, instead of the historical result, of specifically capitalist production. How it itself arises we need not ask here yet. It is enough that it forms the starting point. But all the methods for raising the social productive power of labor that are developed on this basis are at the same time methods for producing more surplus-value or surplus-product, which in its turn is the element out of which accumulation is formed. So they are at the same time methods for producing capital by means of capital, or methods for speeding up its accumulation. The continual turning of surplus-value back into capital now shows itself as the growing size of the capital that enters into the process of production. This in turn is the basis of a larger scale of production, of the methods for raising the productive power of labor that go with it, and of a faster production of surplus-value. So if a certain degree of accumulation of capital appears as a condition of the specifically capitalist mode of production, the capitalist mode of production in its turn causes a faster accumulation of capital. With the accumulation of capital, therefore, the specifically capitalist mode of production develops, and with the capitalist mode of production, the accumulation of capital. These two economic factors, in the compound ratio of the pushes they give each other, bring about that change in the technical composition of capital by which the variable part becomes smaller and smaller compared with the constant part.

<!-- ch25-p024 -->
Every individual capital is a larger or smaller concentration of means of production, with a matching command over a larger or smaller labor-army. Every accumulation becomes the means of new accumulation. As the mass of wealth that functions as capital increases, accumulation increases the concentration of that wealth in the hands of individual capitalists. In this way it widens the basis of production on a large scale and of the specific methods of capitalist production. Social capital grows through the growth of many individual capitals. All other circumstances remaining the same, individual capitals, and with them the concentration of the means of production, grow in proportion to the fraction of the total social capital that each forms. At the same time, portions of the original capitals break away and function as new independent capitals. Among other causes, the division of property within capitalist families plays a large part in this. So, with the accumulation of capital, the number of capitalists grows to a greater or lesser extent. Two points mark this kind of concentration, which grows directly out of accumulation, or rather is identical with it. First: the growing concentration of the social means of production in the hands of individual capitalists is limited, other things remaining equal, by the degree to which social wealth grows. Second: the part of social capital settled in each particular sphere of production is divided among many capitalists, who face one another as independent commodity-producers competing with each other. So accumulation, and the concentration that goes with it, are not only scattered over many points; the growth of each functioning capital is also thwarted by the formation of new capitals and the splitting-up of old ones. Accumulation therefore presents itself, on the one hand, as growing concentration of the means of production and of the command over labor; on the other, as repulsion of many individual capitals from one another.

<!-- ch25-p025 -->
This splitting-up of the total social capital into many individual capitals, or the repulsion of its fractions from one another, is counteracted by their attraction. This attraction does not mean that simple concentration of the means of production and of the command over labor which is identical with accumulation. It is concentration of capitals already formed: the destruction of their individual independence, the expropriation of capitalist by capitalist (expropriation means taking away people's property, here one capitalist's by another), the transformation of many small capitals into few large ones. This process differs from the first in that it presupposes only a change in the distribution of capital already at hand and functioning. So its field of action is not limited by the absolute growth of social wealth, by the absolute limits of accumulation. Capital grows in one place to a huge mass in a single hand because in another place it has been lost by many. This is centralization proper, as distinct from accumulation and concentration.

<!-- ch25-p026 -->
The laws of this centralization of capitals, or of the attraction of capital by capital, cannot be developed here. A brief hint at a few facts must be enough. The battle of competition is fought by making commodities cheaper. Other things being equal, the cheapness of commodities depends on the productivity of labor, and this in turn on the scale of production. So the larger capitals beat the smaller. It will be remembered, too, that with the development of the capitalist mode of production, the minimum amount of individual capital needed to carry on a business under its normal conditions increases. The smaller capitals therefore crowd into spheres of production that modern industry (large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system) has taken hold of only here and there, or incompletely. Here competition rages in direct proportion to the number, and in inverse proportion to the size, of the rival capitals. It always ends in the ruin of many small capitalists, whose capitals partly pass into the hands of their conquerors and partly vanish. Apart from this, an altogether new force comes into play with capitalist production: the credit system. In its first stages it creeps in stealthily as the humble assistant of accumulation, drawing by invisible threads, into the hands of individual or associated capitalists, the money resources that lie scattered in larger or smaller amounts over the surface of society. But it soon becomes a new and terrible weapon in the battle of competition, and is finally transformed into an enormous social mechanism for the centralization of capitals.

<!-- ch25-p027 -->
As capitalist production and accumulation develop, the two most powerful levers of centralization develop to the same degree: competition and credit. At the same time, the progress of accumulation increases the material that can be centralized, that is, the individual capitals. Meanwhile the expansion of capitalist production creates, on the one hand, the social need and, on the other, the technical means for those immense industrial undertakings that can only be carried out after capital has first been centralized. Today, therefore, the force of attraction drawing individual capitals together, and the tendency to centralization, are stronger than ever before. But if the relative extent and energy of the movement toward centralization is determined, to a certain degree, by the size of capitalist wealth and the superiority of the economic mechanism already reached, progress in centralization does not in any way depend on a positive growth in the size of social capital. And this is the specific difference between centralization and concentration, concentration being only another name for reproduction on an extended scale. Centralization may result from a mere change in the distribution of capitals already existing, from a simple change in the quantitative grouping of the parts of social capital. Here capital can grow into powerful masses in a single hand because there it has been withdrawn from many individual hands. In any given branch of industry, centralization would reach its extreme limit if all the individual capitals invested in it were fused into a single capital.[^12] In a given society, the limit would be reached only when the entire social capital was united in the hands of either a single capitalist or a single capitalist company.

[^12]: *Note in the 4th German edition.* — The latest English and American “trusts” are already striving toward this goal. They are trying to unite at least all the large-scale concerns in one branch of industry into one great joint-stock company with a practical monopoly. *F. E.*

<!-- ch25-p028 -->
Centralization completes the work of accumulation by enabling industrial capitalists to extend the scale of their operations. This result may be the consequence of accumulation or of centralization. Centralization may be accomplished by the violent method of annexation, when certain capitals become such overwhelming centers of attraction for others that they shatter the individual cohesion of those others and then draw the separate fragments to themselves. Or a number of capitals, already formed or in the process of forming, may be fused by the smoother process of organizing joint-stock companies. Whichever it is, the economic effect remains the same. Everywhere the increased scale of industrial establishments is the starting point for a more comprehensive organization of the collective work of many, for a wider development of their material motive forces. In other words, it is the starting point for the progressive transformation of isolated processes of production, carried on by customary methods, into processes of production that are socially combined and scientifically arranged.

<!-- ch25-p029 -->
But accumulation, the gradual increase of capital by reproduction as it passes from the circular to the spiral form, is clearly a very slow procedure compared with centralization, which has only to change the quantitative groupings of the parts of social capital. The world would still be without railways if it had had to wait until accumulation had brought a few individual capitals far enough to be adequate for building a railway. Centralization, on the contrary, accomplished this in the twinkling of an eye, by means of joint-stock companies. And while centralization thus intensifies and speeds up the effects of accumulation, it at the same time extends and speeds up those revolutions in the technical composition of capital that raise its constant part at the expense of its variable part, and so reduce the relative demand for labor.

<!-- ch25-p030 -->
The masses of capital fused together overnight by centralization reproduce and multiply as the others do, only faster, and so become new and powerful levers of social accumulation. So when we speak of the progress of social accumulation, we tacitly include – today – the effects of centralization.

<!-- ch25-p031 -->
The additional capitals formed in the normal course of accumulation (see Chapter XXIV, Section 1) serve especially as vehicles for exploiting new inventions and discoveries, and industrial improvements in general. But in time the old capital also reaches the moment of renewal from top to toe. It sheds its skin and is reborn, like the others, in a perfected technical form, in which a smaller quantity of labor is enough to set a larger quantity of machinery and raw materials in motion. This necessarily brings an absolute reduction in the demand for labor. Obviously, that reduction is all the greater, the more the capitals going through this process of renewal are already massed together by the movement of centralization.

<!-- ch25-p032 -->
On the one hand, therefore, the additional capital formed in the course of accumulation attracts fewer and fewer workers in proportion to its size. On the other hand, the old capital, periodically reproduced with a change of composition, repels more and more of the workers it formerly employed.
