## Chapter 10: The Working Day

### Section 1: The Limits of the Working Day

<!-- ch10-p001 -->
We started from the assumption that labor-power is bought and sold at its value. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. Value is what commodities, things produced in order to be exchanged and not for the producer's own use, have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) Like the value of all other commodities, the value of labor-power is determined by the working time necessary to produce it. If producing the worker's average daily means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) takes 6 hours, he must work, on average, 6 hours every day to produce his daily labor-power, or to reproduce the value he received from selling it. The necessary part of his working day (the total hours a worker works in a day) amounts to 6 hours, and so, other things being equal, it is a given quantity. But this does not yet give us the length of the working day itself.

<!-- ch10-p002 -->
Let us assume that the line A–––B represents the length of the necessary working time, say 6 hours. If the labor is extended 1, 3, or 6 hours beyond A––B, we get 3 other lines:

<!-- ch10-p003 -->
```text
Working day I.     Working day II.     Working day III.
  A–––B–C.           A–––B––C.           A–––B–––C.
```

<!-- ch10-p004 -->
These represent 3 different working days, of 7, 9, and 12 hours. The extension B––C of the line A––B represents the length of the surplus labor. (Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist. Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) Since the working day is A––B + B––C, or A––C, it varies with the variable quantity B––C. Since A––B is constant, the ratio of B––C to A––B can always be calculated. In working day I it is 1/6 of A––B, in working day II 3/6, and in working day III 6/6. Further, since the ratio (surplus working-time)/(necessary working-time) determines the rate of surplus-value, that rate is given by the ratio of B–-C to A–-B. In the 3 different working days it comes to 16 2/3, 50, and 100 percent respectively. On the other hand, the rate of surplus-value alone would not tell us the length of the working day. If this rate were, for example, 100 percent, the working day might be 8, 10, 12, or more hours long. The rate would show that the 2 parts that make up the working day, necessary labor-time and surplus labor-time, were equal in length, but not how long each of these two parts was.

<!-- ch10-p005 -->
So the working day is not a constant but a variable quantity. One of its parts, certainly, is determined by the working time required to reproduce the worker's own labor-power. But its total length varies with the duration of the surplus labor. The working day can therefore be determined, but in itself it is undetermined.[^1]

[^1]: “A day’s labour is vague, it may be long or short.” (“An Essay on Trade and Commerce, Containing Observations on Taxes, &c.” London. 1770, p. 73.)

<!-- ch10-p006 -->
Although the working day is not a fixed but a flowing quantity, it can, on the other hand, vary only within certain limits. Its minimum limit, however, cannot be determined. Of course, if we make the extension line B–-C, or the surplus labor, = 0, we get a minimum limit, that is, the part of the day which the worker must necessarily work to maintain himself. On the basis of capitalist production, however, this necessary labor can form only a part of the working day; the working day itself can never be cut down to this minimum. On the other hand, the working day has a maximum limit. It cannot be extended beyond a certain point. This maximum limit is set by two things. First, by the physical bounds of labor-power. Within the 24 hours of the natural day a man can spend only a definite amount of his vital force. In the same way, a horse can work, day after day, only 8 hours. During part of the day this force must rest and sleep; during another part the man has to satisfy other physical needs, to feed, wash, and clothe himself. Besides these purely physical limits, the extension of the working day runs into moral ones. The worker needs time to satisfy his intellectual and social wants, and the extent and number of these wants are conditioned by the general level of social progress. So the variation of the working day moves within physical and social bounds. But both of these limiting conditions are very elastic, and they leave the widest room. So we find working days of 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18 hours, that is, of the most varied lengths.

<!-- ch10-p007 -->
The capitalist has bought the labor-power at its day-rate. Its use-value (its usefulness: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties) belongs to him for one working day. He has thus acquired the right to make the worker work for him for one day. But what is a working day?[^2]

[^2]: This question is far more important than Sir Robert Peel's famous question to the Birmingham Chamber of Commerce: What is a pound? Peel could only have asked that question because he was as much in the dark about the nature of money as the “little shilling men” of Birmingham.

<!-- ch10-p008 -->
At any rate, it is less than a natural day. By how much? The capitalist has his own views about this *ultima Thule* [farthest limit], the necessary limit of the working day. As capitalist, he is only capital personified (capital being value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger). His soul is the soul of capital. But capital has one single life impulse: the tendency to create value and surplus-value, to make its constant factor, the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things), soak up the greatest possible amount of surplus labor.[^3]

[^3]: “It is the aim of the capitalist to obtain with his expended capital the greatest possible quantity of labour (d’obtenir du capital dépense la plus forte somme de travail possible).” J. G. Courcelle-Seneuil. “Traité théorique et pratique des entreprises industrielles.” 2nd ed. Paris, 1857, p. 63.

<!-- ch10-p009 -->
Capital is dead labor that, like a vampire, lives only by sucking living labor, and lives the more, the more labor it sucks. (Dead labor is labor already embodied in means of production; living labor is the labor of workers now at work.) The time during which the worker works is the time during which the capitalist consumes the labor-power he has bought from him.[^4]

[^4]: “An hour’s labour lost in a day is a prodigious injury to a commercial State.... There is a very great consumption of luxuries among the labouring poor of this kingdom: particularly among the manufacturing populace, by which they also consume their time, the most fatal of consumptions.” “An Essay on Trade and Commerce, &c.,” p. 47, and 15

<!-- ch10-p010 -->
If the worker consumes his disposable time for himself, he robs the capitalist.[^5]

[^5]: “Si le manouvrier libre prend un instant de repos, l’économie sordide qui le suit des yeux avec inquiétude, prétend qu’il la vole.” [If the free laborer takes a moment's rest, the stingy management that anxiously follows him with its eyes claims that he is stealing from it.] N. Linguet, “Théorie des Lois Civiles. &c.” London, 1767, t. II., p. 466.

<!-- ch10-p011 -->
The capitalist then takes his stand on the law of the exchange of commodities. Like every other buyer, he tries to get the greatest possible benefit out of the use-value of his commodity. Suddenly the voice of the worker, which had been drowned out in the storm and stress of the process of production, rises:

<!-- ch10-p012 -->
The commodity that I have sold to you differs from the crowd of other commodities in that its use creates value, and a value greater than its own. That is why you bought it. What appears on your side as a spontaneous expansion of capital is, on mine, an extra spending of labor-power. You and I know only one law in the market, the law of the exchange of commodities. And the consumption of a commodity belongs not to the seller who parts with it, but to the buyer who acquires it. So the use of my daily labor-power belongs to you. But with the price that you pay for it each day, I must be able to reproduce it daily and to sell it again. Apart from natural wear through age, etc., I must be able to work tomorrow with the same normal amount of strength, health, and freshness as today. You constantly preach to me the gospel of “saving” and “abstinence.” Good! Like a sensible, saving owner, I will take good care of my only wealth, labor-power, and abstain from all foolish waste of it. Each day I will spend, set in motion, and put into action only as much of it as is compatible with its normal duration and healthy development. By extending the working day without limit, you may use up in one day a quantity of labor-power greater than I can restore in three. What you gain in labor, I lose in substance. Using my labor-power and plundering it are quite different things. If the average time that an average worker can live (doing a reasonable amount of work) is 30 years, the value of my labor-power that you pay me from day to day is 1/(365×30) or 1/10950 of its total value. But if you consume it in 10 years, you pay me daily 1/10950 instead of 1/3650 of its total value, that is, only 1/3 of its daily value. So you rob me every day of 2/3 of the value of my commodity. You pay me for one day's labor-power, while you use up that of 3 days. That is against our contract and against the law of exchange. I therefore demand a working day of normal length, and I demand it without any appeal to your heart, for in money matters sentiment has no place. You may be a model citizen, perhaps a member of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, and with a name for holiness to boot; but the thing that you represent when you face me has no heart in its breast. What seems to beat there is my own heartbeat. I demand the normal working day because, like every other seller, I demand the value of my commodity.[^6]

[^6]: During the great strike of the London builders in 1860-61, for cutting the working day to 9 hours, their Committee published a manifesto that contained, to some extent, the plea of our worker. The manifesto hints, not without irony, that the greatest profit-monger among the building employers, a certain Sir M. Peto, had a name for holiness. (This same Peto, after 1867, came to an end in Strousberg's style.)

<!-- ch10-p013 -->
So we see that, apart from extremely elastic bounds, the nature of the exchange of commodities itself sets no limit to the working day, no limit to surplus labor. The capitalist asserts his right as a buyer when he tries to make the working day as long as possible, and, whenever possible, to make two working days out of one. On the other hand, the special nature of the commodity sold implies a limit to its consumption by the buyer, and the worker asserts his right as a seller when he wants to reduce the working day to one of definite normal length. So there is an antinomy here, a clash of right against right, both equally bearing the seal of the law of exchange. Between equal rights, force decides. That is why, in the history of capitalist production, the fixing of what a working day is presents itself as the result of a struggle, a struggle between collective capital, that is, the class of capitalists, and collective labor, that is, the working class.
