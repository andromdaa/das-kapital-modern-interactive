### Section 3: Branches of English Industry Without Legal Limits to Exploitation

<!-- ch10-p040 -->
So far we have looked at the tendency to lengthen the working day, the werewolf’s hunger for surplus labor, in one department of industry. There the monstrous demands made on the workers were so extreme that, as an English bourgeois economist (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it) says, not even the cruelties of the Spaniards to the Native Americans surpassed them.[^31] In the end, these demands caused capital to be bound by the chains of legal regulation. Now let us glance at certain branches of production in which the exploitation of labor is still free of fetters today, or was free of them until yesterday.

[^31]: “The cupidity of mill-owners whose cruelties in the pursuit of gain have hardly been exceeded by those perpetrated by the Spaniards on the conquest of America in the pursuit of gold.” John Wade, “History of the Middle and Working Classes,” 3rd Ed. London, 1835, p. 114. The theoretical part of this book, a kind of handbook of political economy, is original in some parts, for example on trade crises, considering the time when it was published. The historical part is, to a great extent, a shameless plagiarism of Sir F. M. Eden’s “The State of the Poor,” London, 1797.

> Mr. Broughton Charlton, a county magistrate, was chairman of a meeting held at the Assembly Rooms, Nottingham, on the 14th January, 1860, and in that role he declared “that there was an amount of privation and suffering among that portion of the population connected with the lace trade, unknown in other parts of the kingdom, indeed, in the civilised world .... Children of nine or ten years are dragged from their squalid beds at two, three, or four o’clock in the morning and compelled to work for a bare subsistence until ten, eleven, or twelve at night, their limbs wearing away, their frames dwindling, their faces whitening, and their humanity absolutely sinking into a stone-like torpor, utterly horrible to contemplate.... We are not surprised that Mr. Mallett, or any other manufacturer, should stand forward and protest against discussion.... The system, as the Rev. Montagu Valpy describes it, is one of unmitigated slavery, socially, physically, morally, and spiritually.... What can be thought of a town which holds a public meeting to petition that the period of labour for men shall be diminished to eighteen hours a day? .... We declaim against the Virginian and Carolinian cotton-planters. Is their black-market, their lash, and their barter of human flesh more detestable than this slow sacrifice of humanity which takes place in order that veils and collars may be fabricated for the benefit of capitalists?”[^32]

[^32]: *Daily Telegraph, 17th* January, 1860.

Over the last 22 years, the potteries of Staffordshire have been the subject of three parliamentary inquiries. Their results are set down in three reports. The first is Mr. Scriven’s Report of 1841 to the “Children’s Employment Commissioners.” The second is the report of Dr. Greenhow of 1860, published by order of the medical officer of the Privy Council (Public Health, 3rd Report, 112-113). Lastly, in the “First Report of the Children’s Employment Commission, of the 13th June, 1863,” there is the report of Mr. Longe of 1862. For my purpose it is enough to take some statements of the exploited children themselves from the reports of 1860 and 1863. From the children we may form an opinion about the adults, especially the girls and women. And this is in a branch of industry next to which cotton-spinning looks like a pleasant and healthy occupation.[^33]

[^33]: Compare F. Engels “Lage, etc.” pp. 249-51.

William Wood, 9 years old, was 7 years and 10 months old when he began to work. From the beginning he “ran moulds” (carried the newly molded articles into the drying-room, and afterwards brought back the empty mold). He came to work every day of the week at 6 a.m., and stopped about 9 p.m. “I work till 9 o’clock at night six days in the week. I have done so seven or eight weeks.”

<!-- ch10-p044 -->
> Fifteen hours of labor for a child 7 years old! J. Murray, 12 years of age, says: “I turn jigger, and run moulds. I come at 6. Sometimes I come at 4. I worked all night last night, till 6 o’clock this morning. I have not been in bed since the night before last. There were eight or nine other boys working last night. All but one have come this morning. I get 3 shillings and sixpence. I do not get any more for working at night. I worked two nights last week.”

Dr. Greenhow states that the average length of life in the pottery districts of Stoke-on-Trent and Wolstanton is extraordinarily short. In the district of Stoke, only 36.6% of the adult male population above 20 is employed in the potteries, and in Wolstanton only 30.4%. Yet among the men of that age, more than half of all deaths in the first district, and nearly 2/5 in the second, are the result of lung diseases among the potters. Dr. Boothroyd, a medical practitioner at Hanley, says:

From the report of the Commissioners in 1863, we take the following. Dr. J. T. Arledge, senior physician of the North Staffordshire Infirmary, says:

<!-- ch10-p053 -->
> “The potters as a class, both men and women, represent a degenerated population, both physically and morally. That the ‘degenerescence’ of the population of this district is not even greater than it is, is due to the constant recruiting from the adjacent country, and intermarriages with more healthy races.”[^36]

The manufacture of lucifer matches (early friction matches) dates from 1833, when a method was discovered for putting phosphorus on the match itself. Since 1845 this manufacture has developed rapidly in England. It has spread especially in the thickly populated parts of London, and also in Manchester, Birmingham, Liverpool, Bristol, Norwich, Newcastle and Glasgow. Along with it has spread the form of lockjaw that a Vienna physician discovered in 1845 to be a disease peculiar to lucifer-match makers. Half the workers are children under thirteen and young persons under eighteen. Because it is so unhealthy and unpleasant, the manufacture has such a bad name that only the most miserable part of the working class, half-starved widows and the like, hand their children over to it, “the ragged, half-starved, untaught children.”[^39]

[^39]: cited above, p. liv.

<!-- ch10-p058 -->
Of the witnesses that Commissioner White examined (1863), 270 were under 18, 50 under 10, 10 only 8, and 5 only 6 years old. The working day ranged from 12 to 14 or 15 hours. There was night-labor, and meal-times were irregular. Meals were mostly eaten in the very workrooms that are poisoned with phosphorus. Dante would have found the worst horrors of his Inferno surpassed in this manufacture.

<!-- ch10-p059 -->
In the manufacture of paper-hangings (wallpaper), the coarser kinds are printed by machine and the finer ones by hand (block-printing). The busiest months are from the beginning of October to the end of April. During this time the work goes on fast and furious, without a break, from 6 a.m. to 10 p.m. or further into the night.

<!-- ch10-p060 -->
J. Leach states in evidence:

<!-- ch10-p061 -->
> “Last winter six out of nineteen girls were away from ill-health at one time from over-work. I have to bawl at them to keep them awake.” W. Duffy: “I have seen when the children could none of them keep their eyes open for the work; indeed, none of us could.” J. Lightbourne: “Am 13 ... We worked last winter till 9 (evening), and the winter before till 10. I used to cry with sore feet every night last winter.” G. Apsden: “That boy of mine when he was 7 years old I used to carry him on my back to and fro through the snow, and he used to have 16 hours a day ... I have often knelt down to feed him as he stood by the machine, for he could not leave it or stop.” Smith, the managing partner of a Manchester factory: “We (he means his “hands” who work for “us”) work on with no stoppage for meals, so that day’s work of 10½ hours is finished by 4.30 p.m., and all after that is over-time.”[^40] (Does this Mr. Smith take no meals himself during 10½ hours?) “We (this same Smith) seldom leave off working before 6 p.m. (he means leave off the consumption of “our” labor-power machines), so that we (iterum Crispinus [Crispinus again]) are really working over-time the whole year round. For all these, children and adults alike (152 children and young persons and 140 adults), the average work for the last 18 months has been at the very least 7 days, 5 hours, or 78 1/2 hours a week. For the six weeks ending May 2nd this year (1862), the average was higher – 8 days or 84 hours a week.”

[^40]: This is not to be taken in the same sense as our surplus labor-time. These gentlemen consider 10½ hours of labor as the normal working day, which of course includes the normal surplus labor. After this begins “overtime,” which is paid a little better. We will see later that the labor spent during the so-called normal day is paid below its value. So the overtime is simply a capitalist trick to extort more surplus labor. It would still be that, even if the labor-power spent during the normal working day were properly paid.

<!-- ch10-p062 -->
This same Mr. Smith, who is so extremely devoted to the *pluralis majestatis* [the Royal “we,” i.e., speaking on behalf of his subjects], adds with a smile, “Machine-work is not great.” In the same way, the employers in block-printing say: “Hand labour is more healthy than machine work.” On the whole, the manufacturers declare themselves indignantly against the proposal “to stop the machines at least during meal-times.”

<!-- ch10-p063 -->
> “A clause,” says Mr. Otley, manager of a wall-paper factory in the Borough (Southwark, in London), “which allowed work between, say 6 a.m. and 9 p.m. would suit us (!) very well, but the factory hours, 6 a.m. to 6 p.m., are not suitable. Our machine is always stopped for dinner. (What generosity!) There is no waste of paper and colour to speak of. But,” he adds sympathetically, “I can understand the loss of time not being liked.”

<!-- ch10-p064 -->
The report of the Commission naively gives its opinion on the fear of some “leading firms” of losing time (that is, the time for taking possession of the labor of others) and so losing profit. This fear, it says, is not a good enough reason for letting children under 13, and young persons under 18, who work 12 to 16 hours a day, lose their dinner. Nor is it a good enough reason for giving them their dinner the way coal and water are supplied to the steam-engine, soap to wool, oil to the wheel – as mere auxiliary material for the instruments of labor, during the process of production itself.[^41]

[^41]: cited above, Evidence, pp. 123, 124, 125, 140, and 54.

<!-- ch10-p065 -->
No branch of industry in England has kept, up to the present day, a method of production as archaic as baking. (We leave aside the making of bread by machinery, which was introduced recently.) As we see from the poets of the Roman Empire, it is a pre-Christian method. But capital, as was said earlier, is at first indifferent to the technical character of the labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things). It begins by taking that process just as it finds it.

<!-- ch10-p066 -->
The incredible adulteration of bread, especially in London, was first exposed by the House of Commons Committee “on the adulteration of articles of food” (1855-56), and by Dr. Hassall’s work, “Adulterations detected.”[^42] These revelations led to the Act of August 6th, 1860, “for preventing the adulteration of articles of food and drink.” It was a law that did not work, since it naturally shows the tenderest consideration for every Free-trader who resolves, by buying or selling adulterated commodities, “to turn an honest penny.”[^43] The Committee itself stated, more or less naively, its conviction that Free-trade essentially meant trade in adulterated goods, or, as the English ingeniously put it, “sophisticated” goods. In fact, this kind of sophistry knows better than Protagoras how to make white black and black white. It knows better than the Eleatic philosophers how to demonstrate *ad oculos* [before your own eyes] that everything is only appearance.[^44]

[^42]: Alum, finely powdered or mixed with salt, is a normal article of trade, bearing the significant name of “bakers’ stuff.”

[^43]: Soot is a well-known and very energetic form of carbon. It makes a manure that capitalistic chimney-sweeps sell to English farmers. Now in 1862, in a lawsuit, a British jury had to decide this question: is soot that, without the buyer’s knowledge, has 90% of dust and sand mixed into it genuine soot in the commercial sense, or adulterated soot in the legal sense? The “amis du commerce” [friends of commerce] decided it was genuine commercial soot. They threw out the case of the plaintiff, a farmer, who in addition had to pay the costs of the suit.

[^44]: The French chemist Chevallier wrote a treatise on the “sophistications” of commodities. He reviews 600 or more articles, and for many of them he lists 10, 20, 30 different methods of adulteration. He adds that he does not know all the methods, and does not mention all that he knows. He gives 6 kinds of adulteration of sugar, 9 of olive oil, 10 of butter, 12 of salt, 19 of milk, 20 of bread, 23 of brandy, 24 of meal, 28 of chocolate, 30 of wine, 32 of coffee, etc. Even God Almighty does not escape this fate. See Rouard de Card, “On the Falsifications of the materials of the Sacrament.” (“De la falsification des substances sacramentelles,” Paris, 1856.)

At any rate, the Committee had turned the public’s attention to its “daily bread,” and so to the baking trade. At the same time, the London journeymen bakers raised their cry against their over-work, etc., in public meetings and in petitions to Parliament. The cry was so urgent that Mr. H. S. Tremenheere was appointed Royal Commissioner of Inquiry. He was also a member of the Commission of 1863 that has been mentioned several times. His report,[^45] together with the evidence given, stirred not the heart of the public but its stomach. Englishmen, always well up in the Bible, knew well enough that man is commanded to eat his bread in the sweat of his brow, unless, by the grace of being chosen, he is a capitalist, or a landlord, or the holder of a paid office with no duties. But they did not know that every day, in his bread, he had to eat a certain amount of human sweat mixed with pus from abscesses, cobwebs, dead black-beetles, and rotten German yeast, not counting alum, sand, and other agreeable mineral ingredients. So, without any regard for his holiness, Free-trade, the free baking-trade was placed under the supervision of State inspectors (at the close of the Parliamentary session of 1863). By the same Act of Parliament, work from 9 in the evening to 5 in the morning was forbidden for journeymen bakers under 18. This last clause speaks volumes about the over-work in this old-fashioned, homely line of business.

[^45]: “Report, &c., relative to the grievances complained of by the journeymen bakers, &c., London, 1862,” and “Second Report, &c., London, 1863.”

<!-- ch10-p068 -->
> “The work of a London journeyman baker begins, as a rule, at about eleven at night. At that hour he ‘makes the dough,’ – a laborious process, which lasts from half an hour to three quarters of an hour, according to the size of the batch or the labour bestowed upon it. He then lies down upon the kneading-board, which is also the covering of the trough in which the dough is ‘made’; and with a sack under him, and another rolled up as a pillow, he sleeps for about a couple of hours. He is then engaged in a rapid and continuous labour for about five hours – throwing out the dough, ‘scaling it off,’ moulding it, putting it into the oven, preparing and baking rolls and fancy bread, taking the batch bread out of the oven, and up into the shop, &c., &c. The temperature of a bakehouse ranges from about 75 to upwards of 90 degrees, and in the smaller bakehouses approximates usually to the higher rather than to the lower degree of heat. When the business of making the bread, rolls, &c., is over, that of its distribution begins, and a considerable proportion of the journeymen in the trade, after working hard in the manner described during the night, are upon their legs for many hours during the day, carrying baskets, or wheeling hand-carts, and sometimes again in the bakehouse, leaving off work at various hours between 1 and 6 p.m. according to the season of the year, or the amount and nature of their master’s business; while others are again engaged in the bakehouse in ‘bringing out’ more batches until late in the afternoon.[^46]... During what is called ‘the London season,’ the operatives belonging to the ‘full-priced’ bakers at the West End of the town, generally begin work at 11 p.m., and are engaged in making the bread, with one or two short (sometimes very short) intervals of rest, up to 8 o’clock the next morning. They are then engaged all day long, up to 4, 5, 6, and as late as 7 o’clock in the evening carrying out bread, or sometimes in the afternoon in the bakehouse again, assisting in the biscuit-baking. They may have, after they have done their work, sometimes five or six, sometimes only four or five hours’ sleep before they begin again. On Fridays they always begin sooner, some about ten o’clock, and continue in some cases, at work, either in making or delivering the bread up to 8 p.m. on Saturday night, but more generally up to 4 or 5 o’clock, Sunday morning. On Sundays the men must attend twice or three times during the day for an hour or two to make preparations for the next day’s bread.... The men employed by the underselling masters (who sell their bread under the ‘full price,’ and who, as already pointed out, comprise three-fourths of the London bakers) have not only to work on the average longer hours, but their work is almost entirely confined to the bakehouse. The underselling masters generally sell their bread... in the shop. If they send it out, which is not common, except as supplying chandlers’ shops, they usually employ other hands for that purpose. It is not their practice to deliver bread from house to house. Towards the end of the week ... the men begin on Thursday night at 10 o’clock, and continue on with only slight intermission until late on Saturday evening.”[^47]

The adulteration of bread, and the formation of a class of bakers who sell bread below the full price, date from the beginning of the 18th century. That was when the trade lost its corporate character (its organization as a guild), and the capitalist, in the form of the miller or flour-factor (flour merchant), rose up behind the nominal master baker.[^50] This laid the foundation of capitalist production in this trade, and of the unlimited lengthening of the working day and of night-labor, although night-labor gained a serious footing, even in London, only after 1824.[^51]

[^50]: George Read, cited above. At the end of the 17th and the beginning of the 18th centuries, the factors (agents) who crowded into every possible trade were still denounced as “public nuisances.” Thus the Grand Jury at the quarter session of the Justices of the Peace for the County of Somerset addressed a presentment (a formal complaint) to the Lower House. Among other things, it states “that these factors of Blackwell Hall are a Public Nuisance and Prejudice to the Clothing Trade, and ought to be put down as a Nuisance.” “The Case of our English Wool., &c.,” London, 1685, pp. 6, 7.

After what has just been said, it will be understood that the Report of the Commission classes journeymen bakers among the short-lived workers. Having by good luck escaped the normal decimation of the children of the working class, they rarely reach the age of 42. Nevertheless, the baking trade is always flooded with applicants. London gets its supply of these labor-powers from Scotland, from the western agricultural districts of England, and from Germany.

<!-- ch10-p073 -->
In the years 1858-60, the journeymen bakers in Ireland organized great meetings at their own expense to agitate against night work and Sunday work. The public took their side with Irish warmth, for example at the Dublin meeting in May, 1860. As a result of this movement, day-labor alone was successfully established in Wexford, Kilkenny, Clonmel, Waterford, etc.

<!-- ch10-p074 -->
> “In Limerick, where the grievances of the journeymen are demonstrated to be excessive, the movement has been defeated by the opposition of the master bakers, the miller bakers being the greatest opponents. The example of Limerick led to a retrogression in Ennis and Tipperary. In Cork, where the strongest possible demonstration of feeling took place, the masters, by exercising their power of turning the men out of employment, have defeated the movement. In Dublin, the master bakers have offered the most determined opposition to the movement, and by discountenancing as much as possible the journeymen promoting it, have succeeded in leading the men into acquiescence in Sunday work and night-work, contrary to the convictions of the men.”[^52]

[^52]: Report of Committee on the Baking Trade in Ireland for 1861.

The Committee of the English Government (a Government that, in Ireland, is armed to the teeth and generally knows how to show it) protests in mild, though funereal, tones to the implacable master bakers of Dublin, Limerick, Cork, etc.:

<!-- ch10-p076 -->
> “The Committee believe that the hours of labour are limited by natural laws, which cannot be violated with impunity. That for master bakers to induce their workmen, by the fear of losing employment, to violate their religious convictions and their better feelings, to disobey the laws of the land, and to disregard public opinion (this all refers to Sunday labor), is calculated to provoke ill-feeling between workmen and masters, ... and affords an example dangerous to religion, morality, and social order.... The Committee believe that any constant work beyond 12 hours a-day encroaches on the domestic and private life of the working-man, and so leads to disastrous moral results, interfering with each man’s home, and the discharge of his family duties as a son, a brother, a husband, a father. <!-- ch10-p077 -->
So much for Ireland. On the other side of the channel, in Scotland, the agricultural worker, the plowman, protests against his 13-14 hours’ work in the harshest climate, with 4 hours’ extra work on Sunday (in this land of strict Sabbath-keepers!).[^54] Meanwhile, at the same time, three railway men are standing before a London coroner’s jury: a guard, an engine-driver, and a signalman. A tremendous railway accident has hurried hundreds of passengers into another world. The negligence of the employee is the cause of the disaster. They declare with one voice before the jury that ten or twelve years earlier their labor lasted only eight hours a day. During the last five or six years it had been screwed up to 14, 18, and 20 hours. Under especially heavy pressure from holiday-makers, at times of excursion trains, it often lasted 40 or 50 hours without a break. They were ordinary men, not Cyclops. At a certain point their labor-power failed. A stupor seized them. Their brains stopped thinking, and their eyes stopped seeing. The thoroughly “respectable” British jurymen answered with a verdict that sent them for trial at the next assizes (the periodic criminal courts) on a charge of manslaughter. In a gentle “rider” added to their verdict, the jurymen expressed the pious hope that in future the capitalist magnates of the railways would be more extravagant in buying a sufficient quantity of labor-power, and more “abstemious,” more “self-denying,” more “thrifty,” in draining the labor-power they paid for.[^55]

In one of the most oppressed agricultural districts of England, Buckinghamshire, the workers made a great strike in March, 1867, to raise their weekly wage from 9-10 shillings to 12 shillings. A motley crowd of workers of all trades, ages, and sexes presses on us, more eagerly than the souls of the slain pressed on Ulysses. Without looking at the Blue books under their arms, we see at a glance the mark of over-work on them. From this crowd let us take two more figures, whose striking contrast proves that before capital all men are alike: a milliner and a blacksmith.

<!-- ch10-p079 -->
In the last week of June, 1863, all the London daily papers published a paragraph with the “sensational” heading, “Death from simple over-work.” It dealt with the death of the milliner Mary Anne Walkley, 20 years of age. She was employed in a highly respectable dressmaking establishment, exploited by a lady with the pleasant name of Elise. The old, often-told story[^56] was told once more. This girl worked, on average, 16½ hours, and during the season often 30 hours without a break, while her failing labor-power was revived now and then with sherry, port, or coffee. It was just then the height of the season. Gorgeous dresses had to be conjured up in the twinkling of an eye for the noble ladies invited to the ball in honor of the newly imported Princess of Wales. Mary Anne Walkley had worked without a break for 26½ hours, together with 60 other girls, 30 in one room, which provided only 1/3 of the cubic feet of air they needed. At night they slept in pairs in one of the stifling holes into which the bedroom was divided by board partitions.[^57] And this was one of the best millinery establishments in London. Mary Anne Walkley fell ill on the Friday and died on the Sunday, without, to the astonishment of Madame Elise, having first finished the work in hand.
