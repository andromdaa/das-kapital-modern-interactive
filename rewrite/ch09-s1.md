## Chapter 9: The Rate of Surplus-Value

### Section 1: The Degree of Exploitation of Labor-Power

<!-- ch09-p001 -->
The surplus-value (the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it) that is generated in the process of production by C, the capital advanced (capital: value that is used to make more value, money that is advanced in order to come back larger), is, in other words, the self-expansion of the value of the capital C. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) In the first place, this surplus-value presents itself to us as a surplus: the amount by which the value of the product is greater than the value of the elements that make it up.

<!-- ch09-p002 -->
The capital C is made up of two parts. One is the sum of money c laid out on the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things). The other is the sum of money v spent on the labor-power (a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything; it is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist). c stands for the part that has become constant capital, and v for the part that has become variable capital. (Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production; its value is passed on to the product but does not grow. Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power; it reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies.) At first, then, C = c + v. For example, if £500 is the capital advanced, its parts may be such that the £500 = £410 const. + £90 var. When the process of production is finished, we get a commodity (a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use) whose value = (c + v) + s, where s is the surplus-value. Or, using our earlier figures, the value of this commodity may be (£410 const. + £90 var.) + £90 surpl. The original capital has now changed from C to C', from £500 to £590. The difference is s, or a surplus-value of £90. The value of the elements that make up the product is equal to the value of the capital advanced. So it is a mere tautology, saying the same thing twice, to say that the excess of the value of the product over the value of its elements is equal to the expansion of the capital advanced, or to the surplus-value produced.

<!-- ch09-p003 -->
Even so, we must look at this tautology a little more closely. The two things compared are the value of the product and the value of its elements used up in the process of production. Now, we have seen how the part of the constant capital that consists of the instruments of labor passes on only a fraction of its value to what is being produced, while the rest of that value stays in those instruments. Since this rest plays no part in forming value, we may leave it aside for now. Bringing it into the calculation would make no difference. For instance, take our earlier example, c = £410. Suppose this sum consists of £312 value of raw material, £44 value of auxiliary material, and £54 value of the machinery worn away in the process. And suppose that the total value of the machinery employed is £1,054. Out of this last sum, then, we count as advanced for turning out the product only the £54 that the machinery loses by wear and tear in the process, because this is all it passes on to the product. Now if we also count the remaining £1,000, which still stays in the machinery, as transferred to the product, we ought also to count it as part of the value advanced, and so make it appear on both sides of our calculation.[^1] In this way we would get £1,500 on one side and £1,590 on the other. The difference between these two sums, or the surplus-value, would still be £90. Throughout this Book, therefore, by constant capital advanced for the production of value we always mean the value of the means of production actually used up in the process, and that value alone, unless the context rules this out.

[^1]: “If we reckon the value of the fixed capital employed as a part of the advances, we must reckon the remaining value of such capital at the end of the year as a part of the annual returns.” (Malthus, “Princ. of Pol. Econ.” 2nd. ed., London, 1836, p. 269.)

<!-- ch09-p004 -->
With this settled, let us return to the formula C = c + v, which, as we saw, was transformed into C' = (c + v) + s, C becoming C'. We know that the value of the constant capital is transferred to the product and merely reappears in it. So the new value actually created in the process, the value produced, or value-product, is not the same as the value of the product. It is not, as it would seem at first sight, (c + v) + s, or £410 const. + £90 var. + £90 surpl. It is v + s, or £90 var. + £90 surpl.: not £590 but £180. Suppose c = 0. In other words, suppose there were branches of industry in which the capitalist could do without all means of production made by previous labor, whether raw material, auxiliary material, or instruments of labor, and employed only labor-power and materials supplied by Nature. In that case there would be no constant capital to transfer to the product. This part of the value of the product, that is, the £410 in our example, would disappear. But the sum of £180, the amount of new value created, or the value produced, which contains £90 of surplus-value, would stay just as large as if c stood for the highest value imaginable. We would have C = (0 + v) = v, or C', the expanded capital, = v + s, and therefore C' - C = s, as before. On the other hand, suppose s = 0. In other words, suppose the labor-power, whose value is advanced in the form of variable capital, produced only its equivalent. Then we would have C = c + v, or C', the value of the product, = (c + v) + 0, or C = C'. In this case the capital advanced would not have expanded its value.

<!-- ch09-p005 -->
From what has gone before, we know that surplus-value is purely the result of a change in the value of v, the part of the capital that is turned into labor-power. Consequently, v + s = v + v', or v plus an increase of v. But the fact that it is v alone that varies, and the conditions of that variation, are hidden by a circumstance: because the variable part of the capital increases, the total of the capital advanced increases too. It was originally £500 and becomes £590. So, for our investigation to lead to accurate results, we must leave out of account the part of the value of the product in which only constant capital appears. That is, we must set the constant capital equal to zero, or make c = 0. This is merely an application of a mathematical rule, used whenever we work with constant and variable magnitudes that are related to each other only by the signs of addition and subtraction.

<!-- ch09-p006 -->
A further difficulty comes from the original form of the variable capital. In our example, C' = £410 const. + £90 var. + £90 surpl. But £90 is a given, and therefore a constant, quantity, so it seems absurd to treat it as variable. In fact, though, the term £90 var. is here merely a symbol to show that this value goes through a process. The part of the capital invested in buying labor-power is a definite quantity of materialized labor, a constant value, like the value of the labor-power bought. But in the process of production the place of the £90 is taken by labor-power in action: dead labor is replaced by living labor, something stagnant by something flowing, a constant by a variable. The result is the reproduction of v plus an increase of v. From the standpoint of capitalist production, then, the whole process appears as the spontaneous change of the originally constant value that is turned into labor-power. Both the process and its result appear to be due to this value. So if expressions such as “£90 variable capital” or “so much self-expanding value” seem contradictory, this is only because they bring to the surface a contradiction built into capitalist production.

<!-- ch09-p007 -->
At first sight it seems a strange thing to do, to set the constant capital equal to zero. Yet we do it every day. If, for example, we want to calculate the amount of England's profits from the cotton industry, we first of all deduct the sums paid for cotton to the United States, India, Egypt and other countries. In other words, the value of the capital that merely reappears in the value of the product is put = 0.

<!-- ch09-p008 -->
Of course, it is economically of very great importance to know the ratio of surplus-value not only to the part of the capital from which it directly springs, and whose change in value it represents, but also to the total capital advanced. We shall therefore treat this ratio exhaustively in the third book. For one part of a capital to expand its value by being turned into labor-power, another part must be turned into means of production. For variable capital to perform its function, constant capital must be advanced in the proper proportion, a proportion given by the special technical conditions of each labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things). But the fact that retorts (glass vessels for distilling) and other vessels are necessary to a chemical process does not compel the chemist to take notice of them in the result of his analysis. If we look at the means of production in their relation to the creation of value and to the change in the quantity of value, apart from anything else, they appear simply as the material in which labor-power, the value-creator, embodies itself. Neither the nature nor the value of this material is of any importance. The only requirement is that there be a sufficient supply of it to absorb the labor spent in the process of production. Once that supply is given, the material may rise or fall in value, or even, like land and the sea, have no value in itself; but this will have no influence on the creation of value or on the change in the quantity of value.[^2]

[^2]: What Lucretius says is self-evident: “nil posse creari de nihilo,” out of nothing, nothing can be created. Creating value is turning labor-power into labor. Labor-power itself is energy transferred to a human organism by means of nourishing matter.

<!-- ch09-p009 -->
So, in the first place, we set the constant capital equal to zero. The capital advanced is therefore reduced from c + v to v, and instead of the value of the product (c + v) + s we now have the value produced (v + s). Given that the new value produced = £180, this sum therefore represents the whole labor spent during the process. Subtracting from it £90, the value of the variable capital, we have £90 left, the amount of the surplus-value. This sum of £90, or s, expresses the absolute quantity of surplus-value produced. The relative quantity produced, or the percentage increase of the variable capital, is clearly determined by the ratio of the surplus-value to the variable capital, or is expressed by s/v. In our example this ratio is 90/90, which gives an increase of 100%. This relative increase in the value of the variable capital, or the relative magnitude of the surplus-value, I call “The rate of surplus-value.”[^3]

[^3]: In the same way as the English use the terms “rate of profit” and “rate of interest.” We shall see in Book III that the rate of profit is no mystery as soon as we know the laws of surplus-value. If we reverse the process, we can understand neither the one nor the other.

<!-- ch09-p010 -->
We have seen that during one part of the labor process the worker produces only the value of his labor-power, that is, the value of his means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live). Now, his work forms part of a system based on the social division of labor. So he does not directly produce the actual necessities that he himself consumes. Instead he produces a particular commodity, yarn for example, whose value is equal to the value of those necessities, or of the money with which they can be bought. The part of his day's labor spent on this will be greater or smaller in proportion to the value of the necessities he requires daily on average, or, what comes to the same thing, in proportion to the labor-time required on average to produce them. If the value of those necessities represents on average the expenditure of six hours' labor, the worker must on average work for six hours to produce that value. If, instead of working for the capitalist, he worked independently on his own account, he would, other things being equal, still have to labor for the same number of hours to produce the value of his labor-power, and so to gain the means of subsistence necessary for his maintenance or continued reproduction. But, as we have seen, during the part of his day's labor in which he produces the value of his labor-power, say three shillings, he produces only an equivalent for the value of his labor-power already advanced[^4] by the capitalist; the new value created only replaces the variable capital advanced. Because of this fact, the production of the new value of three shillings takes on the appearance of a mere reproduction. So the part of the working day during which this reproduction takes place I call “*necessary*” labor-time, and the labor spent during that time I call “*necessary*” labor.[^5] It is necessary as regards the worker, because it does not depend on the particular social form of his labor. It is necessary as regards capital and the world of capitalists, because their existence also depends on the continued existence of the worker.

[^4]: *Note added in the 3rd German edition.* — The author is using the economic language in current use here. It will be remembered that on p. 182 (present edition, p. 174) it was shown that in reality the worker “advances” to the capitalist, and not the capitalist to the worker. — *F. E.*

[^5]: In this work we have so far used the term “necessary labor-time” to mean the time necessary under given social conditions for producing any commodity. From now on we also use it to mean the time necessary for producing the particular commodity labor-power. Using one and the same technical term in different senses is inconvenient, but in no science can it be altogether avoided. Compare, for instance, the higher with the lower branches of mathematics.

<!-- ch09-p011 -->
During the second period of the labor process, the one in which his labor is no longer necessary labor, the worker does labor, it is true, and does spend labor-power. But since his labor is no longer necessary labor, he creates no value for himself. He creates surplus-value, which, for the capitalist, has all the charms of a creation out of nothing. This part of the working day I name surplus labor-time, and to the labor spent during that time I give the name of surplus labor. To understand surplus-value correctly, it is every bit as important to see it as merely surplus labor-time in congealed form, as nothing but materialized surplus labor, as it is, to understand value properly, to see value as merely so many hours of labor in congealed form, as nothing but materialized labor. The essential difference between the various economic forms of society, for instance between a society based on slave labor and one based on wage labor, lies only in the way in which this surplus labor is in each case extracted from the actual producer, the worker.[^6]

[^6]: Herr Wilhelm Thucydides Roscher has found a mare's nest (a great discovery that turns out to be nothing). He has made the important discovery that if, on the one hand, the formation of surplus-value, or surplus-produce, and the accumulation of capital that follows from it (accumulation of capital: turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows) are nowadays due to the thrift of the capitalist, on the other hand, in the lowest stages of civilization, it is the strong who compel the weak to economize. (cited above, p. 78.) To economize what? Labor? Or superfluous wealth that does not exist? What is it that makes men like Roscher account for the origin of surplus-value by a mere reheating of the more or less plausible excuses the capitalist gives for appropriating surplus-value? It is, besides their real ignorance, their dread, as apologists for the system, of a scientific analysis of value and surplus-value, and of reaching a result that might not be altogether palatable to the powers that be.

<!-- ch09-p012 -->
On the one hand, the values of the variable capital and of the labor-power bought by that capital are equal, and the value of this labor-power determines the necessary part of the working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor). On the other hand, the surplus-value is determined by the surplus part of the working day. It follows that surplus-value bears the same ratio to variable capital as surplus labor does to necessary labor. In other words, the rate of surplus-value s/v = (surplus labor)/(necessary labor). Both ratios, s/v and (surplus labor)/(necessary labor), express the same thing in different ways: the one by reference to materialized, embodied labor, the other by reference to living, flowing labor.

<!-- ch09-p013 -->
The rate of surplus-value is therefore an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labor-power by capital, or of the worker by the capitalist.[^7]

[^7]: Although the rate of surplus-value is an exact expression for the degree of exploitation of labor-power, it is in no sense an expression for the absolute amount of exploitation. For example, if the necessary labor = 5 hours and the surplus labor = 5 hours, the degree of exploitation is 100%. The amount of exploitation is here measured by 5 hours. If, on the other hand, the necessary labor = 6 hours and the surplus labor = 6 hours, the degree of exploitation stays at 100%, as before, while the actual amount of exploitation has increased 20%, namely from five hours to six.

<!-- ch09-p014 -->
In our example we assumed that the value of the product = £410 const. + £90 var. + £90 surpl., and that the capital advanced = £500. Since the surplus-value = £90 and the capital advanced = £500, the usual way of reckoning would give us a rate of surplus-value (which is generally confused with the rate of profits) of 18%. That rate is so low that it might give a pleasant surprise to Mr. Carey and other harmonizers (economists who taught that the interests of all classes are in harmony). But in truth the rate of surplus-value is not equal to s/C or s/(c+v), but to s/v. So it is not 90/500 but 90/90, or 100%, which is more than five times the apparent degree of exploitation. In the case we have supposed, we do not know the actual length of the working day, or how long the labor process lasts in days or weeks, or the number of workers employed. Even so, the rate of surplus-value s/v accurately reveals to us, through its equivalent expression, surplus labor/necessary labor, the relation between the two parts of the working day. Here this relation is one of equality, the rate being 100%. Clearly, then, the worker in our example works one half of the day for himself and the other half for the capitalist.

<!-- ch09-p015 -->
So, in short, the method of calculating the rate of surplus-value is as follows. We take the total value of the product and set the constant capital, which merely reappears in it, equal to zero. What remains is the only value that has actually been created in the process of producing the commodity. If the amount of surplus-value is given, we have only to deduct it from this remainder to find the variable capital. And the other way round, if the variable capital is given and we need to find the surplus-value. If both are given, we have only to perform the final operation, namely to calculate s/v, the ratio of the surplus-value to the variable capital.

<!-- ch09-p016 -->
Simple as the method is, it may not be out of place to give the reader some practice, by means of a few examples, in applying the new principles that underlie it.

<!-- ch09-p017 -->
First let us take the case of a spinning mill with 10,000 mule spindles, spinning No. 32 yarn from American cotton and producing 1 lb. of yarn per spindle each week. We assume the waste to be 6%. Under these circumstances 10,600 lbs. of cotton are used up weekly, of which 600 lbs. go to waste. The price (the money name of the value – the labor – embodied in a commodity) of the cotton in April, 1871, was 7¾d. per lb., so the raw material costs, in round numbers, £342. The 10,000 spindles, including preparation machinery and motive power, cost, we will assume, £1 per spindle, a total of £10,000. We put the wear and tear at 10%, or £1,000 a year = £20 a week. We suppose the rent of the building to be £300 a year, or £6 a week. The coal used (for 100 horse-power indicated, at 4 lbs. of coal per horse-power per hour during 60 hours, including the coal used for heating the mill) comes to 11 tons a week at 8s. 6d. a ton, about £4½ a week; gas, £1 a week; oil, etc., £4½ a week. The total cost of these auxiliary materials is £10 a week. So the constant part of the value of the week's product is £378. Wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself) amount to £52 a week. The price of the yarn is 12¼d. per lb., which gives £510 as the value of 10,000 lbs. So the surplus-value in this case is £510 - £430 = £80. We set the constant part of the value of the product = 0, since it plays no part in the creation of value. What remains is £132 as the value created each week, which = £52 var. + £80 surpl. The rate of surplus-value is therefore 80/52 = 153 11/13%. In a working day of 10 hours with average labor, the result is: necessary labor = 3 31/33 hours, and surplus labor = 6 2/33.[^8]

[^8]: The above data, which may be relied on, were given to me by a Manchester spinner. In England the horse-power of an engine used to be calculated from the diameter of its cylinder; now the actual horse-power shown by the indicator is taken.

<!-- ch09-p018 -->
One more example. Jacob gives the following calculation for the year 1815. Because several items were adjusted beforehand, it is very imperfect; nevertheless it is sufficient for our purpose. In it he assumes the price of wheat to be 8s. a quarter, and the average yield per acre to be 22 bushels.

<!-- ch09-p019 -->
```text
VALUE PRODUCED PER ACRE
Seed        £1 9s. 0d.       Tithes, Rates,   £1 1s. 0d.
                             and taxes,
Manure      £2 10s. 0d.      Rent             £1 8s. 0d.
Wages       £3 10s. 0d.      Farmer’s Profit  £1 2s. 0d.
                             and Interest
TOTAL       £7 9s. 0d.       TOTAL            £3 11s 0d.
```

<!-- ch09-p020 -->
Assuming that the price of the product is the same as its value, we find here the surplus-value divided up under the various headings of profit, interest, rent, etc. We have nothing to do with these in detail; we simply add them together, and the sum is a surplus-value of £3 11s. 0d. The sum of £3 19s. 0d. paid for seed and manure is constant capital, and we put it equal to zero. What is left is the sum of £3 10s. 0d., which is the variable capital advanced, and we see that a new value of £3 10s. 0d. + £3 11s. 0d. has been produced in its place. Therefore s/v = £3 11s. 0d. / £3 10s. 0d., giving a rate of surplus-value of more than 100%. The worker spends more than one half of his working day producing the surplus-value, which different persons share among themselves under different pretexts.[^9]

[^9]: The calculations given in the text are meant merely as illustrations. We have in fact assumed that prices = values. We shall see in Book III, however, that even in the case of average prices this assumption cannot be made in this very simple way.
