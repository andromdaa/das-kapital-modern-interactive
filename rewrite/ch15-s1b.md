In the first case, a single machine makes the whole product. It performs all the different operations that were previously done by one craftsman with his tool (for instance, by a weaver with his loom), or by several craftsmen one after another, either separately or as members of a system of manufacture.[^15] For example, in the manufacture of envelopes, one man folded the paper with the folder, another put on the gum, a third turned over the flap on which the emblem is stamped, a fourth embossed the emblem, and so on; and for each of these operations the envelope had to change hands. Now one single envelope machine performs all these operations at once, and makes more than 3,000 envelopes in an hour. At the London exhibition of 1862 there was an American machine for making paper cones. It cut the paper, pasted, folded, and finished 300 of them a minute. Here the whole process, which was split up into a series of operations and carried out by them when it was carried on as manufacture, is completed by a single machine working a combination of various tools. Now, such a machine may be merely a reproduction of a complicated hand tool, or it may be a combination of various simple tools that manufacture had specialized. In either case, in the factory, that is, in the workshop in which only machinery is used, we meet simple cooperation again. Leaving the worker out of consideration for the moment, this cooperation appears to us, at first, as the bringing together in one place of similar machines working at the same time. Thus a weaving factory is made up of a number of power-looms working side by side, and a sewing factory of a number of sewing-machines all in the same building. But here the whole system has a technical unity, because all the machines receive their impulse at the same time, and to an equal degree, from the beats of the common prime mover, through the transmitting mechanism. And this mechanism is also, to a certain extent, common to them all, since only particular branches of it branch off to each machine. So, just as a number of tools form the organs of a machine, a number of machines of one kind form the organs of the motive mechanism.

[^15]: From the standpoint of the division of labor in manufacture (the splitting of the making of one product into many separate tasks, each done by a specialized worker), weaving was not simple manual labor. On the contrary, it was complicated manual labor, and so the power-loom is a machine that does very complicated work. It is completely wrong to suppose that modern machinery at first took over only those operations that division of labor had simplified. During the manufacturing period, spinning and weaving were split up into new varieties, and their tools were modified and improved; but the labor itself was in no way divided, and it kept its handicraft character. It is not the labor, but the instrument of labor, that serves as the starting-point of the machine.

<!-- ch15-p015 -->
A real system of machinery, however, does not take the place of these independent machines until the object of labor goes through a connected series of detail processes, carried out by a chain of machines of different kinds, each one supplementing the other. Here we have once more the cooperation by division of labor that is typical of manufacture; only now it is a combination of detail machines. The special tools of the various detail workers (workers who each spend their whole working life performing one small part of a larger process), such as those of the beaters, combers, spinners, etc., in the wool manufacture, are now turned into the tools of specialized machines. Each machine forms a special organ, with a special function, in the system. In those branches of industry where the machinery system is first introduced, manufacture itself provides, in a general way, the natural basis for the division of the process of production, and so for its organization.[^16] Nevertheless an essential difference shows itself at once. In manufacture, it is the workers who, with their hand tools, must carry on each particular detail process, either singly or in groups. If, on the one hand, the worker becomes adapted to the process, on the other hand the process was first made to suit the worker. This subjective principle of the division of labor no longer exists in production by machinery. Here the process as a whole is examined objectively, in itself, that is, without regard to the question of how human hands would carry it out, and it is analyzed into its component phases. The problem of how to carry out each detail process, and how to bind them all into a whole, is solved with the help of machines, chemistry, etc.[^17] But of course here too the theory must be perfected by experience accumulated on a large scale. Each detail machine supplies raw material to the next machine in line. Since they are all working at the same time, the product is always going through the various stages of its making, and is also constantly passing from one phase to another. In manufacture, the direct cooperation of the detail workers sets up a numerical proportion between the special groups. In the same way, in an organized system of machinery, where one detail machine constantly keeps another busy, a fixed relation is set up between their numbers, their size, and their speed. The collective machine is now an organized system of various kinds of single machines, and of groups of single machines. It becomes more and more perfect the more the process as a whole becomes a continuous one, that is, the less the raw material is interrupted on its way from its first phase to its last; in other words, the more its passage from one phase to another is carried out, not by the hand of man, but by the machinery itself. In manufacture, the isolation of each detail process is a condition imposed by the nature of division of labor; in the fully developed factory, on the contrary, the continuity of those processes is essential.

[^16]: Before the era of mechanical industry, the wool manufacture was the leading manufacture in England. Hence it was in this industry that most of the experiments were made, in the first half of the 18th century. Cotton, which needed less careful preparation before machines could work it, benefited from the experience gained on wool; just as, later, the processing of wool by machinery was developed along the lines of cotton-spinning and weaving by machinery. It was only in the 10 years immediately before 1866 that isolated parts of the wool manufacture, such as wool-combing, were brought into the factory system (production in which a system of machines, driven by a central power source, does the work and workers attend the machines). “The application of power to the process of combing wool ... extensively in operation since the introduction of the combingmachine, especially Lister’s ... undoubtedly had the effect of throwing a very large number of men out of work. Wool was formerly combed by hand, most frequently in the cottage of the comber. It is now very generally combed in the factory, and hand-labour is superseded, except in some particular kinds of work, in which hand-combed wool is still preferred. Many of the hand-combers found employment in the factories, but the produce of the hand-combers bears so small a proportion to that of the machine, that the employment of a very large number of combers has passed away.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1856,” p. 16.)

[^17]: “The principle of the factory system, then, is to substitute ... the partition of a process into its essential constituents, for the division or graduation of labour among artisans.” (Andrew Ure: “The Philosophy of Manufactures,” London, 1835, p. 20.)

<!-- ch15-p016 -->
A system of machinery may rest on the mere cooperation of similar machines, as in weaving, or on a combination of different machines, as in spinning. Either way, it forms in itself one huge automaton whenever it is driven by a self-acting prime mover. But although the factory as a whole may be driven by its steam-engine, some of the individual machines may still need the worker's help for some of their movements. (Such help was needed for running in the mule carriage before the invention of the self-acting mule, and is still needed in fine-spinning mills.) Or else, to let a machine do its work, certain parts of it may have to be handled by the worker like a hand tool; this was the case in machine-makers' workshops before the slide rest was turned into a self-actor. As soon as a machine carries out, without man's help, all the movements needed to work up the raw material, and needs only his attendance, we have an automatic system of machinery, and one that can be constantly improved in its details. Such improvements as the device that stops a drawing frame whenever a sliver breaks, and the self-acting stop that halts the power-loom as soon as the shuttle bobbin runs out of weft, are quite modern inventions. As an example both of continuous production and of the carrying out of the automatic principle, we may take a modern paper mill. In the paper industry generally, we can usefully study in detail not only the differences between modes of production based on different means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things), but also the connection between the social conditions of production and those modes. For the old German paper-making gives us a sample of handicraft production; that of Holland in the 17th and of France in the 18th century, a sample of manufacture in the strict sense; and that of modern England, a sample of the automatic making of this article. Besides these, there still exist in India and China two distinct ancient Asiatic forms of the same industry.

<!-- ch15-p017 -->
An organized system of machines, which receives its motion through the transmitting mechanism from a central automaton, is the most developed form of production by machinery. Here, in place of the isolated machine, we have a mechanical monster whose body fills whole factories. His demon power, at first veiled under the slow and measured motions of his giant limbs, at last breaks out into the fast and furious whirl of his countless working organs.

<!-- ch15-p018 -->
There were mules and steam-engines before there were any workers whose only occupation was to make mules and steam-engines, just as men wore clothes before there were such people as tailors. The inventions of Vaucanson, Arkwright, Watt, and others were, however, workable only because these inventors found ready to hand a considerable number of skilled mechanical workers, placed at their disposal by the manufacturing period. Some of these workers were independent craftsmen of various trades; others were grouped together in manufacturing workshops, in which, as mentioned before, division of labor was strictly carried out. As inventions grew in number, and the demand for the newly discovered machines grew larger, the machine-making industry split up more and more into numerous independent branches, and division of labor in these manufacturing workshops developed further and further. Here, then, we see in manufacture the immediate technical foundation of modern industry. Manufacture produced the machinery by means of which modern industry abolished the handicraft and manufacturing systems in those spheres of production that it first seized upon. So the factory system was raised, in the natural course of things, on an inadequate foundation. When the system reached a certain degree of development, it had to root up this ready-made foundation, which in the meantime had been worked out further along the old lines, and to build up for itself a basis that would correspond to its own methods of production. The individual machine stays dwarfish as long as it is worked by human power alone, and no system of machinery could be properly developed before the steam-engine took the place of the earlier motive powers: animals, wind, and even water. In just the same way, modern industry was crippled in its full development as long as its characteristic instrument of production, the machine, owed its existence to personal strength and personal skill, and depended on the muscular development, the keenness of sight, and the cunning of hand with which the detail workers in manufacturing workshops, and the manual workers in handicrafts, wielded their dwarfish tools. Thus, apart from the expense of machines made in this way (a circumstance that is always present to the mind of the capitalist), the expansion of industries carried on by machinery, and the invasion of new branches of production by machinery, depended on the growth of a class of workers who, because their work was almost an art, could increase their numbers only gradually, and not by leaps and bounds. But besides this, at a certain stage of its development, modern industry became technically incompatible with the basis that handicraft and manufacture had provided for it. The prime movers, the transmitting mechanism, and the machines proper kept growing in size. The details of these machines became more complicated, more varied, and more regular, as they departed more and more from the model of the machines originally made by hand labor, and took on a form restricted by nothing except the conditions under which they worked.[^18] The automatic system had to be perfected. And a more stubborn material, such as iron instead of wood, had to be used, which became more unavoidable every day. These problems sprang up by force of circumstances, and the solution of all of them everywhere met with a stumbling-block in personal limitations, which even the collective worker (the whole body of workers, combined, treated as one productive mechanism) of manufacture could break through only to a limited extent. Machines such as the modern hydraulic press, the modern power-loom, and the modern carding engine could never have been supplied by manufacture.

[^18]: The power-loom was at first made mainly of wood; in its improved modern form it is made of iron. How far the old forms of the instruments of production influenced their new forms at the start is shown, among other things, by even the most superficial comparison of the present power-loom with the old one, and of the modern blowing apparatus of a blast-furnace with the first, inefficient mechanical copy of the ordinary bellows. It is shown perhaps more strikingly than in any other way by the attempts, before the present locomotive was invented, to build a locomotive that actually had two feet, which it raised from the ground one after the other, like a horse. Only after considerable development of the science of mechanics, and accumulated practical experience, does the form of a machine become settled entirely in accordance with mechanical principles, and freed from the traditional form of the tool that gave rise to it.

A radical change in the mode of production in one sphere of industry brings with it a similar change in other spheres. This happens at first in those branches of industry that are linked together as separate phases of one process, and yet are isolated by the social division of labor in such a way that each of them produces an independent commodity. Thus spinning by machinery made weaving by machinery a necessity, and both together made imperative the mechanical and chemical revolution that took place in bleaching, printing, and dyeing. So too, on the other hand, the revolution in cotton-spinning called forth the invention of the gin, for separating the seeds from the cotton fiber. Only by means of this invention did it become possible to produce cotton on the enormous scale now required.[^19] But above all, the revolution in the modes of production of industry and agriculture made necessary a revolution in the general conditions of the social process of production, that is, in the means of communication and transport. In a society whose pivot, to use an expression of Fourier's, was small-scale agriculture, with its subsidiary domestic industries, and the urban handicrafts, the means of communication and transport were so utterly inadequate to the productive needs of the manufacturing period, with its extended division of social labor, its concentration of the instruments of labor and of the workers, and its colonial markets, that they were in fact revolutionized. In the same way, the means of communication and transport handed down from the manufacturing period soon became unbearable fetters on modern industry, with its feverish haste of production, its enormous extent, its constant flinging of capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger) and labor from one sphere of production into another, and its newly created connections with the markets of the whole world. Hence, apart from the radical changes made in the construction of sailing ships, the means of communication and transport were gradually adapted to the modes of production of mechanical industry, by the creation of a system of river steamers, railways, ocean steamers, and telegraphs. But the huge masses of iron that now had to be forged, welded, cut, bored, and shaped demanded, for their part, giant, Cyclops-like machines, and the methods of the manufacturing period were utterly inadequate for building them.

[^19]: Eli Whitney's cotton gin had, until very recently, undergone fewer essential changes than any other machine of the 18th century. Only in the last decade (that is, since 1856) has another American, Mr. Emery, of Albany, New York, made Whitney's gin out of date by an improvement as simple as it is effective.

So modern industry had to take the machine, its characteristic instrument of production, into its own hands, and to build machines by machines. Only when it did this did it build up a fitting technical foundation for itself and stand on its own feet. In the first decades of this century, as the use of machinery increased, machinery by degrees took over the making of machines proper. But it was only in the decade before 1866 that the building of railways and ocean steamers on a stupendous scale called into existence the Cyclops-like machines now used in building prime movers.

<!-- ch15-p021 -->
The most essential condition for producing machines by machines was a prime mover capable of exerting any amount of force, and yet under perfect control. The steam-engine already met this condition. But at the same time it was necessary to produce the geometrically exact straight lines, planes, circles, cylinders, cones, and spheres that the detail parts of the machines required. Henry Maudsley solved this problem in the first decade of this century by inventing the slide rest. This tool was soon made automatic, and in a modified form it was applied to other construction machines besides the lathe, for which it was originally intended. This mechanical device replaces, not some particular tool, but the hand itself, which produces a given form by holding and guiding the cutting tool along the iron or other material being worked on. Thus it became possible to produce the forms of the individual parts of machinery

If we now turn our attention to that part of the machinery used in building machines which forms the operating tool, we find the hand tools reappearing, but on a Cyclops-like scale. The operating part of the boring machine is an immense drill driven by a steam-engine; and without this machine, on the other hand, the cylinders of large steam-engines and of hydraulic presses could not be made. The mechanical lathe is only a Cyclops-like reproduction of the ordinary foot-lathe. The planing machine is an iron carpenter that works on iron with the same tools that the human carpenter uses on wood. The instrument that cuts the veneers on the London wharves is a gigantic razor. The tool of the shearing machine, which shears iron as easily as a tailor's scissors cut cloth, is a monster pair of scissors. And the steam-hammer works with an ordinary hammer head, but one so heavy that not Thor himself could wield it.[^21] These steam-hammers are an invention of Nasmyth. There is one that weighs over 6 tons and strikes with a vertical fall of 7 feet onto an anvil weighing 36 tons. It is mere child's play for it to crush a block of granite to powder, yet it is just as capable of driving a nail into a piece of soft wood with a series of light taps.[^22]

[^21]: One of these machines, used in London for forging paddle-wheel shafts, is called “Thor.” It forges a shaft of 16½ tons as easily as a blacksmith forges a horseshoe.

[^22]: Woodworking machines that can also be used on a small scale are mostly American inventions.

The instruments of labor, in the form of machinery, make it necessary to replace human force with natural forces, and rule of thumb with the conscious application of science. In manufacture, the organization of the social labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things) is purely subjective: it is a combination of detail workers. In its machinery system, modern industry has a productive organism that is purely objective, in which the worker becomes a mere appendage to an already existing material condition of production. In simple cooperation, and even in cooperation based on division of labor, the suppression of the isolated worker by the collective worker still appears to be more or less accidental. Machinery, with a few exceptions to be mentioned later, operates only by means of associated labor, or labor in common. Hence the cooperative character of the labor process is, in the case of machinery, a technical necessity dictated by the instrument of labor itself.
