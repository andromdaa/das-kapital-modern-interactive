### Section 5: The Capitalistic Character of Manufacture

<!-- ch14-p040 -->
An increased number of workers under the control of one capitalist is the natural starting-point both of cooperation in general and of manufacture in particular. But the division of labor in manufacture makes this increase in the number of workers a technical necessity. Here the minimum number of workers that any given capitalist is bound to employ is prescribed by the division of labor already established. On the other hand, the advantages of further division can be gained only by adding to the number of workers, and this can be done only by adding multiples of the various detail groups. But an increase in the variable component of the capital employed (the part spent on labor-power) requires an increase in its constant component (the part spent on means of production) as well: in the workshops, implements, etc., and above all in the raw material, the demand for which grows faster than the number of workers. The quantity of raw material that a given amount of labor consumes in a given time increases in the same ratio as the productive power of that labor increases as a result of its division. Hence it is a law, based on the very nature of manufacture, that the minimum amount of capital that each capitalist is bound to have in hand must keep increasing. In other words, the transformation of the social means of production and means of subsistence into capital must keep extending.[^39]

[^39]: “It is not sufficient that the capital” (the writer should have said the necessary means of subsistence and of production) “required for the subdivision of handicrafts should be in readiness in the society: it must also be accumulated in the hands of the employers in sufficiently large quantities to enable them to conduct their operations on a large scale.... The more the division increases, the more does the constant employment of a given number of labourers require a greater outlay of capital in tools, raw material, &c.” (Storch: “Cours d’Econ. Polit.” Paris Ed., t. I., pp. 250, 251.) “La concentration des instruments de production et la division du travail sont aussi inséparables l’une de l’autre que le sont, dans le régime politique, la concentration des pouvoirs publics et la division des intérêts privés.” [The concentration of the instruments of production and the division of labor are as inseparable from each other as, in politics, the concentration of public powers and the division of private interests are.] (Karl Marx, cited above, p. 134.)

<!-- ch14-p041 -->
In manufacture, as in simple cooperation, the collective working organism is a form in which capital exists. The mechanism made up of many individual detail workers belongs to the capitalist. Hence the productive power that results from combining labors appears to be the productive power of capital. Manufacture proper not only subjects the previously independent worker to the discipline and command of capital. It also creates a hierarchy of ranks among the workers themselves. Simple cooperation leaves the individual's way of working mostly unchanged, while manufacture revolutionizes it thoroughly and seizes labor-power by its very roots. It turns the worker into a crippled monstrosity, by forcing on his skill in one detail at the expense of a whole world of productive abilities and instincts; just as in the States of La Plata they butcher a whole beast for the sake of its hide or its tallow. Not only are the detail tasks distributed among different individuals; the individual himself is made into the automatic motor of a fractional operation,[^40] and the absurd fable of Menenius Agrippa, which makes a man a mere fragment of his own body, comes true.[^41] If at first the worker sells his labor-power to capital because he lacks the material means of producing a commodity, now his very labor-power refuses its services unless it has been sold to capital. Its functions can be carried out only in an environment that exists in the capitalist's workshop after the sale. Unfitted by nature to make anything independently, the manufacturing worker develops productive activity as a mere appendage of the capitalist's workshop.[^42] Just as the chosen people bore Jehovah's own signature on their faces, so the division of labor brands the manufacturing worker as the property of capital.

[^40]: Dugald Stewart calls manufacturing workers “living automatons ... employed in the details of the work.” (Cited above, p. 318.)

[^41]: In corals, each individual is in fact the stomach of the whole group. But it supplies the group with nourishment, instead of withdrawing it, as the Roman patrician did.

[^42]: “L’ouvrier qui porte dans ses bras tout un métier, peut aller partout exercer son industrie et trouver des moyens de subsister: l’autre (the manufacturing labourer) n’est qu’un accessoire qui, séparé de ses confrères, n’a plus ni capacité, ni indépendance, et qui se trouve force d’accepter la loi qu’on juge à propos de lui imposer.” [The worker who carries a whole craft in his arms can go anywhere to practice his trade and find the means of subsistence. The other (the manufacturing worker) is only an accessory who, once separated from his fellows, no longer has either ability or independence, and finds himself forced to accept whatever law it is thought fit to impose on him.] (Storch, cited above, Petersb. edit., 1815, t. I., p. 204.)

The independent peasant or craftsman exercises knowledge, judgment, and will, even if only to a very small degree, in the same way that the savage makes the whole art of war consist in the exercise of his own personal cunning. These faculties are now required only for the workshop as a whole. Intelligence in production expands in one direction because it vanishes in many others. What the detail workers lose is concentrated in the capital that employs them.[^43] It is a result of the division of labor in manufacture that the worker is brought face to face with the intellectual powers of the material process of production as the property of another, and as a ruling power. This separation begins in simple cooperation, where the capitalist represents to the individual worker the unity and the will of the combined labor. It develops in manufacture, which cuts the worker down into a detail worker. It is completed in modern industry, which makes science a productive force separate from labor and presses it into the service of capital.[^44]

[^43]: A. Ferguson, cited above, p. 281: “The former may have gained what the other has lost.”

[^44]: “The man of knowledge and the productive labourer come to be widely divided from each other, and knowledge, instead of remaining the handmaid of labour in the hand of the labourer to increase his productive powers ... has almost everywhere arrayed itself against labour ... systematically deluding and leading them (the labourers) astray in order to render their muscular powers entirely mechanical and obedient.” (W. Thompson: “An Inquiry into the Principles of the Distribution of Wealth.” London, 1824, p. 274.)

Some crippling of body and mind is inseparable even from the division of labor in society as a whole. However, because manufacture carries this social separation of branches of labor much further, and also, by its own peculiar kind of division, attacks the individual at the very roots of his life[^50], it is the first to supply the materials for industrial pathology, and to give it a start.

Cooperation based on the division of labor, in other words manufacture, begins as a formation that arises spontaneously. As soon as it gains some solidity and scope, it becomes the recognized, methodical, and systematic form of capitalist production. History shows how the division of labor peculiar to manufacture in the strict sense first finds its best-suited form by experience, as it were behind the backs of the actors. Then, like the guild handicrafts, it strives to hold fast to that form once it has been found, and here and there it succeeds in keeping it for centuries. Any change in this form, except in trivial matters, is due solely to a revolution in the instruments of labor. Wherever modern manufacture arises (I am not referring here to modern industry based on machinery), it either finds the disjecta membra poetae [the scattered limbs of the poet] ready to hand, only waiting to be collected together, as in the manufacture of clothes in large towns; or it can easily apply the principle of division simply by assigning the various operations of a handicraft (such as book-binding) exclusively to particular men. In such cases, a week's experience is enough to fix the proportion between the numbers of workers needed for the various functions.[^52]

[^52]: The simple belief in the inventive genius that the individual capitalist exercises in advance in the division of labor exists nowadays only among German professors of the stamp of Herr Roscher. To reward the capitalist, from whose Jovian head the division of labor sprang ready-made, Roscher dedicates to him “various wages” (diverse *Arbeitslöhne*). How far the division of labor is applied depends on the length of the purse, not on the greatness of the genius.

<!-- ch14-p054 -->
By breaking handicrafts down, by specializing the instruments of labor, by forming detail workers, and by grouping and combining them into a single mechanism, the division of labor in manufacture creates a qualitative gradation and a quantitative proportion in the social process of production. It therefore creates a definite organization of the labor of society, and so at the same time develops new productive forces in society. In its specific capitalist form (and under the given conditions it could take no form other than a capitalist one), manufacture is only a particular method of producing relative surplus-value, or of increasing, at the worker's expense, the self-expansion of capital, which is usually called social wealth, “Wealth of Nations,” etc. (Relative surplus-value is surplus-value obtained by shortening necessary labor-time, by making workers' necessities cheaper through higher productivity, so that more of a working day of given length is surplus labor.) Manufacture increases the social productive power of labor not only for the benefit of the capitalist instead of the worker, but it does this by crippling the individual workers. It creates new conditions for the rule of capital over labor. So if, on the one hand, it appears historically as progress and as a necessary phase in the economic development of society, on the other hand it is a refined and civilized method of exploitation.

<!-- ch14-p055 -->
Political economy (the science of economics as it was then called), which first arose as an independent science during the period of manufacture, views the social division of labor only from the standpoint of manufacture,[^53] and sees in it only a means of producing more commodities with a given quantity of labor, and so of cheapening commodities and speeding up the accumulation of capital (the turning of surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows). In the sharpest contrast with this stress on quantity and exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for) stands the attitude of the writers of classical antiquity, who hold exclusively to quality and use-value.[^54] For them, as a result of the separation of the social branches of production, commodities are better made, people's various inclinations and talents choose a field that suits them,[^55] and without some restraint no important results can be achieved anywhere.[^56] Hence both product and producer are improved by the division of labor. If the growth of the quantity produced is occasionally mentioned, this is only with reference to a greater abundance of use-values. There is not a word about exchange-value or about the cheapening of commodities. This view, from the standpoint of use-value alone, is taken both by Plato,[^57] who treats the division of labor as the foundation of the division of society into classes, and by Xenophon[^58], who, with a characteristically bourgeois instinct, comes closer to the division of labor within the workshop. Plato's Republic, insofar as it treats the division of labor as the formative principle of the State, is merely the Athenian idealization of the Egyptian caste system. Egypt served as the model of an industrial country to many of his contemporaries too, among them Isocrates,[^59] and it kept this importance for the Greeks of the Roman Empire.[^60]

During the manufacturing period proper, that is, the period in which manufacture is the predominant form taken by capitalist production, many obstacles stand in the way of the full development of manufacture's own peculiar tendencies. As we have already seen, manufacture creates a simple separation of the workers into skilled and unskilled, at the same time as it arranges them in a hierarchy of classes. Even so, the number of unskilled workers remains very limited, because of the predominant influence of the skilled. Manufacture adapts the detail operations to the different degrees of maturity, strength, and development of the living instruments of labor, and so tends toward the exploitation of women and children. Yet this tendency as a whole is wrecked on the habits and the resistance of the male workers. The splitting up of handicrafts lowers the cost of training the worker, and so lowers his value. Yet the more difficult detail work still needs a longer apprenticeship, and even where apprenticeship would be unnecessary, the workers jealously insist on it. In England, for example, we find the laws of apprenticeship, with their seven years' probation, in full force right down to the end of the manufacturing period; they are not thrown aside until modern industry arrives. Since handicraft skill is the foundation of manufacture, and since the mechanism of manufacture as a whole has no framework apart from the workers themselves, capital is constantly forced to wrestle with the insubordination of the workers.

Hence, throughout the whole manufacturing period, there runs the complaint that the workers lack discipline[^62]. And even if we did not have the testimony of contemporary writers, the simple facts would speak volumes: that between the 16th century and the epoch of modern industry, capital failed to become master of all the working time the manufacturing workers had available; that manufacturing workshops are short-lived; and that they change their location from one country to another as the workers emigrate or immigrate. “Order must in one way or another be established,” exclaims the often-cited author of the “Essay on Trade and Commerce” in 1770. “Order,” echoes Dr. Andrew Ure 66 years later, “Order” was lacking in manufacture based on “the scholastic dogma of division of labour,” and “Arkwright created order.”

[^62]: This is more the case in England than in France, and more in France than in Holland.

At the same time, manufacture was unable either to take hold of the production of society in its full extent or to revolutionize that production to its very core. It towered up as an economic work of art on the broad foundation of the town handicrafts and the rural domestic industries. At a certain stage of its development, the narrow technical basis on which manufacture rested came into conflict with requirements of production that manufacture itself had created.

<!-- ch14-p060 -->
One of its most finished creations was the workshop for producing the instruments of labor themselves, including above all the complicated mechanical apparatus already in use at the time.

<!-- ch14-p061 -->
> A machine-factory, says Ure, “displayed the division of labour in manifold gradations ‒ the file, the drill, the lathe, having each its different workman in the order of skill.” (P. 21.)

<!-- ch14-p062 -->
This workshop, the product of the division of labor in manufacture, produced in its turn ‒ machines. It is machines that sweep away the craftsman's work as the regulating principle of social production. Thus, on the one hand, the technical reason for tying the worker for life to one detail function is removed. On the other hand, the fetters that this same principle laid on the rule of capital fall away.
