### Section 3: The Two Fundamental Forms of Manufacture: Heterogeneous Manufacture, Serial Manufacture

<!-- ch14-p015 -->
The organization of manufacture has two fundamental forms. Although they sometimes blend, they are essentially different in kind. Moreover, they play very different parts later on, when manufacture is transformed into modern industry carried on by machinery. (Modern industry is large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system.) This double character comes from the nature of the article produced. Either the article results from the mere mechanical fitting together of partial products made independently, or it owes its completed shape to a series of connected processes and manual operations.

<!-- ch14-p016 -->
A locomotive, for instance, consists of more than 5,000 independent parts. It cannot, however, serve as an example of the first kind of genuine manufacture, because it is a structure produced by modern mechanical industry. But a watch can, and William Petty used it to illustrate the division of labor in manufacture. The watch was once the individual work of a Nuremberg craftsman. It has been transformed into the social product of an immense number of detail workers, such as mainspring makers, dial makers, spiral spring makers, jeweled hole makers, ruby lever makers, hand makers, case makers, screw makers, gilders, with numerous subdivisions, such as wheel makers (brass and steel separate), pin makers, movement makers, acheveur de pignon [pinion finisher] (fixes the wheels on the axles, polishes the facets, etc.), pivot makers, planteur de finissage [finishing setter] (puts the wheels and springs in the works), finisseur de barillet [barrel finisher] (cuts teeth in the wheels, makes the holes of the right size, etc.), escapement makers, cylinder makers for cylinder escapements, escapement wheel makers, balance wheel makers, raquette makers (apparatus for regulating the watch), the planteur d’échappement [escapement setter] (escapement maker proper); then the repasseur de barillet [barrel re-finisher] (finishes the box for the spring, etc.), steel polishers, wheel polishers, screw polishers, figure painters, dial enamelers (melt the enamel on the copper), fabricant de pendants [pendant maker] (makes the ring by which the case is hung), finisseur de charnière [hinge finisher] (puts the brass hinge in the cover, etc.), faiseur de secret [secret maker] (puts in the springs that open the case), graveur [engraver], ciseleur [chaser], polisseur de boîte [case polisher], etc., etc., and last of all the repasseur [re-finisher], who fits together the whole watch and hands it over in going order. Only a few parts of the watch pass through several hands. All these membra disjecta [scattered limbs] come together for the first time in the hand that binds them into one mechanical whole. The finished product has only an external relation to its various and diverse elements. This makes it a matter of chance, in this case as in the case of all similar finished articles, whether the detail workers are brought together in one workshop or not. The detail operations may further be carried on like so many independent handicrafts, as they are in the Cantons of Vaud and Neufchâtel. In Geneva, on the other hand, there are large watch manufacturing workshops where the detail workers cooperate directly under the control of a single capitalist. And even in these workshops the dial, the springs, and the case are seldom made in the factory itself. In the watch trade, carrying on the trade as a manufacture, with the workers concentrated in one place, is profitable only under exceptional conditions. This is because competition is greater among the workers who want to work at home, and because splitting the work into a number of heterogeneous processes allows only little use of the instruments of labor in common, and the capitalist, by scattering the work, saves the outlay on workshops, etc.[^7] Nevertheless, the position of this detail worker, who works at home but does so for a capitalist (manufacturer, établisseur [establisher]), is very different from that of the independent craftsman, who works for his own customers.[^8]

[^7]: In the year 1854 Geneva produced 80,000 watches, which is not one-fifth of the production in the Canton of Neufchâtel. La Chaux-de-Fond alone, which we may regard as one huge watch manufacturing workshop, produces twice as many watches each year as Geneva. From 1850-61 Geneva produced 720,000 watches. See “Report from Geneva on the Watch Trade” in “Reports by H. M.’s Secretaries of Embassy and Legation on the Manufactures, Commerce, &c., No. 6, 1863.” Some articles merely consist of parts fitted together, and their production is split up into processes that have no connection with each other. This lack of connection alone makes it very difficult to convert such a manufacture into a branch of modern industry carried on by machinery. But in the case of a watch there are two further obstacles in addition: the minuteness and delicacy of its parts, and its character as a luxury article. Hence their variety, which is so great that in the best London houses scarcely a dozen watches are made alike in the course of a year. The watch manufacturing workshop of Messrs. Vacheron & Constantin, which has used machinery with success, produces at most three or four different varieties of size and form.

[^8]: In watchmaking, that classic example of heterogeneous manufacture, we can study with great accuracy the differentiation and specialization of the instruments of labor, mentioned above, that the subdivision of handicrafts brings about.

The second kind of manufacture, its perfected form, produces articles that go through connected phases of development, through a series of processes step by step. An example is the wire in the manufacture of needles, which passes through the hands of 72, and sometimes even 92, different detail workers.

<!-- ch14-p018 -->
Insofar as such a manufacture, when first started, combines scattered handicrafts, it reduces the distance between the various phases of production. The time taken in passing from one stage to another is shortened, and so is the labor that carries out this passage.[^9] Compared with a handicraft, productive power is gained, and this gain is due to the general cooperative character of manufacture. On the other hand, division of labor, which is the distinguishing principle of manufacture, requires the various stages of production to be isolated and independent of each other. To set up and maintain a connection between the isolated functions, the article must be carried constantly from one hand to another, and from one process to another. From the standpoint of modern mechanical industry, this necessity stands out as a characteristic and costly disadvantage, and one that is built into the principle of manufacture.[^10]

[^9]: “In so close a cohabitation of the people, the carriage [transport] must needs be less.” (“The Advantages of the East India Trade,” p. 106.)

[^10]: “The isolation of the different stages of manufacture, consequent upon the employment of manual labour, adds immensely to the cost of production, the loss mainly arising from the mere removals from one process to another.” (“The Industry of Nations.” London, 1855, Part II, p. 200.)

<!-- ch14-p019 -->
If we focus on some particular batch of raw material (of rags, for instance, in paper manufacture, or of wire in needle manufacture), we see that it passes in succession through a series of stages in the hands of the various detail workers until it is complete. On the other hand, if we look at the workshop as a whole, we see the raw material in all the stages of its production at the same time. The collective worker draws the wire with one set of his many hands, armed with one kind of tool. At the same time, with another set, armed with different tools, he straightens it; with another, he cuts it; with another, he points it; and so on. The different detail processes, which were successive in time, have become simultaneous: they go on side by side in space. Hence a greater amount of finished commodities is produced in a given time.[^11] This simultaneity, it is true, is due to the general cooperative form of the process as a whole. But manufacture not only finds the conditions for cooperation ready to hand; it also, to some extent, creates them by subdividing handicraft labor. On the other hand, it achieves this social organization of the labor process only by riveting each worker to a single fractional detail.

[^11]: “It (the division of labour) produces also an economy of time by separating the work into its different branches, all of which may be carried on into execution at the same moment.... By carrying on all the different processes at once, which an individual must have executed separately, it becomes possible to produce a multitude of pins completely finished in the same time as a single pin might have been either cut or pointed.” (Dugald Stewart, cited above, p. 319.)

<!-- ch14-p020 -->
The fractional product of each detail worker is, at the same time, only a particular stage in the development of one and the same finished article. So each worker, or each group of workers, prepares the raw material for another worker or group. The result of the labor of the one is the starting-point for the labor of the other. The one worker therefore directly gives work to the other. The labor-time needed in each partial process to attain the desired effect is learned by experience. The mechanism of manufacture as a whole is based on the assumption that a given result will be obtained in a given time. Only on this assumption can the various supplementary labor processes go on without interruption, simultaneously, and side by side. Clearly, this direct dependence of the operations, and therefore of the workers, on each other forces each one of them to spend no more than the necessary time on his work. In this way a continuity, uniformity, regularity, order,[^12] and even intensity of labor arise that are of quite a different kind from those found in an independent handicraft or even in simple cooperation. In the production of commodities generally, the rule that the labor-time spent on a commodity should not exceed the socially necessary labor-time for its production (the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor) appears to be established by the mere effect of competition. For, to put it superficially, each single producer is obliged to sell his commodity at its market price. In manufacture, on the contrary, turning out a given amount of product in a given time is a technical law of the process of production itself.[^13]

[^12]: “The more variety of artists [craftsmen] to every manufacture... the greater the order and regularity of every work, the same must needs be done in less time, the labour must be less.” (“The Advantages,” etc., p. 68.)

[^13]: Nevertheless, in many branches of industry the manufacturing system achieves this result only very imperfectly, because it does not know how to control with certainty the general chemical and physical conditions of the process of production.

Different operations, however, take unequal lengths of time, and therefore yield unequal quantities of fractional products in equal times. If, therefore, the same worker has to perform the same operation day after day, there must be a different number of workers for each operation. In type manufacture, for instance, there are four founders and two breakers to one rubber: the founder casts 2,000 type an hour, the breaker breaks up 4,000, and the rubber polishes 8,000. Here we have again the principle of cooperation in its simplest form, the simultaneous employment of many people doing the same thing; only now this principle is the expression of an organic relation. The division of labor, as carried out in manufacture, not only simplifies and multiplies the qualitatively different parts of the social collective worker. It also creates a fixed mathematical relation or ratio that regulates the quantitative extent of those parts, that is, the relative number of workers, or the relative size of the group of workers, for each detail operation. Along with the qualitative subdivision of the social labor process, it develops a quantitative rule and proportionality for that process.

<!-- ch14-p022 -->
Once the most fitting proportion between the numbers of detail workers in the various groups has been established by experiment, for production on a given scale, that scale can be extended only by employing a multiple of each particular group.[^14] There is this in addition: the same individual can do certain kinds of work just as well on a large scale as on a small one; for instance, the labor of supervision, the carrying of the fractional product from one stage to the next, etc. Isolating such functions and assigning them to a particular worker does not become advantageous until the number of workers employed has increased; but this increase must affect every group in proportion.

[^14]: “When (from the peculiar nature of the produce of each manufactory), the number of processes into which it is most advantageous to divide it is ascertained, as well as the number of individuals to be employed, then all other manufactories which do not employ a direct multiple of this number will produce the article at a greater cost.... Hence arises one of the causes of the great size of manufacturing establishments.” (C. Babbage. “On the Economy of Machinery,” 1st ed. London. 1832. Ch. xxi, pp. 172-73.)

<!-- ch14-p023 -->
The isolated group of workers to whom any particular detail function is assigned is made up of elements of the same kind, and is one of the component parts of the total mechanism. In many branches of manufacture, however, the group itself is an organized body of labor, and the total mechanism is a repetition or multiplication of these elementary organisms. Take, for instance, the manufacture of glass bottles. It can be broken down into three essentially different stages. First comes the preliminary stage: preparing the components of the glass, mixing the sand and lime, etc., and melting them into a fluid mass of glass.[^15] Various detail workers are employed in this first stage, as also in the final one of removing the bottles from the drying furnace, sorting and packing them, etc. In the middle, between these two stages, comes the glass melting proper, the working of the fluid mass. At each mouth of the furnace there works a group, called “the hole,” made up of one bottlemaker or finisher, one blower, one gatherer, one putter-up or whetter-off, and one taker-in. These five detail workers are so many special organs of a single working organism that acts only as a whole, and so can operate only through the direct cooperation of all five. The whole body is paralyzed if even one of its members is missing. But a glass furnace has several openings (in England from 4 to 6). Each of them holds an earthenware melting-pot full of molten glass, and employs a similar five-member group of workers. The organization of each group is based on division of labor, but the bond between the different groups is simple cooperation. This cooperation uses one of the means of production, the furnace, in common, and so makes it be used up more economically. (Means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things.) Such a furnace, with its 4-6 groups, makes up a glass house; and a glass manufacturing workshop includes a number of such glass houses, together with the apparatus and workers needed for the preparatory and final stages.

[^15]: In England, the melting-furnace is separate from the glass-furnace in which the glass is worked. In Belgium, one and the same furnace serves for both processes.

<!-- ch14-p024 -->
Finally, just as manufacture arises in part from combining various handicrafts, so too it develops into a combination of various branches of manufacture. The larger English glass manufacturers, for instance, make their own earthenware melting-pots, because the success or failure of the process depends to a great extent on the quality of these pots. Here the manufacture of one of the means of production is united with that of the product. On the other hand, the manufacture of the product may be united with other branches of manufacture for which that product is the raw material, or with branches of manufacture whose products it is itself later mixed with. Thus we find the manufacture of flint glass combined with glass cutting and brass founding, the brass founding being for the metal settings of various glass articles. The various branches of manufacture combined in this way form more or less separate departments of a larger manufacture, but they are at the same time independent processes, each with its own division of labor. In spite of the many advantages this combination of branches of manufacture offers, it never grows into a complete technical system on its own foundation. That happens only when it is transformed into an industry carried on by machinery.

<!-- ch14-p025 -->
Early in the manufacturing period, the principle of lessening the necessary labor-time in the production of commodities[^16] was accepted and formulated. And the use of machines sprang up here and there, especially for certain simple first processes that have to be carried out on a very large scale and with the application of great force. Thus, at an early period in paper manufacture, the rags were torn up by paper-mills; and in metal works, the ores were pounded by stamping mills.[^17] The Roman Empire had handed down the elementary form of all machinery in the water-wheel.[^18]

[^16]: This can be seen from W. Petty, John Bellers, Andrew Yarranton, “The Advantages of the East India Trade,” and J. Vanderlint, not to mention others.

[^17]: Toward the end of the 16th century, mortars and sieves were still used in France for pounding and washing ores.

[^18]: The whole history of the development of machinery can be traced in the history of the grain mill. In England the factory is still a “mill.” In German technological works of the first decade of this century, the term “Mühle” [mill] is still in use, not only for all machinery driven by the forces of Nature, but also for all manufacturing workshops where apparatus of the nature of machinery is applied.

<!-- ch14-p026 -->
The handicraft period handed down to us the great inventions of the compass, of gunpowder, of type-printing, and of the automatic clock. But on the whole, machinery played the subordinate part that Adam Smith assigns to it in comparison with division of labor.[^19] The sporadic use of machinery in the 17th century was of the greatest importance, because it gave the great mathematicians of that time a practical basis and a stimulus for creating the science of mechanics.

[^19]: As will be seen in more detail in the fourth book of this work, Adam Smith has not established a single new proposition about division of labor. What marks him out, however, as the political economist above all others of the period of manufacture is the stress he lays on division of labor. The subordinate part he assigns to machinery gave rise, in the early days of modern mechanical industry, to the polemic of Lauderdale and, at a later period, to that of Ure. A. Smith also confuses the differentiation of the instruments of labor, in which the detail workers themselves took an active part, with the invention of machinery. In the invention of machinery, it is not the workers in manufacturing workshops who play a part, but learned men, craftsmen, and even peasants (Brindley).

<!-- ch14-p027 -->
The collective worker, formed by combining a number of detail workers, is the machinery specially characteristic of the manufacturing period. The producer of a commodity performs various operations in turn, and these merge into one another as production goes on. They make different demands on him. In one operation he must exert more strength, in another more skill, in another more attention; and the same individual does not possess all these qualities in an equal degree. Once manufacture has separated the various operations, made them independent, and isolated them, the workers are divided, classified, and grouped according to their dominant qualities. If their natural gifts are, on the one hand, the foundation on which the division of labor is built, on the other hand manufacture, once introduced, develops in them new powers that are by nature fitted only for limited and special functions. The collective worker now possesses all the qualities needed for production in an equal degree of excellence. He spends them in the most economical way, by using all his organs, which consist of particular workers or groups of workers, exclusively in performing their special functions.[^20] The one-sidedness and the deficiencies of the detail worker become perfections when he is part of the collective worker.[^21] The habit of doing only one thing turns him into a never-failing instrument, while his connection with the whole mechanism forces him to work with the regularity of the parts of a machine.[^22]

[^20]: “The master manufacturer, by dividing the work to be executed into different processes, each requiring different degrees of skill or of force, can purchase exactly that precise quantity of both which is necessary for each process; whereas, if the whole work were executed by one workman, that person must possess sufficient skill to perform the most difficult, and sufficient strength to execute the most laborious of the operations into which the article is divided.” (Ch. Babbage, cited above, ch. xix.)

[^21]: For instance, abnormal development of some muscles, curvature of bones, etc.

[^22]: One of the Inquiry Commissioners asked how the young persons are kept steadily at their work. Mr. Wm. Marshall, the general manager of a glass manufacturing workshop, answered the question very correctly: “They cannot well neglect their work; when they once begin, they must go on; they are just the same as parts of a machine.” (“Children’s Empl. Comm.,” 4th Rep., 1865, p. 247.)

The collective worker has functions both simple and complex, both high and low. So his members, the individual labor-powers, need different degrees of training, and must therefore have different values. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) Manufacture therefore develops a hierarchy of labor-powers, and a scale of wages corresponds to it. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) If, on the one hand, the individual workers are appropriated and annexed for life by a limited function, on the other hand the various operations of the hierarchy are parceled out among the workers according to both their natural and their acquired abilities.[^23] Every process of production, however, requires certain simple manipulations that every man is capable of doing. These too are now cut off from their connection with the weightier elements of activity, and hardened into exclusive functions of specially appointed workers. Hence, in every handicraft it seizes on, manufacture gives rise to a class of so-called unskilled workers, a class that handicraft industry strictly excluded. If it develops a one-sided specialty into a perfection, at the expense of a man's whole working capacity, it also begins to make a specialty of the absence of all development. Alongside the hierarchical grading there appears the simple separation of the workers into skilled and unskilled. For the unskilled, the cost of apprenticeship disappears. For the skilled, it diminishes compared with that of craftsmen, because the functions have been simplified. In both cases the value of labor-power falls.[^24] (The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work.) An exception to this law holds whenever the breaking up of the labor process gives rise to new and comprehensive functions that had either no place at all, or only a very modest one, in handicrafts. The fall in the value of labor-power, caused by the disappearance or reduction of the costs of apprenticeship, means a direct increase of surplus-value for the benefit of capital. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) For everything that shortens the necessary labor-time required for reproducing labor-power extends the domain of surplus labor. (Necessary labor-time is the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages; surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.)

[^23]: Dr. Ure, in his glorification of Modern Mechanical Industry, brings out the peculiar character of manufacture more sharply than earlier economists, who did not have his polemical interest in the matter. He brings it out more sharply even than his contemporaries, Babbage for example, who, though much his superior as a mathematician and expert in mechanics, treated mechanical industry from the standpoint of manufacture alone. Ure says, “This appropriation ... to each, a workman of appropriate value and cost was naturally assigned, forms the very essence of division of labour.” On the other hand, he describes this division as “adaptation of labour to the different talents of men,” and lastly, he characterizes the whole manufacturing system as “a system for the division or gradation of labour,” as “the division of labour into degrees of skill,” etc. (Ure, cited above, pp. 19-23 passim.)

[^24]: “Each handicraftsman being ... enabled to perfect himself by practice in one point, became ... a cheaper workman.” (Ure, cited above, p. 19.)
