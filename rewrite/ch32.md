## Chapter 32: Historical Tendency of Capitalist Accumulation

<!-- ch32-p001 -->
What does the primitive accumulation of capital, that is, its historical origin, come down to? (Capital means value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) To the extent that it is not the direct transformation of slaves and serfs into wage workers, and therefore a mere change of form, it means only the expropriation of the immediate producers, that is, the breaking up of private property based on the labor of its owner. Private property, as the opposite of social, collective property, exists only where the means of labor and the external conditions of labor belong to private individuals. But private property has a different character depending on whether these private individuals are workers or not workers. The countless shades that it shows at first sight correspond to the in-between stages that lie between these two extremes. The worker's private property in his means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things) is the foundation of small-scale industry, whether agricultural, manufacturing, or both. Small-scale industry, in turn, is an essential condition for the development of social production and of the free individuality of the worker himself. Of course, this small-scale mode of production also exists under slavery, serfdom, and other states of dependence. But it flourishes, it lets loose all its energy, it reaches its adequate classical form, only where the worker is the private owner of his own means of labor, which he sets in motion himself: the peasant of the land he cultivates, the artisan of the tool he handles like a virtuoso. This mode of production presupposes that the land is cut up into small plots and that the other means of production are scattered. Just as it excludes the concentration of these means of production, it also excludes cooperation, division of labor within each separate process of production, the control over the forces of Nature and their productive application by society, and the free development of the social productive powers. (Cooperation means many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist's command. Division of labor means the splitting of the making of one product into many separate tasks, each done by a specialized worker.) This mode of production is compatible only with a system of production, and a society, that move within narrow and more or less primitive limits. To make it last forever would be, as Pecqueur rightly says, “to decree universal mediocrity”. At a certain stage of development, it brings into being the material means of its own dissolution. From that moment new forces and new passions spring up in the heart of society; but the old social organization chains them and holds them down. It must be wiped out; it is wiped out. Its destruction is the transformation of the individualized and scattered means of production into socially concentrated ones, of the dwarf-sized property of the many into the huge property of the few. It is the expropriation of the great mass of the people from the soil, from the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), and from the means of labor. This terrible and painful expropriation of the mass of the people forms the prelude to the history of capital. It includes a series of methods of force, and of these we have reviewed only those that marked an epoch as methods of the primitive accumulation of capital. The expropriation of the immediate producers was carried out with merciless vandalism, spurred on by the most infamous, the most sordid, the pettiest, the most meanly hateful passions. Self-earned private property, which is based, so to speak, on the fusing together of the isolated, independent working individual with the conditions of his labor, is pushed out by capitalist private property, which rests on the exploitation of the nominally free labor of others, that is, on wage labor.[^1]

Polit.” t.II, p.434.)

As soon as this process of transformation has broken up the old society enough, from top to bottom; as soon as the workers are turned into proletarians (members of the proletariat, the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power), and their means of labor into capital; as soon as the capitalist mode of production stands on its own feet: then the further socialization of labor, and the further transformation of the land and other means of production into socially exploited and therefore common means of production, as well as the further expropriation of private owners, take on a new form. What is now to be expropriated is no longer the worker working for himself, but the capitalist who exploits many workers. This expropriation is carried out by the working of the built-in laws of capitalist production itself, by the centralization of capital. One capitalist always kills many. Hand in hand with this centralization, or this expropriation of many capitalists by a few, other things develop on an ever-growing scale. These are the cooperative form of the labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things), the conscious technical application of science, and the methodical cultivation of the soil. They are also the transformation of the instruments of labor into instruments of labor that can only be used in common, and the economizing of all means of production by using them as the means of production of combined, socialized labor. And they are the entanglement of all peoples in the net of the world market, and with this, the international character of the capitalist regime. Along with the constantly shrinking number of the magnates of capital, who usurp and monopolize all the advantages of this process of transformation, the mass of misery, oppression, slavery, degradation, and exploitation grows. But with this there also grows the revolt of the working class, a class always increasing in numbers, and disciplined, united, and organized by the very mechanism of the process of capitalist production itself. The monopoly of capital becomes a fetter on the mode of production that has sprung up and flourished along with it and under it. Centralization of the means of production and socialization of labor at last reach a point where they become incompatible with their capitalist shell. This shell is burst apart. The death knell of capitalist private property sounds. The expropriators are expropriated.

The capitalist mode of appropriation, which is the result of the capitalist mode of production, produces capitalist private property. This is the first negation of individual private property, as founded on the labor of the owner. But capitalist production gives birth, with the relentlessness of a law of Nature, to its own negation. It is the negation of the negation. This does not re-establish private property for the producer, but gives him individual property based on what the capitalist era has acquired: that is, on cooperation and the possession in common of the land and of the means of production.

Turning scattered private property, arising from individual labor, into capitalist private property is, naturally, a process incomparably more drawn-out, violent, and difficult than turning capitalist private property, which in practice already rests on socialized production, into socialized property. In the first case, we had the expropriation of the mass of the people by a few usurpers; in the second, we have the expropriation of a few usurpers by the mass of the people.[^2]

[^2]: The advance of industry, whose involuntary promoter is the bourgeoisie [the capitalist class], replaces the isolation of the labourers, due to competition, by their revolutionary combination, due to association. The development of Modern Industry [large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system], therefore, cuts from under its feet the very foundation on which the bourgeoisie produces and appropriates products. What the bourgeoisie, therefore, produces, above all, are its own grave-diggers. Its fall and the victory of the proletariat are equally inevitable.... Of all the classes that stand face-to-face with the bourgeoisie today, the proletariat alone is a really revolutionary class. The other classes perish and disappear in the face of Modern Industry, the proletariat is its special and essential product.... The lower middle classes, the small manufacturers, the shopkeepers, the artisan, the peasant, all these fight against the bourgeoisie, to save from extinction their existence as fractions of the middle class... they are reactionary, for they try to roll back the wheel of history. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, “Manifest der Kommunistischen Partei,” London, 1848, pp. 9, 11.
