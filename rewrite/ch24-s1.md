## Chapter 24: Conversion of Surplus-Value into Capital

### Section 1: Capitalist Production on a Progressively Increasing Scale. Transition of the Laws of Property that Characterize Production of Commodities into Laws of Capitalist Appropriation

<!-- ch24-p001 -->
So far we have investigated how surplus-value comes out of capital; now we have to see how capital arises out of surplus-value. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) Using surplus-value as capital, turning it back into capital, is called accumulation of capital.[^1]

[^1]: “Accumulation of capital; the employment of a portion of revenue as capital.” (Malthus: “Definitions, &c.,” ed. Cazenove, p. 11.) “Conversion of revenue into capital,” (Malthus: “Princ. of Pol. Econ “ 2nd Ed., London. 1836, p. 320.)

<!-- ch24-p002 -->
First let us look at this transaction from the standpoint of the individual capitalist. Suppose a spinner has advanced a capital of £10,000. Four-fifths of it (£8,000) are laid out in cotton, machinery, etc., and one-fifth (£2,000) in wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself). Let him produce 240,000 lbs. of yarn a year, with a value of £2,000. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) The rate of surplus-value is 100%. (The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital, s/v, that is, of surplus labor to necessary labor; it is also called the degree of exploitation of labor-power.) At this rate, the surplus-value lies in the surplus product, or net product, of 40,000 lbs. of yarn. This is one-sixth of the gross product, with a value of £2,000, which will be realized by a sale. £2,000 is £2,000. In this sum of money we can neither see nor smell a trace of surplus-value. When we know that a given value is surplus-value, we know how its owner came by it; but that does not change the nature either of value or of money.

<!-- ch24-p003 -->
To turn this additional sum of £2,000 into capital, the master-spinner will, all circumstances remaining as before, advance four-fifths of it (£1,600) to buy cotton, etc., and one-fifth (£400) to buy additional spinners. These spinners will find in the market the necessities of life whose value the master has advanced to them.

<!-- ch24-p004 -->
Then the new capital of £2,000 functions in the spinning mill, and in its turn brings in a surplus-value of £400.

<!-- ch24-p005 -->
The capital value was originally advanced in the money form. The surplus-value, on the other hand, is originally the value of a definite portion of the gross product. If this gross product is sold, turned into money, the capital value regains its original form. From this moment on, the capital value and the surplus-value are both sums of money, and they are turned back into capital in exactly the same way. The capitalist lays out both the one and the other to buy commodities that put him in a position to start making his goods afresh, and this time on an extended scale. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged (sold), not for the producer's own use.) But to be able to buy those commodities, he must find them ready in the market.

<!-- ch24-p006 -->
His own yarns circulate only because he brings his annual product to market, as all other capitalists likewise do with their commodities. But before coming to market, these commodities were part of the general annual product. They were part of the total mass of objects of every kind into which the sum of the individual capitals, that is, the total capital of society, had been converted in the course of the year, and of which each capitalist had in hand only a fractional part. The transactions in the market only carry out the exchange of the individual parts of this annual product among themselves, moving them from one hand to another. They can neither increase the total annual production nor change the nature of the objects produced. Hence the use that can be made of the total annual product depends entirely on its own composition, and in no way on circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying).

<!-- ch24-p007 -->
The annual production must, in the first place, supply all those objects (use-values) from which the material parts of capital used up in the course of the year have to be replaced. (A use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties.) When these objects are deducted, what remains is the net product or surplus product, in which the surplus-value lies. And what does this surplus product consist of? Only of things meant to satisfy the wants and desires of the capitalist class, things which therefore go into the capitalists' consumption fund? If that were so, the cup of surplus-value would be drained to the very dregs, and nothing but simple reproduction would ever take place. (Simple reproduction is production repeated on the same scale, with the capitalist consuming all the surplus-value.)

<!-- ch24-p008 -->
To accumulate, a portion of the surplus product must be turned into capital. But, short of a miracle, we can turn into capital only such articles as can be used in the labor process (that is, means of production), and such further articles as are suitable for keeping the worker alive (that is, means of subsistence). (The labor process is the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things.) So a part of the annual surplus labor must have been spent on producing additional means of production and subsistence, over and above the amount of these things needed to replace the capital advanced. (Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.) In a word, surplus-value can be turned into capital only because the surplus product, whose value it is, already contains the material elements of new capital.[^2]

[^2]: Here we take no account of export trade, by which a nation can change articles of luxury into either means of production or means of subsistence, and the other way round. To examine the object of our investigation intact, free from all disturbing side circumstances, we must treat the whole world as one nation, and assume that capitalist production is established everywhere and has taken possession of every branch of industry.

<!-- ch24-p009 -->
Now, for these elements actually to function as capital, the capitalist class needs additional labor. If the exploitation of the workers already employed does not increase, either extensively or intensively, then additional labor-power must be found. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) The mechanism of capitalist production provides for this in advance. It turns the working class into a class dependent on wages, a class whose ordinary wages are enough not only to maintain it but to make it grow. Capital need only combine this additional labor-power, which the working class supplies every year in the shape of workers of all ages, with the surplus means of production contained in the annual product, and the conversion of surplus-value into capital is complete. Seen concretely, accumulation comes down to the reproduction of capital on an extended scale. The circle in which simple reproduction moves changes its form and, to use Sismondi's expression, turns into a spiral.[^3]

[^3]: Sismondi's analysis of accumulation suffers from a great defect: he is content, to too great an extent, with the phrase “conversion of revenue into capital,” without getting to the bottom of the material conditions of this operation.

<!-- ch24-p010 -->
Let us now go back to our illustration. It is the old story: Abraham begat Isaac, Isaac begat Jacob, and so on. The original capital of £10,000 brings in a surplus-value of £2,000, which is capitalized. The new capital of £2,000 brings in a surplus-value of £400. This too is capitalized, turned into a second additional capital, which in its turn produces a further surplus-value of £80. And so the ball keeps rolling.

<!-- ch24-p011 -->
Here we leave out of account the portion of the surplus-value that the capitalist consumes. Just as little does it concern us, for the moment, whether the additional capital is joined on to the original capital or separated from it to function independently; or whether the same capitalist who accumulated it employs it, or hands it over to another. Only this we must not forget: alongside the newly formed capital, the original capital goes on reproducing itself and producing surplus-value, and the same is true of all accumulated capital and of the additional capital it gives rise to.

<!-- ch24-p012 -->
The original capital was formed by advancing £10,000. How did its owner come to possess it? “By his own labour and that of his forefathers,” the spokesmen of political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) answer with one voice.[^4] And in fact their assumption seems the only one consistent with the laws of the production of commodities.

[^4]: “Le travail primitif auquel son capital a dû sa naissance.” [The original labor to which his capital owed its birth] Sismondi, cited above, ed. Paris, t. I., p. 109.

<!-- ch24-p013 -->
But it is quite otherwise with the additional capital of £2,000. We know perfectly well how that came into being. There is not one single atom of its value that does not owe its existence to unpaid labor. The means of production with which the additional labor-power is combined, as well as the necessities that keep the workers alive, are nothing but component parts of the surplus product, of the tribute that the capitalist class exacts every year from the working class. Though the capitalist class uses a portion of that tribute to buy the additional labor-power even at its full price, so that equivalent is exchanged for equivalent, the transaction is still, for all that, only the old trick of every conqueror who buys commodities from the conquered with the money he has robbed from them. (Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.)

<!-- ch24-p014 -->
If the additional capital employs the person who produced it, this producer must not only go on increasing the value of the original capital, but must buy back the fruits of his earlier labor with more labor than they cost. Viewed as a transaction between the capitalist class and the working class, it makes no difference that additional workers are employed by means of the unpaid labor of the workers employed before. The capitalist may even turn the additional capital into a machine that throws the producers of that capital out of work and replaces them by a few children. In every case the working class, by the surplus labor of one year, creates the capital meant to employ additional labor in the following year.[^5] And this is what is called: creating capital out of capital.

[^5]: “Labour creates capital before capital employs labour.” E. G. Wakefield, “England and America,” London, 1833, Vol. II, p. 110.

<!-- ch24-p015 -->
The accumulation of the first additional capital of £2,000 presupposes a value of £10,000 that belonged to the capitalist by virtue of his “primitive labour,” and that he advanced. The second additional capital of £400, on the other hand, presupposes only the earlier accumulation of the £2,000, of which the £400 is the capitalized surplus-value. From then on, ownership of past unpaid labor is the only condition for appropriating living unpaid labor on a constantly increasing scale. (Living labor is the labor of workers now at work, as distinct from dead labor, the labor already embodied in means of production.) The more the capitalist has accumulated, the more he is able to accumulate.

<!-- ch24-p016 -->
Insofar as the surplus-value of which additional capital No. 1 consists is the result of buying labor-power with part of the original capital – a purchase that conformed to the laws of the exchange of commodities, and that, from a legal standpoint, presupposes nothing beyond the free disposal by the worker of his own capacities, and by the owner of money or commodities of the values that belong to him; insofar as additional capital No. 2, etc., is merely the result of No. 1, and therefore a consequence of the conditions above; insofar as each single transaction always conforms to the laws of the exchange of commodities, with the capitalist buying labor-power and the worker selling it, and, we will assume, at its real value; insofar as all this is true, clearly the laws of appropriation or of private property, laws based on the production and circulation of commodities, are turned by their own inner and inexorable dialectic into their very opposite. The exchange of equivalents, the original operation we started with, has now been turned round in such a way that there is only an apparent exchange. This is due, first, to the fact that the capital exchanged for labor-power is itself only a portion of the product of other people's labor, appropriated without an equivalent; and, second, to the fact that its producer must not only replace this capital, but replace it together with an added surplus. The relation of exchange between capitalist and worker becomes a mere appearance belonging to the process of circulation, a mere form, foreign to the real nature of the transaction, and only mystifying it. The constantly repeated purchase and sale of labor-power is now the mere form. What really takes place is this: the capitalist again and again appropriates, without an equivalent, a portion of the previously materialized labor of others, and exchanges it for a greater quantity of living labor. At first the rights of property seemed to us to be based on a man's own labor. At least, some such assumption was necessary, since only commodity-owners with equal rights faced each other, and the only means by which a man could come to possess the commodities of others was by parting with his own commodities; and these could be replaced by labor alone. Now, however, property turns out to be, on the side of the capitalist, the right to appropriate the unpaid labor of others or its product, and, on the side of the worker, the impossibility of appropriating his own product. The separation of property from labor has become the necessary consequence of a law that apparently started from their identity.[^6]

[^6]: The capitalist's property in the product of other people's labor “is a strict consequence of the law of appropriation, the fundamental principle of which was, on the contrary, the exclusive title of every labourer to the product of his own labour.” (Cherbuliez, “Richesse ou Pauvreté,” Paris, 1841, p. 58, where, however, the dialectical reversal is not properly developed.)

<!-- ch24-p017 -->
Therefore,[^7] however much the capitalist mode of appropriation may seem to fly in the face of the original laws of commodity production, it arises not from a violation of these laws but, on the contrary, from their application. Let us make this clear once more by briefly reviewing the successive phases of the movement whose culminating point is capitalist accumulation.

[^7]: The following passage (to p. 551 “laws of capitalist appropriation.”) has been added to the English text to conform with the 4th German edition.

<!-- ch24-p018 -->
We saw, in the first place, that the original conversion of a sum of values into capital was carried out in complete accordance with the laws of exchange. One party to the contract sells his labor-power, the other buys it. The seller receives the value of his commodity, and its use-value – labor – is thereby handed over to the buyer. The buyer then uses labor that equally belongs to him to transform means of production that already belong to him into a new product, which is likewise lawfully his.

<!-- ch24-p019 -->
The value of this product includes, first, the value of the used-up means of production. Useful labor (labor of a particular kind, such as tailoring or weaving, that makes a particular useful thing) cannot consume these means of production without transferring their value to the new product. But to be saleable, labor-power must be able to supply useful labor in the branch of industry in which it is to be employed.

<!-- ch24-p020 -->
The value of the new product further includes the equivalent of the value of the labor-power, together with a surplus-value. This is because the value of the labor-power – sold for a definite length of time, say a day, a week, etc. – is less than the value created by its use during that time. But the worker has received payment for the exchange-value of his labor-power (exchange-value being the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for), and by doing so has handed over its use-value – as happens in every sale and purchase.

<!-- ch24-p021 -->
This particular commodity, labor-power, has the peculiar use-value of supplying labor, and therefore of creating value; but that fact cannot affect the general law of commodity production. So if the magnitude of value advanced in wages is not merely found again in the product, but is found there increased by a surplus-value, this is not because the seller has been cheated, for he has really received the value of his commodity. It is due solely to the fact that the buyer has used this commodity up. (Magnitude of value means how much value a commodity has, measured by the socially necessary labor-time needed to produce it.)

<!-- ch24-p022 -->
The law of exchange requires equality only between the exchange-values of the commodities given in exchange for one another. From the very start it even presupposes a difference between their use-values, and it has nothing whatever to do with their consumption, which only begins after the deal is closed and carried out.

<!-- ch24-p023 -->
So the original conversion of money into capital is carried out in the most exact accordance with the economic laws of commodity production and with the right of property derived from them. Nevertheless, its result is:

<!-- ch24-p024 -->
(1) that the product belongs to the capitalist and not to the worker;

<!-- ch24-p025 -->
(2) that the value of this product includes, besides the value of the capital advanced, a surplus-value which costs the worker labor but costs the capitalist nothing, and which nonetheless becomes the legitimate property of the capitalist;

<!-- ch24-p026 -->
(3) that the worker has kept his labor-power and can sell it again if he can find a buyer.

<!-- ch24-p027 -->
Simple reproduction is only the periodic repetition of this first operation; each time, money is converted afresh into capital. So the law is not broken; on the contrary, it is merely enabled to operate continuously. “Several successive acts of exchange have only made the last represent the first” (Sismondi, “Nouveaux Principes, etc.,” p. 70).

<!-- ch24-p028 -->
And yet we have seen that simple reproduction is enough to stamp this first operation, insofar as it is thought of as an isolated process, with a totally changed character. “Of those who share the national income among themselves, the one side (the workers) acquire every year a fresh right to their share by fresh work; the others (the capitalists) have already acquired, by work done originally, a permanent right to their share” (Sismondi, cited above, pp. 110, 111). It is indeed notorious that the sphere of labor is not the only one in which primogeniture (the eldest son's right to inherit) works miracles.

<!-- ch24-p029 -->
Nor does it matter if simple reproduction is replaced by reproduction on an extended scale, by accumulation. In the first case the capitalist squanders the whole surplus-value on dissipation; in the second he shows his bourgeois virtue by consuming only a portion of it and converting the rest into money. (Bourgeois means belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it.)

<!-- ch24-p030 -->
The surplus-value is his property; it has never belonged to anyone else. If he advances it for the purposes of production, the advances come from his own funds, exactly as on the day when he first entered the market. The fact that this time the funds come from the unpaid labor of his workers makes absolutely no difference. If worker B is paid out of the surplus-value that worker A produced, then, in the first place, A supplied that surplus-value without having the just price of his commodity cut by a half-penny, and, in the second place, the transaction is no concern whatever of B's. What B claims, and has a right to claim, is that the capitalist should pay him the value of his labor-power. “Both were still gainers: the worker because he was advanced the fruits of his labour” (should read: of the unpaid labor of other workers) “before the work was done” (should read: before his own labor had borne fruit); “the employer *(le maître)* [the master], because the labour of this worker was worth more than his wages” (should read: produced more value than the value of his wages). (Sismondi, cited above, p. 135.)

<!-- ch24-p031 -->
To be sure, the matter looks quite different if we consider capitalist production in the unbroken flow of its renewal, and if, instead of the individual capitalist and the individual worker, we look at them in their totality: the capitalist class and the working class facing each other. But in doing so we would be applying standards entirely foreign to commodity production.

<!-- ch24-p032 -->
In commodity production, only buyer and seller face each other, independent of each other. The relations between them end on the day the term set in the contract they made expires. If the transaction is repeated, it is repeated as the result of a new agreement, which has nothing to do with the earlier one and which only by chance brings the same seller together again with the same buyer.

<!-- ch24-p033 -->
So if commodity production, or one of its associated processes, is to be judged according to its own economic laws, we must consider each act of exchange by itself, apart from any connection with the act of exchange before it and the one after it. And since sales and purchases are negotiated only between particular individuals, it is not admissible to look here for relations between whole social classes.

<!-- ch24-p034 -->
However long a series of periodic reproductions and earlier accumulations the capital functioning today may have passed through, it always keeps its original virginity. So long as the laws of exchange are observed in every single act of exchange, the mode of appropriation can be completely revolutionized without in any way affecting the property rights that correspond to commodity production. These same rights remain in force both at the outset, when the product belongs to its producer, who, exchanging equivalent for equivalent, can grow rich only by his own labor, and also in the period of capitalism, when social wealth becomes, to an ever-increasing degree, the property of those who are in a position to appropriate the unpaid labor of others continually and ever afresh.

<!-- ch24-p035 -->
This result becomes inevitable from the moment the worker himself freely sells labor-power as a commodity. But it is also only from then on that commodity production becomes general and the typical form of production; only from then on is every product produced for sale from the start, and does all the wealth produced pass through the sphere of circulation. Only when and where wage labor is its basis does commodity production impose itself on society as a whole; but only then and there, too, does it unfold all its hidden potential. To say that the arrival of wage labor adulterates commodity production is to say that commodity production must not develop if it is to remain unadulterated. To the extent that commodity production, in accordance with its own built-in laws, develops further into capitalist production, the property laws of commodity production change into the laws of capitalist appropriation.[^8]

[^8]: We may well, therefore, be astonished at the cleverness of Proudhon, who would abolish capitalist property by enforcing the eternal laws of property that are based on commodity production!

<!-- ch24-p036 -->
We have seen that even in the case of simple reproduction, all capital, whatever its original source, is turned into accumulated capital, capitalized surplus-value. But in the flood of production all the capital originally advanced becomes a vanishing quantity *(magnitudo evanescens* [vanishing magnitude], in the mathematical sense) compared with the directly accumulated capital, that is, with the surplus-value or surplus product that is turned back into capital, whether it functions in the hands of the one who accumulated it or in the hands of others. Hence political economy describes capital in general as “accumulated wealth” (converted surplus-value or revenue), “that is employed over again in the production of surplus-value,”[^9] and the capitalist as “the owner of surplus-value.”[^10] It is merely another way of expressing the same thing to say that all existing capital is accumulated or capitalized interest, for interest is a mere fragment of surplus-value.[^11]

[^9]: “Capital, viz., accumulated wealth employed with a view to profit.” (Malthus, cited above.) “Capital ... consists of wealth saved from revenue, and used with a view to profit.” (R. Jones: “An Introductory Lecture on Polit. Econ.,” London, 1833, p. 16.)

[^10]: “The possessors of surplus-produce or capital.” (“The Source and Remedy of the National Difficulties. A Letter to Lord John Russell.” London, 1821.)

[^11]: “Capital, with compound interest on every portion of capital saved, is so all engrossing that all the wealth in the world from which income is derived, has long ago become the interest on capital.” (London, *Economist,* 19th July, 1851.)
