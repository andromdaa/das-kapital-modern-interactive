### Section 2: The Production of Surplus-Value

<!-- ch07-p025 -->
The product that the capitalist appropriates (takes as his own) is a use-value, such as yarn, for example, or boots. Boots are, in one sense, the basis of all social progress, and our capitalist is a decided “progressist.” But even so, he does not manufacture boots for their own sake. In the production of commodities, use-value is by no means the thing “qu’on aime pour lui-même” [that one loves for its own sake]. Capitalists produce use-values only because, and only insofar as, they are the material base, the containers, of exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for). Our capitalist has two aims in view. First, he wants to produce a use-value that has an exchange-value, that is, an article meant to be sold: a commodity. Second, he wants to produce a commodity whose value will be greater than the sum of the values of the commodities used to produce it, that is, of the means of production and the labor-power that he bought with his good money on the open market. His aim is to produce not only a use-value, but also a commodity; not only use-value, but value; and not only value, but surplus-value at the same time (surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it).

<!-- ch07-p026 -->
We must keep in mind that we are now dealing with the production of commodities, and that so far we have looked at only one aspect of the process. Just as commodities are at the same time use-values and values, so the process of producing them must be a labor process and, at the same time, a process of creating value.[^11]

[^11]: As was stated in an earlier note, the English language has two different words for these two different aspects of labor. In the simple labor process, the process of producing use-values, it is *Work;* in the process of creating value, it is *Labour,* taking the term in its strictly economic sense. — *F. E.*

<!-- ch07-p027 -->
Let us now examine production as a creation of value.

<!-- ch07-p028 -->
We know that the value of each commodity is determined by the quantity of labor spent on it and materialized in it, that is, by the working time necessary, under given social conditions, to produce it. This rule also holds for the product that came to our capitalist as the result of the labor process carried on for him. Let us assume this product is 10 lbs. of yarn. Our first step is to calculate the quantity of labor realized in it.

<!-- ch07-p029 -->
To spin the yarn, raw material is needed; suppose in this case 10 lbs. of cotton. For now we have no need to investigate the value of this cotton, because our capitalist has, we will assume, bought it at its full value, say ten shillings. In this price, the labor needed to produce the cotton is already expressed in terms of the average labor of society. (A price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) We will further assume that the wear and tear of the spindle amounts to a value of 2s. For our present purpose, the spindle may stand for all the other instruments of labor used. So if twenty-four hours’ labor, or two working days, are needed to produce the quantity of gold represented by twelve shillings, then we have here, to begin with, two days’ labor already embodied in the yarn.

<!-- ch07-p030 -->
We must not let ourselves be misled by the fact that the cotton has taken a new shape, while the substance of the spindle has been used up to a certain extent. By the general law of value, if the value of 40 lbs. of yarn = the value of 40 lbs. of cotton + the value of a whole spindle, that is, if the same working time is needed to produce the commodities on either side of this equation, then 10 lbs. of yarn are an equivalent for 10 lbs. of cotton together with one-fourth of a spindle. In the case we are looking at, the same working time is materialized in the 10 lbs. of yarn on the one hand, and in the 10 lbs. of cotton and the fraction of a spindle on the other. So whether value appears in cotton, in a spindle, or in yarn makes no difference to the amount of that value. The spindle and cotton do not rest quietly side by side. Instead, they join together in the process, their forms are altered, and they are turned into yarn. But this affects their value no more than it would if they had simply been exchanged for their equivalent in yarn.

<!-- ch07-p031 -->
The labor needed to produce the cotton, the raw material of the yarn, is part of the labor necessary to produce the yarn, and so it is contained in the yarn. The same applies to the labor embodied in the spindle, since without the spindle’s wear and tear the cotton could not be spun.

<!-- ch07-p032 -->
Hence, in determining the value of the yarn, or the labor-time needed to produce it, all the special processes may together be looked on as different and successive phases of one and the same process. These are the processes, carried on at various times and in different places, that were necessary first to produce the cotton and the worn-away part of the spindle, and then to spin the yarn with the cotton and spindle. All the labor in the yarn is past labor. It is of no importance that the operations needed to produce its component elements were carried on at times further back from the present than the final operation of spinning. Suppose a definite quantity of labor, say thirty days, is needed to build a house. The total amount of labor embodied in it is not changed by the fact that the work of the last day is done twenty-nine days later than that of the first. Therefore the labor contained in the raw material and the instruments of labor can be treated just as if it were labor spent at an earlier stage of the spinning process, before the actual spinning began.

<!-- ch07-p033 -->
The values of the means of production, that is, of the cotton and the spindle, which are expressed in the price of twelve shillings, are therefore component parts of the value of the yarn, or, in other words, of the value of the product.

<!-- ch07-p034 -->
Two conditions must nevertheless be met. First, the cotton and spindle must work together to produce a use-value; in the present case they must become yarn. Value does not depend on the particular use-value that carries it, but it must be embodied in a use-value of some kind. Second, the time taken up by the labor of production must not exceed the time really necessary under the given social conditions of the case. So if no more than 1 lb. of cotton is needed to spin 1 lb. of yarn, care must be taken that no more than this weight of cotton is used up in producing 1 lb. of yarn; and the same goes for the spindle. Suppose the capitalist has a hobby and uses a gold spindle instead of a steel one. Even so, the only labor that counts for anything in the value of the yarn is the labor that would be needed to produce a steel spindle, because no more is necessary under the given social conditions.

<!-- ch07-p035 -->
We now know what part of the value of the yarn is due to the cotton and the spindle. It amounts to twelve shillings, or the value of two days’ work. The next point to consider is what part of the value of the yarn is added to the cotton by the labor of the spinner.

<!-- ch07-p036 -->
We must now look at this labor from a very different angle from the one it had during the labor process. There, we viewed it only as the particular kind of human activity that changes cotton into yarn. There, the better suited the labor was to the work, the better the yarn, other things being equal. The spinner’s labor was then seen as specifically different from other kinds of productive labor. It was different, on the one hand, in its special aim, namely spinning. On the other hand, it was different in the special character of its operations, in the special nature of its means of production, and in the special use-value of its product. Cotton and spindles are a necessity for spinning, but for making rifled cannon they would be of no use whatever. Here, on the contrary, we consider the spinner’s labor only insofar as it creates value, that is, only as a source of value. Seen this way, his labor does not differ in any respect from the labor of the man who bores cannon, or (what concerns us more closely here) from the labor of the cotton-planter and the spindle-maker embodied in the means of production. It is only because of this sameness that cotton planting, spindle making, and spinning can form the parts of one whole, parts that differ from each other only in quantity. That whole is the value of the yarn. Here we no longer have anything to do with the quality, the nature, and the specific character of the labor, but only with its quantity. And this simply has to be calculated. We proceed on the assumption that spinning is simple, unskilled labor, the average labor of a given state of society. Later we shall see that the opposite assumption would make no difference.

<!-- ch07-p037 -->
While the worker is at work, his labor constantly undergoes a transformation: from being motion, it becomes an object without motion; from being the worker working, it becomes the thing produced. At the end of one hour’s spinning, that act is represented by a definite quantity of yarn. In other words, a definite quantity of labor, namely one hour’s labor, has become embodied in the cotton. We say labor, that is, the spinner’s spending of his vital force, and not spinning labor. This is because the special work of spinning counts here only insofar as it is the expenditure of labor-power in general, and not insofar as it is the specific work of the spinner.

<!-- ch07-p038 -->
In the process we are now considering, it is extremely important that no more time be spent in turning the cotton into yarn than is necessary under the given social conditions. Suppose that under normal, that is, average social conditions of production, *a* pounds of cotton ought to be made into *b* pounds of yarn by one hour’s labor. Then a day’s labor does not count as 12 hours’ labor unless 12 *a* pounds of cotton have been made into 12 *b* pounds of yarn. For in the creation of value, only the time that is socially necessary counts.

<!-- ch07-p039 -->
Not only the labor, but also the raw material and the product now appear in quite a new light, very different from the one in which we saw them in the labor process pure and simple. The raw material now serves merely to absorb a definite quantity of labor. By this absorption it is in fact changed into yarn, because it is spun, because labor-power in the form of spinning is added to it. But the product, the yarn, is now nothing more than a measure of the labor absorbed by the cotton. If in one hour 1 2/3 lbs. of cotton can be spun into 1 2/3 lbs. of yarn, then 10 lbs. of yarn indicate the absorption of 6 hours’ labor. Definite quantities of product, quantities fixed by experience, now represent nothing but definite quantities of labor, definite masses of crystallized labor-time. They are nothing more than the materialization of so many hours or so many days of social labor.

<!-- ch07-p040 -->
Here we are no more concerned with the facts that the labor is the specific work of spinning, that its object is cotton, and that its product is yarn, than we are with the fact that the object itself is already a product, and therefore raw material. If the spinner were working in a coal mine instead of spinning, the object of his labor, the coal, would be supplied by Nature. Nevertheless, a definite quantity of extracted coal, a hundredweight for example, would represent a definite quantity of absorbed labor.

<!-- ch07-p041 -->
When the labor-power was sold, we assumed that the value of a day’s labor-power is three shillings, and that six hours’ labor is embodied in that sum. We assumed, therefore, that this amount of labor is needed to produce the necessaries of life that the worker requires daily on average. Now if our spinner, by working for one hour, can turn 1 2/3 lbs. of cotton into 1 2/3 lbs. of yarn,[^12] it follows that in six hours he will turn 10 lbs. of cotton into 10 lbs. of yarn. Hence, during the spinning process, the cotton absorbs six hours’ labor. The same quantity of labor is also embodied in a piece of gold worth three shillings. Consequently, the mere labor of spinning adds a value of three shillings to the cotton.

[^12]: These figures are quite arbitrary.

<!-- ch07-p042 -->
Let us now consider the total value of the product, the 10 lbs. of yarn. Two and a half days’ labor has been embodied in it. Two of these days were contained in the cotton and in the substance of the spindle that was worn away, and half a day was absorbed during the spinning process. This two and a half days’ labor is also represented by a piece of gold worth fifteen shillings. Hence fifteen shillings is an adequate price for the 10 lbs. of yarn, or the price of one pound is eighteenpence.

<!-- ch07-p043 -->
Our capitalist stares in astonishment. The value of the product is exactly equal to the value of the capital advanced. The value advanced has not expanded, no surplus-value has been created, and so money has not been turned into capital. The price of the yarn is fifteen shillings, and fifteen shillings were spent on the open market on the component elements of the product, or, what amounts to the same thing, on the factors of the labor process: ten shillings were paid for the cotton, two shillings for the substance of the spindle worn away, and three shillings for the labor-power. The swollen value of the yarn is no use, for it is merely the sum of the values that already existed in the cotton, the spindle, and the labor-power. Out of such a simple addition of existing values, no surplus-value can possibly arise.[^13] These separate values are now all concentrated in one thing. But so they were in the sum of fifteen shillings, too, before the purchase of the commodities split it into three parts.

[^13]: This is the fundamental proposition on which the Physiocrats based their doctrine that all labor that is not agriculture is unproductive. For the orthodox economist it is irrefutable. “Cette façon d’imputer à une seule chose la valeur de plusieurs autres” (par exemple au lin la consommation du tisserand), “d’appliquer, pour ainsi dire, couche sur couche, plusieurs valeurs sur une seule, fait que celle-ci grossit d’autant.... Le terme d’addition peint trés bien la maniere dont se forme le prix des ouvrages de main d’oeuvre; ce prix n’est qu’un total de plusieurs valeurs consommées et additionnées ensemble; or, additionner n’est pas multiplier.” [“This way of attributing to one single thing the value of several others” (for example, attributing to the flax the consumption of the weaver), “of applying, so to speak, layer upon layer, several values onto a single one, makes that one grow by just as much.... The term ‘addition’ paints very well the way in which the price of handmade goods is formed; this price is only a total of several values consumed and added together; but adding is not multiplying.”] (“Mercier de la Rivière,” cited above, p. 599.)

<!-- ch07-p044 -->
There is really nothing very strange in this result. Since the value of one pound of yarn is eighteenpence, if our capitalist buys 10 lbs. of yarn on the market, he must pay fifteen shillings for them. Clearly, whether a man buys his house ready built or has it built for him, in neither case will the way he acquires it increase the amount of money spent on the house.

<!-- ch07-p045 -->
Our capitalist, who is at home in his vulgar economics (economics that only describes and defends the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations), exclaims: “Oh! but I advanced my money for the express purpose of making more money.” The road to Hell is paved with good intentions, and he might just as easily have intended to make money without producing at all.[^14] He threatens all sorts of things. He won’t be caught napping again. In future he will buy the commodities on the market, instead of manufacturing them himself. But if all his brother capitalists were to do the same, where would he find his commodities on the market? And he cannot eat his money. He tries persuasion. “Consider my abstinence. I might have played ducks and drakes with (frittered away) the 15 shillings; but instead I consumed it productively and made yarn with it.” Very well, and as his reward he now has good yarn instead of a bad conscience. As for playing the part of a miser, it would never do for him to slip back into such bad ways as that; we have seen before where such asceticism leads. Besides, where there is nothing, the king has lost his rights. Whatever the merit of his abstinence may be, there is nothing with which to reward it specially, because the value of the product is merely the sum of the values of the commodities that were thrown into the process of production. So let him console himself with the thought that virtue is its own reward. But no, he becomes insistent. He says: “The yarn is of no use to me: I produced it for sale.” In that case let him sell it. Or, better still, let him in future produce only things to satisfy his personal wants, a remedy that his physician MacCulloch has already prescribed as infallible against an epidemic of over-production. Now he gets obstinate. “Can the worker,” he asks, “produce commodities out of nothing, merely with his arms and legs? Did I not supply him with the materials by means of which, and in which alone, his labor could be embodied? And as the greater part of society consists of such ne’er-do-wells, have I not done society an incalculable service with my instruments of production, my cotton and my spindle? And not only society, but the worker too, whom I have also provided with the necessaries of life? And am I to be allowed nothing in return for all this service?” Well, but has not the worker done him the equivalent service of changing his cotton and spindle into yarn? Besides, there is no question of service here.[^15] A service is nothing more than the useful effect of a use-value, whether of a commodity or of labor.[^16] But here we are dealing with exchange-value. The capitalist paid the worker a value of 3 shillings, and the worker gave him back an exact equivalent: the value of 3 shillings that he added to the cotton. He gave him value for value. Our friend, until now so purse-proud, suddenly takes on the modest manner of his own worker, and exclaims: “Have I myself not worked? Have I not performed the labor of superintendence, of overseeing the spinner? And does not this labor, too, create value?” His overseer and his manager try to hide their smiles. Meanwhile, after a hearty laugh, he puts on his usual face again. He has chanted the whole creed of the economists to us, but in reality, he says, he would not give a brass farthing for it. He leaves this and all such evasions and juggling tricks to the professors of political economy (the science of economics as it was then called), who are paid for it. He himself is a practical man. He does not always consider what he says outside his business, but in his business he knows what he is about.

[^14]: Thus from 1844-47 he withdrew part of his capital from productive use in order to throw it away on railway speculation. And so too, during the American Civil War, he closed his factory and turned his workers out into the streets in order to gamble on the Liverpool cotton exchange.

[^15]: “Extol thyself, put on finery and adorn thyself ... but whoever takes more or better than he gives, that is usury, and is not service, but wrong done to his neighbour, as when one steals and robs. All is not service and benefit to a neighbour that is called service and benefit. For an adulteress and adulterer do one another great service and pleasure. A horseman does an incendiary [arsonist] a great service, by helping him to rob on the highway, and pillage land and houses. The papists do ours a great service, in that they don’t drown, burn, murder all of them, or let them all rot in prison; but let some live, and only drive them out, or take from them what they have. The devil himself does his servants inestimable service.... To sum up, the world is full of great, excellent, and daily service and benefit.” (Martin Luther: “An die Pfarrherrn wider den Wucher zu predigen,” Wittenberg, 1540.)

[^16]: In “Zur Kritik der Pol. Oek.,” p. 14, I make the following remark on this point: “It is not difficult to understand what ‘service’ the category ‘service’ must render to a class of economists like J. B. Say and F. Bastiat.”

<!-- ch07-p046 -->
Let us examine the matter more closely. The value of a day’s labor-power amounts to 3 shillings because, on our assumption, half a day’s labor is embodied in that quantity of labor-power; that is, because the means of subsistence needed daily for the production of labor-power cost half a day’s labor. But the past labor that is embodied in the labor-power and the living labor that it can set in motion, the daily cost of maintaining it and its daily expenditure in work, are two totally different things. The former determines the exchange-value of the labor-power; the latter is its use-value. The fact that half a day’s labor is necessary to keep the worker alive for 24 hours does not in any way prevent him from working a whole day. Therefore the value of labor-power, and the value which that labor-power creates in the labor process, are two entirely different magnitudes. This difference between the two values was what the capitalist had in view when he was buying the labor-power. The useful qualities that labor-power possesses, by which it makes yarn or boots, were to him nothing more than an indispensable condition; for in order to create value, labor must be spent in a useful way. What really influenced him was the specific use-value that this commodity possesses, of being *a source not only of value, but of more value than it has itself.* This is the special service that the capitalist expects from labor-power, and in this transaction he acts in accordance with the “eternal laws” of the exchange of commodities. The seller of labor-power, like the seller of any other commodity, realizes its exchange-value and parts with its use-value. He cannot take the one without giving the other. The use-value of labor-power, in other words labor, belongs just as little to its seller as the use-value of oil, once sold, belongs to the dealer who sold it. The owner of the money has paid the value of a day’s labor-power; so the use of it for a day is his; a day’s labor belongs to him. On the one hand, the daily upkeep of labor-power costs only half a day’s labor. On the other hand, the very same labor-power can work for a whole day, and consequently the value that its use creates during one day is double what he pays for that use. This circumstance is, without doubt, a piece of good luck for the buyer, but by no means an injury to the seller.

<!-- ch07-p047 -->
Our capitalist foresaw this state of things, and that was the cause of his laughter. So the worker finds in the workshop the means of production needed for working not only six hours, but twelve. Just as during the six hours’ process our 10 lbs. of cotton absorbed six hours’ labor and became 10 lbs. of yarn, so now 20 lbs. of cotton will absorb 12 hours’ labor and be changed into 20 lbs. of yarn. Let us now examine the product of this prolonged process. In these 20 lbs. of yarn the labor of five days is now materialized. Four days are due to the cotton and the lost steel of the spindle, and the remaining day was absorbed by the cotton during the spinning process. Expressed in gold, the labor of five days is thirty shillings. This is therefore the price of the 20 lbs. of yarn, giving, as before, eighteenpence as the price of a pound. But the sum of the values of the commodities that went into the process amounts to 27 shillings. The value of the yarn is 30 shillings. Therefore the value of the product is 1/9 greater than the value advanced for its production; 27 shillings have been transformed into 30 shillings; a surplus-value of 3 shillings has been created. The trick has worked at last; money has been turned into capital.

<!-- ch07-p048 -->
Every condition of the problem is satisfied, and the laws that regulate the exchange of commodities have been in no way violated. Equivalent has been exchanged for equivalent. For the capitalist, as buyer, paid its full value for each commodity: for the cotton, the spindle, and the labor-power. He then did what every buyer of commodities does: he consumed their use-value. The consumption of the labor-power, which was also the process of producing commodities, resulted in 20 lbs. of yarn with a value of 30 shillings. The capitalist, formerly a buyer, now returns to market as a seller of commodities. He sells his yarn at eighteenpence a pound, which is its exact value. Yet for all that, he draws 3 shillings more out of circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying) than he originally threw into it. This metamorphosis, this conversion of money into capital, takes place both inside the sphere of circulation and outside it. It takes place inside circulation, because it depends on the purchase of the labor-power in the market. It takes place outside circulation, because what is done inside it is only a stepping-stone to the production of surplus-value, a process entirely confined to the sphere of production. Thus “tout est pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles.” [“Everything is for the best in the best of all possible worlds.” – Voltaire, *Candide*]

<!-- ch07-p049 -->
By turning his money into commodities that serve as the material elements of a new product and as factors in the labor process, and by joining living labor to their dead substance, the capitalist at the same time turns value, that is, past, materialized, dead labor, into capital: into value pregnant with value, a live monster that is fruitful and multiplies.

<!-- ch07-p050 -->
If we now compare the two processes, producing value and creating surplus-value, we see that the second is nothing but the continuation of the first beyond a definite point. On the one hand, if the process is not carried beyond the point where the value paid by the capitalist for the labor-power is replaced by an exact equivalent, it is simply a process of producing value. On the other hand, if it is continued beyond that point, it becomes a process of creating surplus-value.

<!-- ch07-p051 -->
If we go further, and compare the process of producing value with the labor process pure and simple, we find that the labor process consists of the useful labor, the work, that produces use-values. Here we look at the labor as producing a particular article; we view it in its qualitative aspect alone, with regard to its end and aim. But viewed as a value-creating process, the same labor process presents itself in its quantitative aspect alone. Here it is merely a question of the time the worker takes to do the work, of the period during which the labor-power is usefully spent. Here, the commodities that take part in the process no longer count as necessary aids to labor-power in producing a definite, useful object. They count merely as containers of so much absorbed or materialized labor. That labor may have been embodied in the means of production before, or it may be incorporated in them for the first time during the process by the action of labor-power. In either case it counts only according to its duration: it amounts to so many hours or days, as the case may be.

<!-- ch07-p052 -->
Moreover, only as much of the time spent in producing any article is counted as is necessary under the given social conditions. This has various consequences. In the first place, the labor must be carried on under normal conditions. If a self-acting mule is the implement in general use for spinning, it would be absurd to supply the spinner with a distaff and spinning wheel. The cotton, too, must not be such rubbish as to cause extra waste in being worked; it must be of suitable quality. Otherwise the spinner would turn out to spend more time producing a pound of yarn than is socially necessary, and in that case the excess time would create neither value nor money. But whether the material factors of the process are of normal quality or not depends not on the worker, but entirely on the capitalist. Then again, the labor-power itself must be of average effectiveness. In the trade in which it is employed, it must have the average skill, dexterity, and speed usual in that trade, and our capitalist took good care to buy labor-power of such normal quality. This power must be applied with the average amount of effort and with the usual degree of intensity. The capitalist is as careful to see that this is done as he is to see that his workers are not idle for a single moment. He has bought the use of the labor-power for a definite period, and he insists on his rights. He has no intention of being robbed. Lastly, all wasteful consumption of raw material or instruments of labor is strictly forbidden, and for this purpose our friend has a penal code of his own. For what is wasted in this way represents labor spent needlessly, labor that does not count in the product or enter into its value.[^17]

[^17]: This is one of the circumstances that make production by slave labor such a costly process. Here the worker, to use a striking expression of the ancients, is distinguished only as instrumentum vocale [speaking tool] from an animal as instrumentum semi-vocale [half-speaking tool], and from an implement as instrumentum mutum [mute tool]. But he himself takes care to let both beast and implement feel that he is none of them, but is a man. He convinces himself, with immense satisfaction, that he is a different being, by treating the one mercilessly and damaging the other lovingly. Hence the principle, applied everywhere in this method of production, of using only the crudest and heaviest implements, ones that are hard to damage because of their sheer clumsiness. In the slave states bordering on the Gulf of Mexico, right up to the date of the Civil War, the only plows to be found were built on old Chinese models, which turned up the soil like a hog or a mole instead of making furrows. Compare J. E. Cairnes. “The Slave Power,” London, 1862, p. 46 and following. In his “Sea Board Slave States,” Olmsted tells us: “I am here shown tools that no man in his senses, with us, would allow a labourer, for whom he was paying wages, to be encumbered with; and the excessive weight and clumsiness of which, I would judge, would make work at least ten per cent greater than with those ordinarily used with us. And I am assured that, in the careless and clumsy way they must be used by the slaves, anything lighter or less rude could not be furnished them with good economy, and that such tools as we constantly give our labourers and find our profit in giving them, would not last out a day in a Virginia cornfield – much lighter and more free from stones though it be than ours. So, too, when I ask why mules are so universally substituted for horses on the farm, the first reason given, and confessedly the most conclusive one, is that horses cannot bear the treatment that they always must get from negroes; horses are always soon foundered [lamed] or crippled by them, while mules will bear cudgelling [beating with a club], or lose a meal or two now and then, and not be materially injured, and they do not take cold or get sick, if neglected or overworked. But I do not need to go further than to the window of the room in which I am writing, to see at almost any time, treatment of cattle that would ensure the immediate discharge of the driver by almost any farmer owning them in the North.”

<!-- ch07-p053 -->
We now see that the difference between labor considered on the one hand as producing useful things, and on the other hand as creating value, a difference we discovered by our analysis of a commodity, comes down to a distinction between two aspects of the process of production.

<!-- ch07-p054 -->
The process of production, considered on the one hand as the unity of the labor process and the process of creating value, is production of commodities. Considered on the other hand as the unity of the labor process and the process of producing surplus-value, it is the capitalist process of production, or capitalist production of commodities.

<!-- ch07-p055 -->
We stated on an earlier page that in the creation of surplus-value it does not matter in the least whether the labor the capitalist appropriates is simple unskilled labor of average quality or more complicated skilled labor. All labor of a higher or more complicated character than average labor is the expenditure of a more costly kind of labor-power: labor-power whose production has cost more time and labor, and which therefore has a higher value, than unskilled or simple labor-power. Since this power has a higher value, its consumption is labor of a higher class, labor that creates proportionally higher values in equal times than unskilled labor does. Whatever difference in skill there may be between the labor of a spinner and that of a jeweler, the part of his labor by which the jeweler merely replaces the value of his own labor-power does not differ in quality in any way from the additional part by which he creates surplus-value. In making jewelry, just as in spinning, the surplus-value results only from a quantitative excess of labor, from lengthening one and the same labor process: in the one case the process of making jewels, in the other the process of making yarn.[^18]

[^18]: The distinction between skilled and unskilled labor rests partly on pure illusion, or, to say the least, on distinctions that long ago ceased to be real and survive only through traditional convention. It rests partly on the helpless condition of some groups of the working class, a condition that prevents them from extracting the value of their labor-power as fully as the rest do. Accidental circumstances play so great a part here that these two forms of labor sometimes change places. Take, for instance, places where the physique of the working class has deteriorated and is, relatively speaking, exhausted, which is the case in all countries with a well-developed capitalist production. There, the lower forms of labor, which demand great expenditure of muscle, are in general considered skilled compared with much more delicate forms of labor, and the latter sink down to the level of unskilled labor. Take as an example the labor of a bricklayer, which in England ranks much higher than that of a damask-weaver. Again, although the labor of a fustian cutter demands great bodily effort and is unhealthy as well, it counts only as unskilled labor. And then we must not forget that so-called skilled labor does not take up a large space in the field of national labor. Laing estimates that in England (and Wales) the livelihood of 11,300,000 people depends on unskilled labor. If from the total population of 18,000,000 living at the time when he wrote we deduct 1,000,000 for the “genteel population,” 1,500,000 for paupers, vagrants, criminals, prostitutes, etc., and 4,650,000 who make up the middle class, there remain the above-mentioned 11,000,000. But in his middle class he includes people who live on the interest of small investments, officials, writers, artists, schoolmasters, and the like, and to swell the number he also includes among these 4,650,000 the better-paid part of the factory workers! The bricklayers, too, figure among them. (S. Laing: “National Distress,” etc., London, 1844). “The great class who have nothing to give for food but ordinary labour, are the great bulk of the people.” (James Mill, in art.: “Colony,” Supplement to the Encyclop. Brit., 1831.)

But on the other hand, in every process of creating value, the reduction of skilled labor to average social labor, for example of one day of skilled labor to six days of unskilled labor, is unavoidable.[^19] So we spare ourselves an unnecessary operation, and simplify our analysis, by assuming that the labor of the worker employed by the capitalist is unskilled average labor.

[^19]: “Where reference is made to labour as a measure of value, it necessarily implies labour of one particular kind ... the proportion which the other kinds bear to it being easily ascertained.” (“Outlines of Pol. Econ.,” London, 1832, pp. 22 and 23.)
