# Part 5: Production of Absolute and Relative Surplus-Value

## Chapter 16: Absolute and Relative Surplus- Value

<!-- ch16-p001 -->
In considering the labor process, we began (see Chapter VII.) by treating it in the abstract, apart from its historical forms, as a process between man and Nature. (The labor process is the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things.) We stated there: “If we examine the whole labour-process, from the point of view of its result, it is plain that both the instruments and the subject of labour [the material worked on] are means of production, and that the labour itself is productive labour.” (Means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things.) And in Note 2, on the same page, we added: “This method of determining, from the standpoint of the labour-process alone, what is productive labour, is by no means directly applicable to the case of the capitalist process of production.” We now go on to develop this subject further.

<!-- ch16-p002 -->
To the extent that the labor process is purely individual, one and the same worker unites in himself all the functions that later become separated. When an individual takes hold of natural objects to make his living, no one controls him but himself. Later he is controlled by others. A single man cannot act upon Nature without calling his own muscles into play under the control of his own brain. Just as in the natural body head and hand serve each other, so the labor process unites the labor of the hand with that of the head. Later on they part company and even become deadly enemies. The product stops being the direct product of the individual and becomes a social product, produced in common by a collective worker, that is, by a combination of workers, each of whom takes only a part, greater or smaller, in handling the object of their labor. As the cooperative character of the labor process becomes more and more marked, our notion of productive labor, and of its agent, the productive worker, necessarily becomes wider as a consequence. To labor productively, it is no longer necessary for you to do manual work yourself; it is enough if you are an organ of the collective worker and perform one of its subordinate functions. The first definition of productive labor given above, a definition drawn from the very nature of the production of material objects, still remains correct for the collective worker, considered as a whole. But it no longer holds good for each member taken individually.

<!-- ch16-p003 -->
On the other hand, however, our notion of productive labor also becomes narrower. Capitalist production is not merely the production of commodities; it is essentially the production of surplus-value. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use. Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) The worker produces, not for himself, but for capital. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) So it is no longer enough that he should simply produce. He must produce surplus-value. Only the worker who produces surplus-value for the capitalist, and so works for the self-expansion of capital, is productive. If we may take an example from outside the sphere of producing material objects: a schoolmaster is a productive worker when, besides belaboring the heads of his pupils, he works like a horse to enrich the owner of the school. That the owner has laid out his capital in a teaching factory instead of a sausage factory does not change the relation. So the notion of a productive worker implies not merely a relation between work and useful effect, between worker and product of labor, but also a specific, social relation of production. It is a relation that has arisen historically and that stamps the worker as the direct means of creating surplus-value. To be a productive worker is therefore not a piece of luck, but a misfortune. In Book IV, which deals with the history of the theory, it will be seen more clearly that the classical political economists have at all times made the production of surplus-value the distinguishing mark of the productive worker. (Political economy is the science of economics as it was then called; classical political economy means the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production.) Hence their definition of a productive worker changes with their understanding of the nature of surplus-value. Thus the Physiocrats insist that only agricultural labor is productive, since that alone, they say, yields a surplus-value. And they say so because, for them, surplus-value exists only in the form of rent.

<!-- ch16-p004 -->
Lengthening the working day beyond the point at which the worker would have produced just an equivalent for the value of his labor-power, and capital's appropriation of that surplus labor: this is the production of absolute surplus-value. (The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work. Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) The production of absolute surplus-value forms the general groundwork of the capitalist system, and the starting-point for the production of relative surplus-value. Relative surplus-value presupposes that the working day is already divided into two parts, necessary labor and surplus labor. To lengthen the surplus labor, the necessary labor is shortened by methods that let the equivalent of the wages be produced in less time. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) The production of absolute surplus-value turns solely on the length of the working day; the production of relative surplus-value revolutionizes through and through the technical processes of labor and the makeup of society. It therefore presupposes a specific mode, the capitalist mode of production. This mode, along with its methods, means, and conditions, arises and develops spontaneously on the foundation provided by the formal subjection of labor to capital. In the course of this development, the formal subjection is replaced by the real subjection of labor to capital.

<!-- ch16-p005 -->
It will be enough merely to mention certain intermediate forms, in which surplus labor is not squeezed out of the producer by direct compulsion, nor has the producer himself yet been formally subjected to capital. In such forms capital has not yet gained direct control of the labor process. Alongside independent producers who carry on their handicrafts and agriculture in the traditional old-fashioned way, there stands the usurer or the merchant, with his usurer's capital or merchant's capital, feeding on them like a parasite. Where this form of exploitation predominates in a society, it rules out the capitalist mode of production; but this form may serve as a transition to that mode, as it did toward the close of the Middle Ages. Finally, as modern “domestic industry” shows, some intermediate forms are reproduced here and there in the background of modern industry, though their whole appearance is totally changed. (Modern industry is large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system.)

<!-- ch16-p006 -->
On the one hand, the mere formal subjection of labor to capital is enough for the production of absolute surplus-value. For example, it is enough that craftsmen who previously worked on their own account, or as apprentices of a master, should become wage workers under the direct control of a capitalist. On the other hand, we have seen how the methods of producing relative surplus-value are at the same time methods of producing absolute surplus-value. Indeed, more than that: the excessive lengthening of the working day turned out to be the peculiar product of modern industry. Generally speaking, the specifically capitalist mode of production stops being a mere means of producing relative surplus-value as soon as that mode has conquered an entire branch of production, and still more so as soon as it has conquered all the important branches. It then becomes the general, socially predominant form of production. As a special method of producing relative surplus-value, it remains effective only, first, insofar as it seizes on industries that previously were only formally subject to capital, that is, insofar as it is propagandist; and second, insofar as the industries it has already taken over continue to be revolutionized by changes in the methods of production.

<!-- ch16-p007 -->
From one standpoint, any distinction between absolute and relative surplus-value appears illusory. Relative surplus-value is absolute, since it compels the absolute lengthening of the working day beyond the labor-time necessary for the worker's own existence. Absolute surplus-value is relative, since it requires such a development of the productivity of labor as will allow the necessary labor-time to be confined to a part of the working day. (The productivity of labor is how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time.) But if we keep in mind how surplus-value behaves, this appearance of identity vanishes. Once the capitalist mode of production is established and has become general, the difference between absolute and relative surplus-value makes itself felt whenever there is a question of raising the rate of surplus-value. (The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital (s/v), that is, of surplus labor to necessary labor; it is also called the degree of exploitation of labor-power.) Assuming that labor-power is paid for at its value, we face this alternative. Given the productivity of labor and its normal intensity, the rate of surplus-value can be raised only by actually lengthening the working day. On the other hand, given the length of the working day, that rise can be brought about only by a change in the relative magnitudes of the parts of the working day, namely necessary labor and surplus labor. And if wages are not to fall below the value of labor-power, such a change presupposes a change either in the productivity or in the intensity of labor.

<!-- ch16-p008 -->
If the worker needs all his time to produce the necessary means of subsistence for himself and his kind, he has no time left in which to work free of charge for others. (Means of subsistence are the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live.) Without a certain degree of productivity in his labor, he has no such spare time at his disposal. Without such spare time, there is no surplus labor, and therefore no capitalists, no slave-owners, no feudal lords, in a word, no class of large proprietors.[^1]

[^1]: “The very existence of the master-capitalists, as a distinct class, is dependent on the productiveness of industry.” (Ramsay, cited above, p. 206.) “If each man’s labour were but enough to produce his own food, there could be no property.” (Ravenstone, cited above, p. 14, 15.)

<!-- ch16-p009 -->
So we may say that surplus-value rests on a natural basis. But this is permissible only in the very general sense that there is no natural obstacle absolutely preventing one man from unloading from himself the labor needed for his own existence and loading it onto another, any more than, for instance, unconquerable natural obstacles prevent one man from eating the flesh of another.[^2] No mystical ideas must in any way be attached to this historically developed productivity of labor, as sometimes happens. Only after men have raised themselves above the level of animals, and when therefore their labor has to some extent been socialized, does a state of things arise in which the surplus labor of one becomes a condition of existence for another. At the dawn of civilization the productivity that labor has acquired is small, but so too are the wants, which develop with the means of satisfying them and through those means. Further, at that early period, the part of society that lives on the labor of others is infinitely small compared with the mass of direct producers. As the productivity of labor progresses, that small part of society grows both absolutely and relatively.[^3] Besides, capital, with the relations that go with it, springs up from an economic soil that is the product of a long process of development. The productivity of labor that serves as its foundation and starting-point is a gift, not of nature, but of a history embracing thousands of centuries.

[^2]: According to a recent calculation, there are still at least 4,000,000 cannibals in those parts of the earth that have already been explored.

[^3]: “Among the wild Indians in America, almost everything is the labourer’s, 99 parts of a hundred are to be put upon the account of labour. In England, perhaps, the labourer has not 2/3.” (The Advantages of the East India Trade, &c., p. 73.)

<!-- ch16-p010 -->
Apart from the greater or lesser degree of development in the form of social production, the productivity of labor is tied down by physical conditions. These can all be traced back to the constitution of man himself (race, etc.) and to surrounding nature. The external physical conditions fall into two great economic classes: (1) natural wealth in means of subsistence, that is, a fruitful soil, waters teeming with fish, etc., and (2) natural wealth in the instruments of labor, such as waterfalls, navigable rivers, wood, metal, coal, etc. At the dawn of civilization, it is the first class that tips the scale; at a higher stage of development, it is the second. Compare, for example, England with India, or, in ancient times, Athens and Corinth with the shores of the Black Sea.

<!-- ch16-p011 -->
The fewer the natural wants that urgently demand satisfaction, and the greater the natural fertility of the soil and the more favorable the climate, the less is the labor-time necessary for maintaining and reproducing the producer. So much greater, therefore, can be the excess of his labor for others over his labor for himself. Diodorus remarked on this long ago in the case of the ancient Egyptians.

<!-- ch16-p012 -->
> “It is altogether incredible how little trouble and expense the bringing up of their children causes them. They cook for them the first simple food at hand; they also give them the lower part of the papyrus stem to eat, so far as it can be roasted in the fire, and the roots and stalks of marsh plants, some raw, some boiled and roasted. Most of the children go without shoes and unclothed, for the air is so mild. Hence a child, until he is grown up, costs his parents not more, on the whole, than twenty drachmas. It is this, chiefly, which explains why the population of Egypt is so numerous, and, therefore, why so many great works can be undertaken.”[^4]

[^4]: Diodorus, cited above, l. I., c. 80.

<!-- ch16-p013 -->
Nevertheless, the grand structures of ancient Egypt are due less to the size of its population than to the large proportion of it that was freely available. Just as the individual worker can do more surplus labor the smaller his necessary labor-time is, so it is with the working population. The smaller the part of it that is needed for producing the necessary means of subsistence, the greater is the part that can be set to do other work.

<!-- ch16-p014 -->
So, once capitalist production is assumed, with all other circumstances remaining the same and the length of the working day given, the quantity of surplus labor will vary with the physical conditions of labor, especially with the fertility of the soil. But it by no means follows from this that the most fruitful soil is the best suited for the growth of the capitalist mode of production. This mode is based on man's mastery over nature. Where nature is too lavish, she “keeps him in hand, like a child in leading-strings [strings used to guide a small child learning to walk].” She does not force on him any need to develop himself.[^5] It is not the tropics with their lush vegetation, but the temperate zone, that is the mother-country of capital. It is not the mere fertility of the soil, but the differentiation of the soil, the variety of its natural products, and the changes of the seasons, that form the physical basis for the social division of labor. And these, by changes in the natural surroundings, spur man on to multiply his wants, his capabilities, and his means and modes of labor. It is the necessity of bringing a natural force under the control of society, of economizing it, of appropriating or subduing it on a large scale by the work of man's hand, that first plays the decisive part in the history of industry. Examples are the irrigation works in Egypt,[^6] Lombardy, and Holland, or in India and Persia. There, irrigation by means of artificial canals not only supplies the soil with the water it cannot do without, but also carries down to it mineral fertilizers, in the form of sediment from the hills. The secret of the flourishing state of industry in Spain and Sicily under the rule of the Arabs lay in their irrigation works.[^7]

[^5]: “The first (natural wealth) as it is most noble and advantageous, so doth it make the people careless, proud, and given to all excesses; whereas the second enforceth vigilancy, literature, arts and policy.” (England’s Treasure by Foreign Trade. Or the Balance of our Foreign Trade is the Rule of our Treasure. Written by Thomas Mun of London, merchant, and now published for the common good by his son John Mun. London, 1669, p. 181, 182.) “Nor can I conceive a greater curse upon a body of people, than to be thrown upon a spot of land, where the productions for subsistence and food were, in great measure, spontaneous, and the climate required or admitted little care for raiment [clothing] and covering... there may be an extreme on the other side. A soil incapable of produce by labour is quite as bad as a soil that produces plentifully without any labour.” (An Inquiry into the Present High Price of Provisions. London 1767, p. 10.)

[^6]: The need to predict the rise and fall of the Nile created Egyptian astronomy, and with it the rule of the priests as directors of agriculture. “Le solstice est le moment de l’année ou commence la crue du Nil, et celui que les Egyptiens ont du observer avec le plus d’attention.... C’était cette année tropique qu’il leur importait de marquer pour se diriger dans leurs opérations agricoles. Ils durent donc chercher dans le ciel un signe apparent de son retour.” [The solstice is the moment of the year when the Nile flood begins, and the one the Egyptians had to watch with the greatest attention .... So they had to look in the sky for a visible sign of its return.] (Cuvier: Discours sur les révolutions du globe, ed. Hoefer, Paris, 1863, p. 141.)

[^7]: One of the material bases of the state's power over the small, unconnected producing organisms in India was the regulation of the water supply. The Muslim rulers of India understood this better than their English successors. It is enough to recall the famine of 1866, which cost the lives of more than a million Hindus in the district of Orissa, in the Bengal presidency.

<!-- ch16-p015 -->
Favorable natural conditions alone give us only the possibility, never the reality, of surplus labor, nor, therefore, of surplus-value and a surplus-product. The result of differences in the natural conditions of labor is this: the same quantity of labor satisfies a different mass of needs in different countries,[^8] and consequently, under circumstances that are otherwise similar, the necessary labor-time is different. These conditions affect surplus labor only as natural limits, that is, by fixing the points at which labor for others can begin. As industry advances, these natural limits recede. In the midst of our West European society, where the worker buys the right to work for his own livelihood only by paying for it in surplus labor, the idea easily takes root that it is an inherent quality of human labor to provide a surplus-product.[^9] But consider, for example, an inhabitant of the eastern islands of the Asiatic Archipelago, where sago grows wild in the forests.

[^8]: “There are no two countries which furnish an equal number of the necessaries of life in equal plenty, and with the same quantity of labour. Men’s wants increase or diminish with the severity or temperateness of the climate they live in; consequently, the proportion of trade which the inhabitants of different countries are obliged to carry on through necessity cannot be the same, nor is it practicable to ascertain the degree of variation farther than by the degrees of Heat and Cold; from whence one may make this general conclusion, that the quantity of labour required for a certain number of people is greatest in cold climates, and least in hot ones; for in the former men not only want more clothes, but the earth more cultivating than in the latter.” (An Essay on the Governing Causes of the Natural Rate of Interest. London 1750. p. 60.) The author of this epoch-making anonymous work is J. Massy. Hume took his theory of interest from it.

[^9]: “Chaque travail doit (this also appears to be one of the *droits et devoirs du citoyen* [rights and duties of the citizen]) laisser un excédent.” [All labor must leave a surplus] Proudhon.

<!-- ch16-p016 -->
> “When the inhabitants have convinced themselves, by boring a hole in the tree, that the pith is ripe, the trunk is cut down and divided into several pieces, the pith is extracted, mixed with water and filtered: it is then quite fit for use as sago. One tree commonly yields 300 lbs., and occasionally 500 to 600 lbs. There, then, people go into the forests, and cut bread for themselves, just as with us they cut fire-wood.”[^10]

[^10]: F. Schouw: “Die Erde, die Pflanze und der Mensch,” 2. Ed. Leipz. 1854, p. 148.

<!-- ch16-p017 -->
Suppose now that such an eastern bread-cutter needs 12 working hours a week to satisfy all his wants. Nature's direct gift to him is plenty of leisure time. Before he can use this leisure time productively for himself, a whole series of historical events is required. Before he spends it in surplus labor for strangers, compulsion is necessary. If capitalist production were introduced, the honest fellow would perhaps have to work six days a week in order to take for himself the product of one working day. The bounty of Nature does not explain why he would then have to work 6 days a week, or why he must provide 5 days of surplus labor. It explains only why his necessary labor-time would be limited to one day a week. But in no case would his surplus-product arise from some mysterious quality inherent in human labor.

<!-- ch16-p018 -->
Thus not only the historically developed social productivity of labor, but also its natural productivity, appears to be the productivity of the capital with which that labor is incorporated.

<!-- ch16-p019 -->
Ricardo never concerns himself with the origin of surplus-value. He treats it as something inherent in the capitalist mode of production, a mode which, in his eyes, is the natural form of social production. Whenever he discusses the productivity of labor, he looks in it not for the cause of surplus-value, but for the cause that determines the magnitude of that value. His school, on the other hand, has openly proclaimed the productivity of labor to be the originating cause of profit (read: surplus-value). This at any rate is progress compared with the mercantilists. They, for their part, derived the excess of the price over the cost of production of the product from the act of exchange, from the product being sold above its value. (Price is the money name of the value, that is, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) Nevertheless, Ricardo's school simply dodged the problem; they did not solve it. In fact these bourgeois economists saw by instinct, and rightly so, that it is very dangerous to stir too deeply the burning question of the origin of surplus-value. (Bourgeois means belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it.) But what are we to think of John Stuart Mill, who, half a century after Ricardo, solemnly claims to be superior to the mercantilists by clumsily repeating the wretched evasions of Ricardo's earliest vulgarizers?

<!-- ch16-p020 -->
Mill says:

<!-- ch16-p021 -->
> “The cause of profit is that labour produces more than is required for its support.”

<!-- ch16-p022 -->
So far, nothing but the old story; but Mill, wishing to add something of his own, goes on:

<!-- ch16-p023 -->
> “To vary the form of the theorem; the reason why capital yields a profit, is because food, clothing, materials and tools, last longer than the time which was required to produce them.”

<!-- ch16-p024 -->
Here he confuses the duration of labor-time with the duration of its products. On this view, a baker whose product lasts only a day could never extract the same profit from his workers as a machine-maker whose products last for 20 years and more. Of course it is very true that if a bird's nest did not last longer than the time it takes to build, birds would have to do without nests.

<!-- ch16-p025 -->
Once this fundamental truth is established, Mill establishes his own superiority over the mercantilists.

<!-- ch16-p026 -->
> “We thus see,” he proceeds, “that profit arises, not from the incident of exchange, but from the productive power of labour; and the general profit of the country is always what the productive power of labour makes it, whether any exchange takes place or not. If there were no division of employments, there would be no buying or selling, but there would still be profit.”

<!-- ch16-p027 -->
For Mill, then, exchange, buying and selling, those general conditions of capitalist production, are a mere incident, and there would always be profits even without the purchase and sale of labor-power!

<!-- ch16-p028 -->
“If,” he continues, “the labourers of the country collectively produce twenty per cent more than their wages, profits will be twenty per cent, whatever prices may or may not be.” On the one hand, this is a rare bit of tautology; for if workers produce a surplus-value of 20% for the capitalist, his profit will be to the total wages of the workers as 20:100. On the other hand, it is absolutely false to say that “profits will be 20%.” They will always be less, because they are calculated on the *sum total* of the capital advanced. If, for example, the capitalist has advanced £500, of which £400 is laid out in means of production and £100 in wages, and if the rate of surplus-value is 20%, the rate of profit will be 20:500, that is, 4% and not 20%.

<!-- ch16-p029 -->
Next comes a splendid example of Mill's way of handling the different historical forms of social production.

<!-- ch16-p030 -->
> “I assume, throughout, the state of things which, where the labourers and capitalists are separate classes, prevails, with few exceptions, universally; namely, that the capitalist advances the whole expenses, including the entire remuneration of the labourer.”

<!-- ch16-p031 -->
A strange optical illusion, to see everywhere a state of things that as yet exists on our earth only as an exception.[^11] But let us finish – Mill is willing to concede,

[^11]: In earlier editions of *Capital* the quotation from John Stuart Mill, “I assume throughout...of the labourer,” had been given incorrectly: the words “where the labourers and capitalists are separate classes” had been left out. In a letter dated November 28, 1878, Marx pointed this out to Danielson, the Russian translator of *Capital*, and added: “The next two sentences, viz. ‘Strange optical illusion to see everywhere a state of things which as yet exists only exceptionally on our earth. But let us finish’ ‒ should be deleted and the following sentence substituted:

    “Mr. Mill is good enough to believe that this state of things is not an absolute necessity, even in that economic system in which ‘labourers and capitalists are separate classes.’”

    The content of this note is taken from the *Volksausgabe* [People's Edition]. The quotation from Mill is from his *Principles of Political Economy*, Book II, Chap XV, 5.

<!-- ch16-p032 -->
> “that he should do so is not a matter of inherent necessity.” On the contrary: “the labourer might wait, until the production is complete, for all that part of his wages which exceeds mere necessaries: and even for the whole, if he has funds in hand sufficient for his temporary support. But in the latter case, the labourer is to that extent really a capitalist in the concern, by supplying a portion of the funds necessary for carrying it on.”

<!-- ch16-p033 -->
Mill might have gone further and added that the worker who advances to himself not only the necessaries of life but also the means of production is in reality nothing but his own wage worker. He might also have said that the American peasant proprietor is only a serf who does forced labor for himself instead of for his lord.

<!-- ch16-p034 -->
After thus proving clearly that even if capitalist production did not exist, it would still always exist, Mill is consistent enough to show, on the contrary, that it does not exist even when it does exist.

<!-- ch16-p035 -->
> “And even in the former case” (when the worker is a wage worker to whom the capitalist advances all the necessaries of life, he, the worker), “may be looked upon in the same light,” (that is, as a capitalist), “since, contributing his labour at less than the market-price, (!) he may be regarded as lending the difference (?) to his employer and receiving it back with interest, &c.”[^12]

[^12]: J. St. Mill. Principles of Pol. Econ. London 1868, p. 252-53 passim.

<!-- ch16-p036 -->
In reality, the worker advances his labor free of charge to the capitalist for, say, one week, in order to receive the market price at the end of the week, etc.; and it is this, according to Mill, that turns him into a capitalist. On a level plain, simple mounds look like hills; and the imbecile flatness of today's bourgeoisie is to be measured by the height of its great intellects.
