## Chapter 33: The Modern Theory of Colonization[^1]

<!-- ch33-p001 -->
Political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) confuses, on principle, two very different kinds of private property. One rests on the producers’ own labor, the other on employing the labor of others. It forgets that the second is not only the direct opposite of the first, but grows absolutely only on its grave. In Western Europe, the home of political economy, the process of primitive accumulation (the historical process that separated the producers from their means of production, above all by driving peasants off the land, and so created the conditions for capitalism) is more or less complete. Here the capitalist regime has either directly conquered the whole field of national production, or, where economic conditions are less developed, it at least indirectly controls those layers of society which belong to the outdated mode of production but go on existing side by side with it, slowly decaying. To this ready-made world of capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger), the political economist applies the ideas of law and property he inherited from a pre-capitalist world. He does so with all the more anxious zeal and all the more pious oiliness, the more loudly the facts cry out against his ideology. It is different in the colonies. There the capitalist regime everywhere collides with the resistance of the producer, who owns his own conditions of labor and uses that labor to enrich himself instead of the capitalist. The contradiction between these two diametrically opposed economic systems shows itself here in practice, as a struggle between them. Where the capitalist has the power of the mother-country behind him, he tries to clear out of his way, by force, the modes of production and appropriation based on the producer’s independent labor. In the mother-country, the sycophant of capital, the political economist, is driven by his interest to proclaim that the capitalist mode of production is in theory identical with its opposite. That same interest drives him in the colonies to come clean and to proclaim aloud the antagonism between the two modes of production. To this end, he proves that the development of the social productive power of labor, cooperation (many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist’s command), division of labor (the splitting of the making of one product into many separate tasks, each done by a specialized worker), the use of machinery on a large scale, etc., are impossible without the expropriation of the workers (taking away people’s property, here land and means of production) and the matching transformation of their means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things) into capital. In the interest of the so-called national wealth, he looks for artificial means to make sure the people stay poor. Here his apologist’s armor crumbles off, bit by bit, like rotten touchwood. It is the great merit of E. G. Wakefield to have discovered, not anything new about the Colonies,[^2] but in the Colonies the truth about the conditions of capitalist production in the mother country. The system of protective tariffs, at its origin,[^3] tried to manufacture capitalists artificially in the mother-country. In the same way, Wakefield’s theory of colonization, which England tried for a time to enforce by Acts of Parliament, tried to manufacture wage workers in the Colonies. This he calls “systematic colonisation.”

[^2]: Wakefield’s few glimpses on the subject of modern colonization were fully anticipated by Mirabeau Pere, the physiocrat, and even much earlier by English economists.

[^3]: Later, it became a temporary necessity in the international competitive struggle. But whatever its motive, the consequences remain the same.

First of all, Wakefield discovered that in the Colonies, owning money, means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), machines, and other means of production does not yet stamp a man as a capitalist if the counterpart is missing: the wage worker, the other man who is forced to sell himself of his own free will. He discovered that capital is not a thing, but a social relation between persons, set up by means of things.[^4] Mr. Peel, he moans, took with him from England to Swan River, West Australia, means of subsistence and of production worth £50,000. Mr. Peel had the foresight to bring with him, as well, 300 persons of the working class, men, women, and children. Once he arrived at his destination, “Mr. Peel was left without a servant to make his bed or fetch him water from the river.”[^5] Unhappy Mr. Peel, who provided for everything except the export of English modes of production to Swan River!

[^4]: “A negro is a negro. In certain circumstances he becomes a slave. A mule is a machine for spinning cotton. Only under certain circumstances does it become capital. Outside these circumstances, it is no more capital than gold is intrinsically money, or sugar is the price [the money name of the value embodied in a commodity] of sugar.... Capital is a social relation of production. It is a historical relation of production.” (Karl Marx, “Lohnarbeit und Kapital,” *N. Rh. Z*., No.266, April 7, 1849.)

[^5]: E. G. Wakefield: “England and America,” vol.ii. p.33.

To understand the following discoveries of Wakefield, two preliminary remarks are needed. We know that the means of production and subsistence, as long as they remain the property of the immediate producer, are not capital. They become capital only under circumstances in which they serve at the same time as means of exploiting and subjugating the worker. But in the head of the political economist this capitalist soul of theirs is so closely wedded to their material substance that he christens them capital under all circumstances, even when they are its exact opposite. So it is with Wakefield. Further: when the means of production are split up into the individual property of many independent workers, working on their own account, he calls this the equal division of capital. The political economist is like the feudal lawyer. The feudal lawyer stuck the labels supplied by feudal law onto purely monetary relations.

<!-- ch33-p004 -->
“If,” says Wakefield, “all members of the society are supposed to possess equal portions of capital... no man would have a motive for accumulating more capital than he could use with his own hands. This is to some extent the case in new American settlements, where a passion for owning land prevents the existence of a class of labourers for hire.”[^6] So as long as the worker can accumulate for himself (and he can do this as long as he remains the owner of his means of production), capitalist accumulation (turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows) and the capitalist mode of production are impossible. The class of wage workers, which they cannot do without, is missing. How, then, in old Europe, was the worker expropriated from his conditions of labor? How, that is, did capital and wage labor come to exist side by side? By a social contract of a quite original kind. “Mankind have adopted a... simple contrivance for promoting the accumulation of capital,” which, of course, had floated in their imagination since the time of Adam as the sole and final purpose of their existence: “they have divided themselves into owners of capital and owners of labour.... The division was the result of concert and combination.”[^7] In a word: the mass of mankind expropriated itself in honor of the “accumulation of capital.” Now one would think that this instinct of self-denying fanaticism would have full play especially in the Colonies, the only place where the men and conditions exist that could turn a social contract from a dream into reality. But why, then, should “systematic colonisation” be called in to replace its opposite, spontaneous, unregulated colonization? But ‒ but ‒ “In the Northern States of the American Union; it may be doubted whether so many as a tenth of the people would fall under the description of hired labourers.... In England... the labouring class compose the bulk of the people.”[^8] Indeed, working humanity’s urge to expropriate itself for the glory of capital barely exists, so much so that slavery, according to Wakefield himself, is the only natural basis of Colonial wealth. His systematic colonization is a mere last resort, since he unfortunately has to deal with free men, not with slaves. “The first Spanish settlers in Saint Domingo did not obtain labourers from Spain. But, without labourers, their capital must have perished, or at least, must soon have been diminished to that small amount which each individual could employ with his own hands. This has actually occurred in the last Colony founded by England – the Swan River Settlement – where a great mass of capital, of seeds, implements, and cattle, has perished for want of labourers to use it, and where no settler has preserved much more capital than he can employ with his own hands.”[^9]

We have seen that the expropriation of the mass of the people from the soil forms the basis of the capitalist mode of production. The essence of a free colony, on the contrary, is this: the bulk of the soil is still public property, so every settler on it can turn part of it into his private property and individual means of production, without preventing later settlers from doing the same.[^10] This is the secret both of the colonies’ prosperity and of their deep-rooted vice: their opposition to the establishment of capital. “Where land is very cheap and all men are free, where every one who so pleases can easily obtain a piece of land for himself, not only is labour very dear, as respects the labourer’s share of the produce, but the difficulty is to obtain combined labour at any price.”[^11]

[^10]: “Land, to be an element of colonisation, must not only be waste, but it must be public property, liable to be converted into private property.” (Cited above, Vol.II, p.125.)

[^11]: Cited above, Vol.I, p.247.

In the colonies the separation of the worker from the conditions of labor and from their root, the soil, does not exist, or exists only here and there, or on too limited a scale. So neither does the separation of agriculture from industry exist, nor the destruction of the peasants’ household industry. Where, then, is the internal market for capital to come from? “No part of the population of America is exclusively agricultural, excepting slaves and their employers who combine capital and labour in particular works. Free Americans, who cultivate the soil, follow many other occupations. Some portion of the furniture and tools which they use is commonly made by themselves. They frequently build their own houses, and carry to market, at whatever distance, the produce of their own industry. They are spinners and weavers; they make soap and candles, as well as, in many cases, shoes and clothes for their own use. In America the cultivation of land is often the secondary pursuit of a blacksmith, a miller or a shopkeeper.”[^12] With such odd people as these, where is the “field of abstinence” for the capitalists?

[^12]: Cited above, pp.21, 22.

The great beauty of capitalist production is this: it not only constantly reproduces the wage worker as a wage worker, but also always produces, in proportion to the accumulation of capital, a relative surplus population of wage workers (workers made “surplus” to capital’s needs for the time being, who serve as a reserve of available labor and press down on the employed). In this way the law of the supply and demand of labor is kept in the right groove, the ups and downs of wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself) are penned within limits that suit capitalist exploitation, and finally the worker’s social dependence on the capitalist, that indispensable requirement, is secured. This is an unmistakable relation of dependence. Yet at home, in the mother-country, the smug political economist can turn it, as if by magic, into a relation of free contract between buyer and seller, between equally independent owners of commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use): the owner of the commodity capital and the owner of the commodity labor. But in the colonies this pretty fancy is torn apart. Here the absolute population grows much faster than in the mother-country, because many workers enter this world as ready-made adults, and yet the labor-market is always short of workers. The law of the supply and demand of labor falls to pieces. On the one hand, the old world constantly throws in capital, thirsting for exploitation and “abstinence”; on the other, the regular reproduction of the wage worker as wage worker runs into obstacles of the most impertinent kind, some of them impossible to overcome. What becomes of the production of wage workers who are surplus in proportion to the accumulation of capital? The wage worker of today is tomorrow an independent peasant, or a craftsman working for himself. He vanishes from the labor-market, but not into the workhouse. Wage workers are constantly turning into independent producers, who work for themselves instead of for capital, and enrich themselves instead of the capitalist gentlemen. This in turn has a very perverse effect on the conditions of the labor-market. Not only does the degree of exploitation of the wage worker stay indecently low. On top of that, the wage worker loses, along with the relation of dependence, the feeling of dependence on the abstemious capitalist as well. Hence all the inconveniences that our E. G. Wakefield paints so stoutly, so eloquently, so touchingly. The supply of wage labor, he complains, is neither constant, nor regular, nor sufficient. “The supply of labour is always not only small but uncertain.”[^13] “Though the produce divided between the capitalist and the labourer be large, the labourer takes so great a share that he soon becomes a capitalist.... Few, even those whose lives are unusually long, can accumulate great masses of wealth.”[^14] The workers flatly refuse to allow the capitalist to abstain from paying for the greater part of their labor. It does him no good if he is cunning enough to import his own wage workers from Europe with his own capital. They soon “cease... to be labourers for hire; they... become independent landowners, if not competitors with their former masters in the labour-market.”[^15] Think of the horror! The excellent capitalist has imported from Europe, in the flesh and with his own good money, his own competitors! The end of the world has come! No wonder Wakefield laments that the wage workers in the colonies have no dependence at all, and no feeling of dependence either. Because of the high wages, says his disciple Merivale, there is in the colonies “the urgent desire for cheaper and more subservient labourers – for a class to whom the capitalist might dictate terms, instead of being dictated to by them.... In ancient civilised countries the labourer, though free, is by a law of Nature dependent on capitalists; in colonies this dependence must be created by artificial means.”[^16]

[^14]: Cited above, Vol.I, p.131.

[^15]: Cited above, Vol.II, p.5.

[^16]: Merivale, cited above, Vol.II, pp.235-314 passim. Even the mild Free Trade vulgar economist Molinari (vulgar economics: economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations) says: “Dans les colonies où l’esclavage a été aboli sans que le travail forcé se trouvait remplacé par une quantité équivalente de travail libre, on a vu s’opérer la contre-partie du fait qui se réalise tous les jours sous nos yeux. On a vu les simples travailleurs exploiter à leur tour les entrepreneurs d’industrie, exiger d’eux des salaires hors de toute proportion avec la part légitime qui leur revenait dans le produit. Les planteurs, ne pouvant obtenir de leurs sucres un prix suffisant pour couvrir la hausse de salaire, ont été obligés de fournir l’excédant, d’abord sur leurs profits, ensuite sur leurs capitaux mêmes. Une foule de planteurs ont été ruinés de la sorte, d’autres ont fermé leurs ateliers pour échapper à une ruine imminente.... Sans doute, il vaut mieux voir périr des accumulations de capitaux que des générations d’hommes [how generous Mr. Molinari!]: mais ne vaudrait-il pas mieux que ni les uns ni les autres périssent? [In the colonies where slavery has been abolished without the forced labor being replaced by an equivalent quantity of free labor, we have seen the reverse of what happens every day before our eyes. We have seen simple workers exploit the industrial entrepreneurs in their turn, demanding from them wages out of all proportion to the legitimate share in the product that was due to them. The planters, unable to get a price for their sugar high enough to cover the rise in wages, were obliged to make up the difference, first out of their profits, then out of their capital itself. A great many planters have been ruined in this way; others have closed their workshops to escape the ruin that threatened them ... No doubt it is better to see accumulations of capital perish than generations of men ... but would it not be better if neither perished?] (Molinari, cited above, pp.51,52.) Mr. Molinari, Mr. Molinari! What then becomes of the ten commandments, of Moses and the prophets, of the law of supply and demand, if in Europe the “entrepreneur” can cut down the worker’s legitimate part, and in the West Indies the worker can cut down the entrepreneur’s? And what, if you please, is this “legitimate part,” which, as you yourself show, the capitalist in Europe neglects to pay every day? Over there, in the colonies where the workers are so “simple” as to “exploit” the capitalist, Mr. Molinari feels a strong itch to use the police to set on the right road the law of supply and demand, which elsewhere works automatically.

What, according to Wakefield, is the consequence of this unfortunate state of things in the colonies? A “barbarising tendency of dispersion” of producers and of national wealth.[^17] When the means of production are parceled out among countless owners, each working on his own account, this destroys, along with the centralization of capital (the joining of already existing capitals into fewer, bigger ones), the whole foundation of combined labor. Every drawn-out undertaking that stretches over several years and demands an outlay of fixed capital is prevented from being carried out. In Europe, capital invests without a moment’s hesitation, for the working class is its living accessory, always in excess, always available. But in the colonies! Wakefield tells an extremely mournful anecdote. He was talking with some capitalists of Canada and the state of New York, where the wave of immigrants often stagnates and leaves behind a sediment of “supernumerary” workers. “Our capital,” says one of the characters in the melodrama, “was ready for many operations which require a considerable period of time for their completion; but we could not begin such operations with labour which, we knew, would soon leave us. If we had been sure of retaining the labour of such emigrants, we should have been glad to have engaged it at once, and for a high price: and we should have engaged it, even though we had been sure it would leave us, provided we had been sure of a fresh supply whenever we might need it.”[^18]

[^17]: Wakefield, cited above, Vol.II, p.52.

[^18]: Cited above, pp.191, 192.

Wakefield sets English capitalist agriculture and its “combined” labor against the scattered farming of American peasants. After that, he unwittingly gives us a glimpse of the other side of the medal. He depicts the mass of the American people as well-off, independent, enterprising, and comparatively cultured, while “the English agricultural labourer is miserable wretch, a pauper.... In what country, except North America and some new colonies, do the wages of free labour employed in agriculture much exceed a bare subsistence for the labourer? ... Undoubtedly, farm-horses in England, being a valuable property, are better fed than English peasants.”[^19] But never mind: national wealth is, once again, by its very nature identical with the misery of the people.

[^19]: Cited above, Vol.I, p.47, 246.

How, then, is the anti-capitalist cancer of the colonies to be healed? If people were willing to turn all the soil from public into private property at one blow, they would certainly destroy the root of the evil, but also – the colonies. The trick is to kill two birds with one stone. Let the Government put an artificial price on the virgin soil, a price independent of the law of supply and demand. It must be a price that forces the immigrant to work a long time for wages before he can earn enough money to buy land and turn himself into an independent peasant.[^20] The sale of land at a price that is relatively prohibitive for the wage workers produces a fund. This fund of money, squeezed out of the wages of labor by violating the sacred law of supply and demand, the Government is in turn to use, in proportion as it grows, to import have-nothings from Europe into the colonies, and so keep the wage labor market full for the capitalists. Under these circumstances, tout sera pour le mieux dans le meilleur des mondes possibles [everything will be for the best in the best of all possible worlds]. This is the great secret of “systematic colonisation.” By this plan, Wakefield cries in triumph, “the supply of labour *must* be constant and regular, because, first, as no labourer would be able to procure land until he had worked for money, all immigrant labourers, working for a time for wages and in combination, would produce capital for the employment of more labourers; secondly, because every labourer who left off working for wages and became a landowner would, by purchasing land, provide a fund for bringing fresh labour to the colony.”[^21] The price of the soil imposed by the State must, of course, be a “sufficient price,” that is, so high “as to prevent the labourers from becoming independent landowners until others had followed to take their place.”[^22] This “sufficient price for the land” is nothing but a roundabout, polite name for the ransom that the worker pays the capitalist for permission to retire from the wage labor market to the land. First, he must create “capital” for the capitalist, so that the capitalist can exploit more workers with it. Then he must put, at his own expense, a *locum tenens* [stand-in] on the labor market, whom the Government sends across the sea for the benefit of his old master, the capitalist.

Quand vous mettez un homme dans le vide, vous vous emparez de l’atmosphère. Ainsi faites-vous, quand vous vous emparez du sol.... C’est le mettre dans le vide le richesses, pour ne la laisser vivre qu’à votre volonté.” [It is, you add, thanks to the appropriation of the soil and of capital that the man who has nothing but his arms finds work and makes an income for himself ... but on the contrary, it is thanks to the individual appropriation of the soil that there are men who have nothing but their arms. ... It is very characteristic that the English Government for years practiced this method of “primitive accumulation,” which Mr. Wakefield prescribed expressly for use in the colonies. The fiasco was, of course, as complete as that of Sir Robert Peel’s Bank Act. The stream of emigration was merely diverted from the English colonies to the United States. Meanwhile, the advance of capitalist production in Europe, together with growing Government pressure, has made Wakefield’s recipe unnecessary. On the one hand, the enormous and ceaseless stream of people driven to America year after year leaves behind a stationary sediment in the east of the United States. There the wave of immigration from Europe throws people onto the labor-market faster than the wave of emigration westwards can wash them away. On the other hand, the American Civil War brought in its train a colossal national debt, and with it the pressure of taxes, the rise of the vilest financial aristocracy, the squandering of a huge part of the public land on speculative companies for the exploitation of railways, mines, etc., in short, the most rapid centralization of capital. So the great republic has ceased to be the promised land for emigrant workers. Capitalist production advances there with giant strides, even though wages have not yet been lowered, and the wage worker has not yet been made dependent, anywhere near the normal European level. The Government has shamelessly lavished uncultivated colonial land on aristocrats and capitalists, as even Wakefield loudly denounced. Especially in Australia,[^23] this has produced an ample “relative surplus labouring population.” It did so together with the stream of people that the gold diggings attract, and with the competition that imported English commodities cause even to the smallest craftsman. As a result, almost every mail brings the Job’s news (bad news, like the messages brought to Job) of a “glut of the Australia labour-market,” and in some places prostitution flourishes as wantonly as in the London Haymarket.

[^23]: As soon as Australia became her own lawmaker, she passed, of course, laws favorable to the settlers, but the squandering of the land, already carried out by the English Government, stands in the way. “The first and main object at which new Land Act of 1862 aims is to give increased facilities for the settlement of the people.” (“The Land Law of Victoria,” by the Hon. C. G. Duffy, Minister of Public Lands, London, 1862.)

However, we are not concerned here with the conditions of the colonies. The only thing that interests us is the secret that the political economy of the old world discovered in the new world and proclaimed from the housetops: that the capitalist mode of production and accumulation, and therefore capitalist private property, have as their fundamental condition the destruction of self-earned private property; in other words, the expropriation of the worker.
