<!-- ch25-p201 -->
Dr. Hunter investigated 5,375 cottages of agricultural workers, not only in the purely agricultural districts but in every county of England. Of these, 2,195 had only one bedroom (which was often used as the living-room as well), 2,930 had only two, and 250 had more than two. I will give a few samples picked from a dozen counties.

<!-- ch25-p202 -->
(1.) Bedfordshire

<!-- ch25-p203 -->
*Wrestlingworth.* The bedrooms are about 12 feet long and 10 broad, although many are smaller than this. The small one-story cottages are often divided by partitions into two bedrooms, and there is frequently one bed in a kitchen 5 feet 6 inches high. Rent, £3 a year. The tenants have to make their own outdoor toilets; the landlord supplies only a hole. As soon as one tenant has made a toilet, the whole neighborhood uses it. One house, belonging to a family called Richardson, was of a beauty quite beyond compare. “Its plaster walls bulged very like a lady’s dress in a curtsey. One gable end was convex, the other concave, and on this last, unfortunately, stood the chimney, a curved tube of clay and wood like an elephant’s trunk. A long stick served as prop to prevent the chimney from falling. The doorway and window were rhomboidal.” Of 17 houses visited, only 4 had more than one bedroom, and those four were overcrowded. The cottages with one bedroom housed 3 adults and 3 children, a married couple with 6 children, etc.

<!-- ch25-p204 -->
*Dunton.* Rents are high, from £4 to £5; the man’s weekly wage is 10s. The families hope to pay the rent through the straw-plaiting they do. The higher the rent, the more people must work together to pay it. Six adults, living with 4 children in one sleeping room, pay £3 10s. for it. The cheapest house in Dunton, 15 feet long on the outside and 10 broad, was let for £3. Only one of the houses investigated had 2 bedrooms. A little outside the village stood a house whose “tenants dunged against the house-side.” The lower 9 inches of its door had been eaten away by sheer rot. The doorway was a single opening, closed at night by a few bricks, cleverly pushed up after shutting and covered with some matting. Half a window, glass and frame, had gone the way of all flesh. Here, without furniture, 3 adults and 5 children were huddled together. Dunton is no worse than the rest of Biggleswade Union.

<!-- ch25-p205 -->
(2.) Berkshire

<!-- ch25-p206 -->
*Beenham.* In June, 1864, a man, his wife, and 4 children lived in a cot (a one-story cottage). A daughter came home from service with scarlet fever. She died. One child fell ill and died. The mother and one child were down with typhus when Dr. Hunter was called in. The father and one child slept outside. But here one could see how hard it was to keep the sick apart, for the linen of the fever-stricken household lay in the crowded marketplace of the miserable village, waiting to be washed. The rent of H.’s house was 1s. a week, with one bedroom for the man, his wife, and 6 children. One house was let for 8d. a week. It was 14 feet 6 inches long and 7 feet broad, with a kitchen 6 feet high. The bedroom had no window, fireplace, door, or opening, except into the lobby. There was no garden. A man lived here for a little while with two grown-up daughters and one grown-up son. The father and son slept on the bed, the girls in the passage. Each of the girls had a child while the family was living here, but one went to the workhouse to give birth and then came home.

<!-- ch25-p207 -->
(3.) Buckinghamshire

<!-- ch25-p208 -->
Here 30 cottages – on 1,000 acres of land – held about 130-140 persons. The parish of *Bradenham* covers 1,000 acres. In 1851 it had 36 houses and a population of 84 males and 54 females. This inequality of the sexes was partly put right by 1861, when there were 98 males and 87 females: an increase in 10 years of 14 men and 33 women. Meanwhile, the number of houses had fallen by one.

<!-- ch25-p209 -->
*Winslow.* A great part of this place is newly built, in good style. The demand for houses appears to be very marked, since very miserable cottages are let at 1s. to 1s. 3d. per week.

<!-- ch25-p210 -->
*Water Eaton.* Here, in view of the growing population, the landlords have destroyed about 20 percent of the existing houses. A poor worker, who had to walk about 4 miles to his work, was asked whether he could not find a cottage nearer. He answered: “No; they know better than to take a man in with my large family.”

<!-- ch25-p211 -->
*Tinker’s End,* near Winslow. A bedroom 11 feet long, 9 feet broad, and 6 feet 5 inches high at its highest point held 4 adults and 4 children. Another, 11 feet 3 inches by 9 feet, and 5 feet 10 inches high, housed 6 persons. Each of these families had less space than is considered necessary for a convict. No house had more than one bedroom, and not one of them had a back door. Water was very scarce. The weekly rent ran from 1s. 4d. to 2s. In 16 of the houses visited, only 1 man earned 10s. a week. Under the conditions just described, the amount of air for each person equals what he would have if he were shut up for the whole night in a box measuring 4 feet each way. But then, the ancient dens allowed a certain amount of unintended ventilation.

<!-- ch25-p212 -->
(4.) Cambridgeshire

<!-- ch25-p213 -->
*Gamblingay* belongs to several landlords. It contains the most wretched cottages to be found anywhere. There is much straw-plaiting. “A deadly lassitude, a hopeless surrendering up to filth,” reigns in Gamblingay. The neglect at its center turns into gangrene at its edges, north and south, where the houses are rotting to pieces. The absentee landlords bleed this poor slum too freely. The rents are very high. 8 or 9 persons are packed into one sleeping room; in 2 cases, 6 adults, each with 1 or 2 children, are in one small bedroom.

<!-- ch25-p214 -->
(5.) Essex

<!-- ch25-p215 -->
In this county, in many parishes, the number of persons and the number of cottages go down hand in hand. In no fewer than 22 parishes, however, the destruction of houses has not prevented the population from growing, or has not brought about the expulsion which generally happens under the name of “migration to towns.” In Fingringhoe, a parish of 3,443 acres, there were 145 houses in 1851, but in 1861 only 110. But the people did not want to go away, and even managed to increase under these circumstances. In 1851, 252 persons lived in 61 houses, but in 1861, 262 persons were squeezed into 49 houses. In Basilden, in 1851, 157 persons lived on 1,827 acres, in 35 houses; at the end of ten years there were 180 persons in 27 houses. In the parishes of Fingringhoe, South Fambridge, Widford, Basilden, and Ramsden Crags, 1,392 persons were living on 8,449 acres in 316 houses in 1851; in 1861, on the same area, there were 1,473 persons in 249 houses.

<!-- ch25-p216 -->
(6.) Herefordshire

<!-- ch25-p217 -->
This little county has suffered more from the “eviction-spirit” than any other in England. At Nadby the cottages are generally overcrowded, with only 2 bedrooms, and they belong for the most part to the farmers. The farmers easily let them for £3 or £4 a year, and paid a weekly wage of 9s.

<!-- ch25-p218 -->
(7.) Huntingdon

<!-- ch25-p219 -->
*Hartford* had 87 houses in 1851. Shortly after that, 19 cottages were destroyed in this small parish of 1,720 acres. The population was 452 in 1831, 382 in 1852, and 341 in 1861. 14 cottages, each with 1 bedroom, were visited. In one lived a married couple, 3 grown-up sons, 1 grown-up daughter, and 4 children – 10 in all; in another, 3 adults and 6 children. One of these rooms, in which 8 people slept, was 12 feet 10 inches long, 12 feet 2 inches broad, and 6 feet 9 inches high. On average, without subtracting anything for parts jutting into the room, this gave about 130 cubic feet per head. In the 14 sleeping rooms there were 34 adults and 33 children. These cottages seldom have gardens, but many of the occupants are able to farm small allotments at 10s. or 12s. per rood. These allotments are some distance from the houses, which have no toilets. The family “must either go to the allotment to deposit their ordures,” or, as happens in this place, if you will pardon the expression, “use a closet with a trough set like a drawer in a chest of drawers, and drawn out weekly and conveyed to the allotment to be emptied where its contents were wanted.” In Japan, the cycle of the conditions of life runs more decently than this.

(8.) Lincolnshire

<!-- ch25-p221 -->
*Langtoft.* A man lives here in Wright’s house with his wife, her mother, and 5 children. The house has a front kitchen, a scullery, and a bedroom over the front kitchen. The front kitchen and the bedroom measure 12 feet 2 inches by 9 feet 5 inches; the whole ground floor, 21 feet 2 inches by 9 feet 5 inches. The bedroom is an attic: the walls run together into the roof like a sugar-loaf, with a dormer window opening in front. “Why did he live here? On account of the garden? No; it is very small. Rent? (10.) Northamptonshire

<!-- ch25-p229 -->
*Brinworth, Pickford and Floore:* in these villages, in the winter, 20-30 men were hanging about the streets for lack of work. The farmers do not always till the grain and turnip land enough, and the landlord has found it best to throw all his farms together into 2 or 3. Hence the lack of work. While on one side of the wall the land calls for labor, on the other side the cheated workers cast longing glances at it. Feverishly overworked in summer and half-starved in winter, it is no wonder if they say in their peculiar dialect that “the parson and gentlefolk seem frit [frightened] to death at them.”

<!-- ch25-p230 -->
At Floore there were cases, in one bedroom of the smallest size, of couples with 4, 5, or 6 children; of 3 adults with 5 children; of a couple with a grandfather and 6 children down with scarlet fever, etc. In two houses with two bedrooms there were two families, of 8 and 9 adults respectively.

(11.) Wiltshire

<!-- ch25-p232 -->
*Stratton.* 31 houses were visited, 8 with only one bedroom. Pentill, in the same parish: a cottage let at 1s. 3d. a week, with 4 adults and 4 children, had nothing good about it except the walls, from the floor of rough-hewn pieces of stone to the roof of worn-out thatch.

(12.) Worcestershire

<!-- ch25-p234 -->
House-destruction here is not quite so extreme. Yet from 1851 to 1861 the average number of inhabitants per house rose from 4.2 to 4.6.

<!-- ch25-p235 -->
*Badsey.* There are many cottages and little gardens here. Some of the farmers declare that the cottages are “a great nuisance here, because they bring the poor.” One gentleman stated:

<!-- ch25-p236 -->
“The poor are none the better for them; if you build 500 they will let fast enough, in fact, the more you build, the more they want”

<!-- ch25-p237 -->
(according to him, the houses give birth to the inhabitants, who then, by a law of Nature, press on “the means of housing”). Dr. Hunter remarks:

<!-- ch25-p238 -->
“Now these poor must come from somewhere, and as there is no particular attraction, such as doles, at Badsey, it must be repulsion from some other unfit place, which will send them here. If each could find an allotment near his work, he would not prefer Badsey, where he pays for his scrap of ground twice as much as the farmer pays for his.”

<!-- ch25-p239 -->
The continual migration to the towns, the continual formation of a surplus population in the countryside through the concentration of farms, the turning of arable land into pasture, machinery, etc., and the continual eviction of the agricultural population through the destruction of their cottages, all go hand in hand. The emptier the district is of people, the greater is its “relative surplus population,” the greater is their pressure on the means of employment, and the greater is the absolute excess of the agricultural population over the means of housing it. The greater, therefore, in the villages, is the local surplus population and the most disease-breeding packing together of human beings. The packing together of knots of people in scattered little villages and small country towns corresponds to the forcible draining of people from the surface of the land. The continual displacement of the agricultural workers, in spite of their falling numbers and the growing mass of their products, gives birth to their pauperism. Their pauperism is in the end a motive for their eviction and the chief source of their miserable housing, which breaks down their last power of resistance and makes them all the more slaves of the landowners and the farmers.[^107] Thus wages at their minimum become a law of Nature for them. On the other hand, the land, in spite of its constant “relative surplus population,” is at the same time underpopulated. This shows not only locally, at the points where the outflow of people to towns, mines, railroad-building, etc., is most marked. It can be seen everywhere, at harvest-time as well as in spring and summer, at those frequently recurring times when English agriculture, so careful and intensive, needs extra workers. There are always too many agricultural workers for the ordinary needs of cultivating the soil, and always too few for its exceptional or temporary needs.[^108] So we find in the official documents contradictory complaints, from the same places at the same time, of a shortage of labor and of an excess of it. The temporary or local shortage of labor brings about no rise in wages, but a forcing of the women and children into the fields, and exploitation at an ever lower age. As soon as the exploitation of the women and children takes place on a larger scale, it becomes in its turn a new means of making the male agricultural worker surplus and of keeping down his wage. In the east of England a beautiful fruit of this vicious circle thrives – the so-called gang system, to which I must briefly return here.[^109]

[^107]: “The heaven-born employment of the hind [farm laborer] gives dignity even to his position. He is not a slave, but a soldier of peace, and deserves his place in married men’s quarters to be provided by the landlord, who has claimed a power of enforced labour similar to that the country demands of the soldier. He no more receives market-price for his work than does the soldier. Like the soldier he is caught young, ignorant, knowing only his own trade, and his own locality. Early marriage and the operation of the various laws of settlement affect the one as enlistment and the Mutiny Act [the law governing army discipline] affect the other.” (Dr. Hunter, cited above, p. 132.) Sometimes an exceptionally soft-hearted landlord is moved by the solitude he has created. “It is a melancholy thing to stand alone in one’s country,” said Lord Leicester, when he was congratulated on the completion of Hookham. “I look around and not a house is to be seen but mine. I am the giant of Giant Castle, and have eat up all my neighbours.”

[^108]: A similar movement has been seen in France during the last ten years. In proportion as capitalist production there takes possession of agriculture, it drives the “surplus” agricultural population into the towns. Here too we find the housing and other conditions getting worse at the source of the surplus population. On the special “prolétariat foncier” [land proletariat] to which this system of parceling out the land has given rise, see, among others, the work of Colins, already quoted, and Karl Marx, “Der Achtzehnte Brumaire des Louis Bonaparte.” 2nd edition. Hamburg, 1869, pp. 56, etc. In 1846 the town population of France stood at 24.42, the agricultural at 75.58; in 1861, the town at 28.86, the agricultural at 71.14 percent. During the last 5 years the fall in the agricultural share of the population has been even more marked. As early as 1846, Pierre Dupont sang in his “Ouvriers”:

    Mal vêtus, logés dans des trous, Sous les combles, dans les décombres, Nous vivons avec les hiboux Et les larrons, amis des ombres. [Badly clothed, lodged in holes, under the eaves, in the ruins, we live with the owls and the thieves, friends of the shadows]

[^109]: “Sixth and last Report of the Children’s Employment Commission,” published at the end of March, 1867. It deals only with the agricultural gang system.

<!-- ch25-p240 -->
The gang system is found almost exclusively in the counties of Lincoln, Huntingdon, Cambridge, Norfolk, Suffolk, and Nottingham, and here and there in the neighboring counties of Northampton, Bedford, and Rutland. Lincolnshire will serve us as an example. A large part of this county is new land, formerly marsh, or even, as in others of the eastern counties just named, recently won from the sea. The steam-engine has worked wonders in draining it. What were once fens and sandbanks now bear a lush sea of grain and the highest of rents. The same holds for the alluvial lands won by human effort, as on the island of Axholme and in other parishes on the banks of the Trent. As the new farms arose, not only were no new cottages built: old ones were pulled down, and the supply of labor had to come from open villages miles away, by long roads that wound along the hillsides. Only there had the population formerly found shelter from the endless floods of winter. The workers who live on the farms of 400-1,000 acres (they are called “confined labourers”) are employed only on the kinds of agricultural work that are permanent, difficult, and done with the help of horses. For every 100 acres there is, on average, scarcely one cottage. A fen farmer, for example, gave evidence before the Commission of Inquiry:

<!-- ch25-p241 -->
> “I farm 320 acres, all arable land. I have not one cottage on my farm. I have only one labourer on my farm now. I have four horsemen lodging about. We get light work done by gangs.”[^110]

[^110]: “Child. Emp. Comm., VI. Report.” Evidence 173, p. 37.

<!-- ch25-p242 -->
The soil needs much light field labor, such as weeding, hoeing, certain kinds of manuring, removing stones, etc. This is done by the gangs, or organized bands, who live in the open villages.

<!-- ch25-p243 -->
The gang consists of 10 to 40 or 50 persons: women, young persons of both sexes (13-18 years of age, although the boys are for the most part dropped at the age of 13), and children of both sexes (6-13 years of age). At its head is the gang-master, always an ordinary agricultural worker, generally what is called a bad lot, a rascal, unsteady and drunken, but with a dash of enterprise and know-how. He is the recruiting-sergeant for the gang, which works under him, not under the farmer. He generally arranges piece-work with the farmer, and his income, which on average is not very much above that of an ordinary agricultural worker,[^111] depends almost entirely on the skill with which he manages to extract the greatest possible amount of labor from his gang in the shortest time. The farmers have discovered that women work steadily only under the direction of men, but that women and children, once set going, spend their life-force recklessly – as Fourier knew – while the adult male worker is shrewd enough to save his as much as he can. The gang-master goes from one farm to another, and so employs his gang for 6 to 8 months of the year. Employment by him is therefore much more profitable and more certain for the working families than employment by the individual farmer, who employs children only occasionally. This fact fastens his influence on the open villages so completely that children can generally be hired only through him. Hiring these children out individually, apart from the gang, is his second trade.

[^111]: Some gang-masters, however, have worked their way up to become farmers of 500 acres, or owners of whole rows of houses.

<!-- ch25-p244 -->
The “drawbacks” of the system are the overwork of the children and young persons, the enormous marches they make every day to and from the farms 5, 6, and sometimes 7 miles away, and finally the moral corruption of the gang. Although the gang-master, who in some districts is called “the driver,” is armed with a long stick, he uses it only seldom, and complaints of brutal treatment are exceptional. He is a democratic emperor, or a kind of Pied Piper of Hamelin. He must therefore be popular with his subjects, and he binds them to himself by the charms of the roving, vagabond life under his direction. Coarse freedom, noisy jollity, and the most obscene impudence make the gang attractive. Generally the gang-master settles up in a pub; then he returns home at the head of the procession, reeling drunk, propped up right and left by a strapping, domineering woman, while children and young persons bring up the rear, rowdy, singing teasing and bawdy songs. On the return journey, what Fourier calls “phanerogamie” [open coupling] is the order of the day. It is common for girls of 13 and 14 to be made pregnant by their male companions of the same age. The open villages that supply the gang’s recruits become Sodoms and Gomorrahs,[^112] and have twice as high a rate of illegitimate births as the rest of the kingdom. The moral character of girls brought up in these schools, once they are married women, was shown above. Their children, when opium does not finish them off, are born recruits of the gang.

[^112]: “Half the girls of Ludford have been ruined by going out” (in gangs). Cited above, p. 6, § 32.

<!-- ch25-p245 -->
The gang in the classic form just described is called the public, common, or tramping gang. For there are also private gangs. These are made up in the same way as the common gang, but have fewer members, and work not under a gang-master but under some old farm servant whom the farmer does not know how to employ in any better way. The vagabond fun has vanished here, but according to all witnesses the children are paid and treated worse.

<!-- ch25-p246 -->
The gang system, which has steadily grown in recent years,[^113] clearly does not exist for the sake of the gang-master. It exists to enrich the large farmers,[^114] and indirectly the landlords.[^115] For the farmer there is no more ingenious method of keeping his workers well below the normal level and yet always having an extra worker ready for extra work, of extracting the greatest possible amount of labor with the least possible amount of money,[^116] and of making adult male labor “redundant.” From what has already been explained, it will be understood why, on the one hand, a greater or lesser lack of work for the agricultural worker is admitted, while on the other hand the gang system is at the same time declared “necessary” because of the shortage of adult male labor and its migration to the towns.[^117] The cleanly weeded land and the uncleanly human weeds of Lincolnshire are pole and counterpole of capitalist production.[^118]

[^113]: “They (gangs) have greatly increased of late years. In some places they are said to have been introduced at comparatively late dates; in others where gangs ... have been known for many years ... more and younger children are employed in them.” (cited above, p. 79, § 174).

[^114]: “Small farmers never employ gangs.” “It is not on poor land, but on land which affords rent of from 40 to 50 shillings, that women and children are employed in the greatest numbers.” (cited above, pp. 17, 14.)

[^115]: One of these gentlemen found the taste of his rent so pleasing that he indignantly declared to the Commission of Inquiry that the whole fuss was due only to the name of the system. If, instead of “gang,” it were called “the Agricultural Juvenile Industrial Self-supporting Association,” everything would be all right.

[^116]: “Gang work is cheaper than other work; that is why they are employed,” says a former gang-master (cited above, p. 17, § 14). “The gang-system is decidedly the cheapest for the farmer, and decidedly the worst for the children,” says a farmer (cited above, p. 16, § 3.)

[^117]: “Undoubtedly much of the work now done by children in gangs used to be done by men and women. More men are out of work now where children and women are employed than formerly.” (cited above, p. 43, n. 202.) On the other hand, “the labour question in some agricultural districts, particularly the arable, is becoming so serious in consequence of emigration, and the facility afforded by railways for getting to large towns that I (the “I” is the steward of a great lord) think the services of children are most indispensable,” (cited above, p. 80, n. 180.) For the “labour question” in English agricultural districts, unlike in the rest of the civilized world, means the landlords’ and farmers’ question, namely: how is it possible, despite an ever-growing exodus of the country people, to keep up a sufficient relative surplus population in the countryside, and by means of it to keep the wages of the agricultural worker at a minimum?

[^118]: The “Public Health Report,” which deals with the gang system in passing when it treats children’s mortality, remains unknown to the press, and therefore to the English public. On the other hand, the last report of the “Child. Empl. Comm.” gave the press the sensational copy it always welcomes. While the Liberal press asked how the fine gentlemen and ladies, and the well-paid clergy of the State Church, who swarm in Lincolnshire, could allow such a system to arise on their estates, under their very eyes – they who send out special missions to the Antipodes “for the improvement of the morals of South Sea Islanders” — the more refined press confined itself to reflections on the coarse degradation of the agricultural population, who are capable of selling their children into such slavery! Under the accursed conditions to which these “delicate” people condemn the agricultural worker, it would not be surprising if he ate his own children. What is really wonderful is the healthy integrity of character that he has, in great part, kept. The official reports prove that the parents, even in the gang districts, loathe the gang system. “There is much in the evidence that shows that the parents of the children would, in many instances, be glad to be aided by the requirements of a legal obligation, to resist the pressure and the temptations to which they are often subject. They are liable to be urged, at times by the parish officers, at times by employers, under threats of being themselves discharged, to be taken to work at an age when ... school attendance ... would be manifestly to their greater advantage.... All that time and strength wasted; all the suffering from extra and unprofitable fatigue produced to the labourer and to his children; every instance in which the parent may have traced the moral ruin of his child to the undermining of delicacy by the over-crowding of cottages, or to the contaminating influences of the public gang, must have been so many incentives to feelings in the minds of the labouring poor which can be well understood, and which it would be needless to particularise. They must be conscious that much bodily and mental pain has thus been inflicted upon them from causes for which they were in no way answerable; to which, had it been in their power, they would have in no way consented; and against which they were powerless to struggle.” (cited above, p. xx., § 82, and xxiii., n. 96.)
