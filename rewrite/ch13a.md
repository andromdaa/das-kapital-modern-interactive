## Chapter 13: Cooperation

<!-- ch13-p001 -->
As we have already seen, capitalist production really begins only when each individual capital employs a comparatively large number of workers at the same time, and when, as a result, the labor process is carried on on a large scale and yields relatively large quantities of products. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. The labor process is the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things.) A larger number of workers working together, at the same time, in one place (or, if you like, in the same field of labor), to produce the same sort of commodity under the mastery of one capitalist, is the starting-point of capitalist production, both historically and logically. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use.) As far as the mode of production itself is concerned, manufacture in the strict sense of the word can hardly be told apart, in its earliest stages, from the handicraft trades of the guilds, except by the greater number of workers employed at the same time by one and the same individual capital. (Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) The workshop of the medieval master craftsman is simply made larger.

<!-- ch13-p002 -->
So at first the difference is purely quantitative. We have shown that the surplus-value produced by a given capital is equal to the surplus-value produced by each worker multiplied by the number of workers employed at the same time. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. Labor-power is a person’s capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) The number of workers does not in itself affect either the rate of surplus-value or the degree of exploitation of labor-power. (The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital, that is, of surplus labor to necessary labor.) If a working day of 12 hours is embodied in six shillings, 1,200 such days will be embodied in 1,200 times 6 shillings. (The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor.) In one case 12 × 1,200 working hours are incorporated in the product, and in the other 12 such hours. In the production of value, a number of workers count merely as so many individual workers. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) So it makes no difference to the value produced whether the 1,200 men work separately or are united under the control of one capitalist.

<!-- ch13-p003 -->
Nevertheless, within certain limits, a change does take place. The labor realized in value is labor of an average social quality, and so it is the expenditure of average labor-power. Any average magnitude, however, is merely the average of a number of separate magnitudes, all of one kind but differing in quantity. In every industry, each individual worker, be he Peter or Paul, differs from the average worker. These individual differences, or “errors” as they are called in mathematics, cancel one another out and vanish whenever a certain minimum number of workers are employed together. The celebrated sophist and sycophant Edmund Burke goes so far as to make the following assertion, based on his practical observations as a farmer: namely, that “in so small a platoon” as that of five farm laborers, all individual differences in the labor vanish, and that therefore any five adult farm laborers taken together will do as much work in the same time as any other five.[^1] But however that may be, it is clear that the collective working day of a large number of workers employed at the same time, divided by the number of these workers, gives one day of average social labor. For example, let the working day of each individual be 12 hours. Then the collective working day of 12 men employed at the same time consists of 144 hours. The labor of each of the dozen men may deviate more or less from average social labor, each of them needing a different time for the same operation. Yet since the working day of each is one-twelfth of the collective working day of 144 hours, it has the qualities of an average social working day. From the point of view of the capitalist who employs these 12 men, however, the working day is that of the whole dozen. Each individual man’s day is an equal fraction of the collective working day. It makes no difference whether the 12 men help one another in their work, or whether the only connection between their operations is the fact that they are all working for the same capitalist. But if the 12 men are employed in six pairs, by as many different small employers, it will be quite a matter of chance whether each of these employers produces the same value, and so whether he realizes the general rate of surplus-value. Deviations would occur in individual cases. Suppose one worker needed considerably more time to produce a commodity than is socially necessary. Then in his case the length of the necessary labor-time would noticeably deviate from the labor-time socially necessary on average. (Socially necessary labor-time is the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor.) So his labor would not count as average labor, nor his labor-power as average labor-power. It would either not be saleable at all, or only for something below the average value of labor-power, that is, below the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker (and to raise the next generation of workers) able to work. A fixed minimum of efficiency in all labor is therefore assumed, and we shall see later on that capitalist production provides the means of fixing this minimum. Nevertheless, this minimum deviates from the average, although on the other hand the capitalist has to pay the average value of labor-power. So of the six small employers, one would squeeze out more than the average rate of surplus-value, another less. The inequalities would balance out for society at large, but not for the individual employers. Thus the laws of the production of value are only fully realized for the individual producer when he produces as a capitalist and employs a number of workers together, whose labor, by its collective nature, is at once stamped as average social labor.[^2]

[^1]: “Unquestionably, there is a good deal of difference between the value of one man’s labour and that of another from strength, dexterity, and honest application. But I am quite sure, from my best observation, that any given five men will, in their total, afford a proportion of labour equal to any other five within the periods of life I have stated; that is, that among such five men there will be one possessing all the qualifications of a good workman, one bad, and the other three middling, and approximating to the first, and the last. So that in so small a platoon as that of even five, you will find the full complement of all that five men can earn.” (E. Burke, cited above, pp. 15, 16.) Compare Quételet on the average individual.

[^2]: Professor Roscher claims to have discovered that one needlewoman employed by Mrs. Roscher for two days does more work than two needlewomen employed together for one day. The learned professor should not study the capitalist process of production in the nursery, nor in circumstances where the principal character, the capitalist, is missing.

Even without any change in the method of working, employing a large number of workers at the same time brings about a revolution in the material conditions of the labor process. The buildings in which they work, the storehouses for the raw material, the implements and utensils used by the workers at the same time or in turns, in short, a portion of the means of production, are now consumed in common. (The means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things.) On the one hand, the exchange-value of these means of production is not increased, for the exchange-value of a commodity is not raised by its use-value being consumed more thoroughly and to greater advantage. (Exchange-value is the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for. Use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties.) On the other hand, they are used in common, and therefore on a larger scale than before. A room where twenty weavers work at twenty looms must be larger than the room of a single weaver with two assistants. But it costs less labor to build one workshop for twenty persons than to build ten workshops for two weavers each. So the value of the means of production that are concentrated for use in common on a large scale does not increase in direct proportion to their expansion and to their increased useful effect. When consumed in common, they give up a smaller part of their value to each single product. This is partly because the total value they part with is spread over a greater quantity of products. It is partly because their value, though greater in absolute terms, is relatively less than the value of isolated means of production, when we consider their sphere of action in the process. Because of this, the value of a part of the constant capital falls, and the total value of the commodity falls in proportion to the size of that fall. (Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production. Its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) The effect is the same as if the means of production had cost less. The saving in their use is due entirely to their being consumed in common by a large number of workers. Moreover, they acquire this character of being necessary conditions of social labor even when the many workers gathered together do not help one another, but merely work side by side. It is a character that distinguishes them from the scattered and relatively more costly means of production of isolated, independent workers, or of small employers. A portion of the instruments of labor acquires this social character before the labor process itself does.

<!-- ch13-p005 -->
Saving in the use of the means of production has to be looked at from two sides. First, as cheapening commodities, and thereby bringing about a fall in the value of labor-power. Second, as changing the ratio of the surplus-value to the total capital advanced, that is, to the sum of the values of the constant and variable capital. (Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power. It reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies.) The second side will not be considered until we come to the third book. To that book we also put off many other points relating to the present question, so as to treat them in their proper connection. The course of our analysis forces us to split up the subject-matter in this way, and this splitting up is quite in keeping with the spirit of capitalist production. For in this mode of production the worker finds the instruments of labor existing independently of him, as another man’s property. So, as far as he is concerned, saving in their use appears to be a separate operation, one that does not concern him, and which therefore has no connection with the methods by which his own personal productivity is increased.

When many workers work together side by side, whether in one and the same process, or in different but connected processes, they are said to cooperate, or to work in cooperation.[^3]

[^3]: “Concours de forces.” [Combination of forces] (Destutt de Tracy, cited above, p. 80.)

<!-- ch13-p007 -->
The attacking power of a squadron of cavalry, or the defensive power of a regiment of infantry, is essentially different from the sum of the attacking or defensive powers of the individual cavalry or infantry soldiers taken separately. In just the same way, the sum total of the mechanical forces exerted by isolated workers differs from the social force that is developed when many hands take part at the same time in one and the same undivided operation, such as raising a heavy weight, turning a winch, or removing an obstacle.[^4] In such cases the effect of the combined labor either could not be produced at all by isolated individual labor, or could be produced only by a great expenditure of time, or only on a very stunted scale. Here we have not only an increase, by means of cooperation, in the productive power of the individual, but the creation of a new power, namely, the collective power of masses.[^5]

[^4]: “There are numerous operations of so simple a kind as not to admit a division into parts, which cannot be performed without the co-operation of many pairs of hands. I would instance the lifting of a large tree on to a wain [wagon] ... everything, in short, which cannot be done unless a great many pairs of hands help each other in the same undivided employment and at the same time.” (E. G. Wakefield: “A View of the Art of Colonisation.” London, 1849, p. 168.)

[^5]: “As one man cannot, and ten men must strain to lift a ton of weight, yet 100 men can do it only by the strength of a finger of each of them.” (John Betters: “Proposals for Raising a Colledge of Industry.” London, 1696, p. 21.)

Apart from the new power that arises from the fusion of many forces into one single force, in most industries mere social contact gives rise to rivalry and a stirring of the animal spirits that raise the efficiency of each individual worker. That is why a dozen persons working together will, in their collective working day of 144 hours, produce far more than twelve isolated men each working 12 hours, or than one man who works twelve days in a row.[^6] The reason for this is that man is, if not a political animal,[^7] as Aristotle contends, at any rate a social animal.

[^6]: “There is also” (when the same number of men are employed by one farmer on 300 acres, instead of by ten farmers with 30 acres a piece) “an advantage in the proportion of servants, which will not so easily be understood but by practical men; for it is natural to say, as 1 is to 4, so are 3 to 12; but this will not hold good in practice; for in harvest time and many other operations which require that kind of despatch by the throwing many hands together, the work is better and more expeditiously done: f i. [for instance] in harvest, 2 drivers, 2 loaders, 2 pitchers, 2 rakers, and the rest at the rick [haystack], or in the barn, will despatch double the work that the same number of hands would do if divided into different gangs on different farms.” (“An Inquiry into the Connexion between the Present Price of Provisions and the Size of Farms.” By a Farmer. London, 1773, pp. 7, 8.)

[^7]: Strictly, Aristotle’s definition is that man is by nature a town-citizen. This is quite as characteristic of ancient classical society as Franklin’s definition of man as a tool-making animal is characteristic of Yankeedom.

A number of men may be occupied together at the same time on the same work, or the same kind of work. Yet the labor of each, as a part of the collective labor, may correspond to a distinct phase of the labor process. Because of cooperation, the object of their labor passes more quickly through all these phases. For instance, suppose a dozen masons place themselves in a row, so as to pass stones from the foot of a ladder to its top. Each of them does the same thing. Nevertheless, their separate acts form connected parts of one total operation. They are particular phases that each stone must go through, and so the stones are carried up more quickly by the 24 hands of the row of men than they could be if each man went up and down the ladder separately with his load.[^8] The object is carried over the same distance in a shorter time. Again, labor is combined whenever a building, for instance, is worked on from different sides at the same time, although here too the cooperating masons are doing the same work, or the same kind of work. The 12 masons, in their collective working day of 144 hours, make much more progress with the building than one mason could make working for 12 days, or 144 hours. The reason is that a body of men working in concert has hands and eyes both in front and behind, and is, to a certain degree, everywhere at once. The various parts of the work progress at the same time.

[^8]: “On doit encore remarquer que cette division partielle de travail peut se faire quand même les ouvriers sont occupés d’une même besogne. Des maçons par exemple, occupés à faire passer de mains en mains des briques à un échafaudage supérieur, font tous la même besogne, et pourtant il existe parmi eux une espèce de division de travail, qui consiste en ce que chacun d’eux fait passer la brique par un espace donné, et que tous ensemble la font parvenir beaucoup plus promptement à l’endroit marqué qu’ils ne le feraient si chacun d’eux portait sa brique séparément jusqu’à l’échafaudage supérieur.” [It should also be noted that this partial division of labor can happen even when the workers are busy with one and the same task. Masons, for example, who are busy passing bricks from hand to hand up to a higher scaffold, are all doing the same task, and yet there is a kind of division of labor among them. It consists in this: each of them passes the brick through a given space, and all of them together make it reach the marked spot much more quickly than they would if each of them carried his brick separately up to the higher scaffold.] (F. Skarbek: “Théorie des richesses sociales.” Paris, 1839, t. I, pp. 97, 98.)

In the examples above we have stressed that the men do the same work, or the same kind of work. We did so because this, the simplest form of labor in common, plays a great part in cooperation, even in its most fully developed stage. If the work is complicated, then the mere number of men who cooperate allows the various operations to be shared out among different workers, and so to be carried on at the same time. The time needed to complete the whole work is shortened in this way.[^9]

[^9]: “Est-il question d’exécuter un travail compliqué, plusieurs choses doivent être faites simultanément. L’un en fait une pendant que l’autre en fait une autre, et tous contribuent à l’effet qu’un seul homme n’aurait pu produire. L’un rame pendant que l’autre tient le gouvernail, et qu’un troisième jette le filet on harponne le poisson, et la pêche a un succès impossible sans ce concours.” [Is it a matter of carrying out a complicated piece of work? Then several things must be done at the same time. One person does one thing while another does something else, and they all contribute to an effect that a single man could not have produced. One rows while another holds the rudder, and a third casts the net or harpoons the fish, and the fishing has a success that would be impossible without this combined effort.] (Destutt de Tracy, cited above.)

In many industries there are critical periods, set by the nature of the process, during which certain definite results must be obtained. For instance, if a flock of sheep has to be shorn, or a field of wheat cut and harvested, the quantity and quality of the product depend on the work being begun and finished within a certain time. In these cases the time the process ought to take is fixed in advance, just as it is in herring fishing. A single person cannot carve a working day of more than, say, 12 hours out of the natural day, but 100 men cooperating extend the working day to 1,200 hours. The shortness of the time allowed for the work is made up for by the large mass of labor thrown onto the field of production at the decisive moment. Finishing the task in the proper time depends on applying many combined working days at the same time. The amount of useful effect depends on the number of workers. This number, however, is always smaller than the number of isolated workers needed to do the same amount of work in the same period.[^10] It is because this kind of cooperation is missing that every year quantities of grain are wasted in the western part of the United States, and quantities of cotton in those parts of East India where English rule has destroyed the old communities.[^11]

[^11]: “The next evil is one which one would scarcely expect to find in a country which exports more labour than any other in the world, with the exception, perhaps, of China and England ‒ the impossibility of procuring a sufficient number of hands to clean the cotton.
