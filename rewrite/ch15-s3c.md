#### C. Intensification of Labor

<!-- ch15-p071 -->
Machinery in the hands of capital produces an immoderate lengthening of the working day. This leads to a reaction on the part of society, whose very sources of life are threatened, and from there to a normal working day whose length is fixed by law. From then on, a phenomenon we have already met with, namely the intensification of labor, grows to great importance. Our analysis of absolute surplus-value dealt mainly with the extension or duration of labor, and took its intensity as given. We now go on to consider how more intensified labor is substituted for labor of longer duration, and the degree of that more intensified labor.

<!-- ch15-p072 -->
It is self-evident that as the use of machinery spreads, and as a special class of workers used to machinery gathers experience, the speed and intensity of labor increase as a natural consequence. Thus in England, for half a century, the lengthening of the working day went hand in hand with the increasing intensity of factory labor. Nevertheless, the reader will clearly see the following. Where labor is not carried on by fits and starts, but repeated day after day with unvarying uniformity, a point must inevitably be reached where extending the working day and intensifying the labor exclude one another. Lengthening the working day then becomes compatible only with a lower degree of intensity, and a higher degree of intensity only with a shorter working day. The gradually surging revolt of the working class compelled Parliament to shorten the hours of labor by law, and to begin by imposing a normal working day on factories proper. As soon as that happened, and so as soon as an increased production of surplus-value by lengthening the working day was stopped once and for all, capital threw itself with all its might into producing relative surplus-value, by hurrying on the further improvement of machinery. At the same time a change took place in the nature of relative surplus-value. Generally speaking, relative surplus-value is produced by raising the productive power of the worker, so that he can produce more in a given time with the same expenditure of labor. Labor-time goes on transferring the same value to the total product as before, but this unchanged amount of exchange-value is spread over more use-value; hence the value of each single commodity falls. It is otherwise, however, as soon as the hours of labor are shortened by law. The immense impetus this gives to the development of productive power, and to economy in the means of production, imposes on the worker an increased expenditure of labor in a given time, a heightened tension of labor-power, and a closer filling up of the pores of the working day, or condensation of labor, to a degree that can be reached only within the limits of the shortened working day. From then on, this condensation of a greater mass of labor into a given period counts for what it really is: a greater quantity of labor. Besides a measure of its extension, that is, its duration, labor now acquires a measure of its intensity, or of the degree of its condensation or density.[^75] The denser hour of the ten hours' working day contains more labor, that is, more expended labor-power, than the more porous hour of the twelve hours' working day. So the product of one of the denser hours has as much value as the product of 1 1/5 of the more porous hours, or more. Leaving aside the increased yield of relative surplus-value through the higher productivity of labor, the same mass of value is now produced for the capitalist by, say, 3 1/3 hours of surplus labor and 6 2/3 hours of necessary labor, as was produced before by four hours of surplus labor and eight hours of necessary labor.

[^75]: There are, of course, always differences in the intensity of labor in various industries. But, as Adam Smith has shown, these differences are partly compensated by minor circumstances peculiar to each sort of labor. Labor-time as a measure of value is not affected in this case, however, except insofar as the duration of labor and the degree of its intensity are two opposite and mutually exclusive expressions for one and the same quantity of labor.

<!-- ch15-p073 -->
We now come to the question: How is labor intensified?

<!-- ch15-p074 -->
The first effect of shortening the working day follows from a self-evident law: the efficiency of labor-power is in inverse ratio to the length of time over which it is spent. Hence, within certain limits, what is lost by shortening the duration is gained through the increased tension of labor-power. Moreover, the way the capitalist pays the worker ensures that the worker really does spend more labor-power.[^76] In industries such as potteries, where machinery plays little or no part, the introduction of the Factory Acts has strikingly shown that merely shortening the working day increases to a wonderful degree the regularity, uniformity, order, continuity, and energy of the labor.[^77] It seemed doubtful, however, whether this effect was produced in the factory proper, where the worker's dependence on the continuous and uniform motion of the machinery had already created the strictest discipline. Hence, when the reduction of the working day to less than twelve hours was being debated in 1844, the employers almost unanimously declared

[^77]: See “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st October, 1865.”

<!-- ch15-p075 -->
> “that their overlookers in the different rooms took good care that the hands lost no time,” that “the extent of vigilance and attention on the part of the workmen was hardly capable of being increased,” and, therefore, that the speed of the machinery and other conditions remaining unaltered, “to expect in a well-managed factory any important result from increased attention of the workmen was an absurdity.”[^78]

[^78]: Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 1844 and the quarter ending 30th April, 1845, pp. 20-21.

<!-- ch15-p076 -->
Experiments contradicted this assertion. Mr. Robert Gardner reduced the hours of labor in his two large factories at Preston from twelve to eleven hours a day, starting on the 20th April, 1844. After about a year's working the result was that “the same amount of product for the same cost was received, and the workpeople as a whole earned in eleven hours as much wages as they did before in twelve.”[^79] I pass over the experiments made in the spinning and carding rooms, because they went together with an increase of 2% in the speed of the machines. But in the weaving department, where, moreover, many sorts of patterned fancy articles were woven, there was not the slightest change in the conditions of the work. The result was: “From 6th January to 20th April, 1844, with a twelve hours’ day, average weekly wages of each hand 10s. 1½d., from 20th April to 29th June, 1844, with day of eleven hours, average weekly wages 10s. 3½d.”[^80] Here we have more produced in eleven hours than before in twelve, and entirely because the workers applied themselves more steadily and used their time more economically. While they got the same wages and gained one hour of free time, the capitalist got the same amount produced and saved the cost of coal, gas, and other such items for one hour. Similar experiments, with the same success, were carried out in the mills of Messrs. Horrocks and Jacson.[^81]

[^79]: Cited above, p. 19. Since the wages for piece-work were unchanged, the weekly wages depended on the quantity produced.

[^80]: Cited above, p. 20.

[^81]: The moral element played an important part in the experiments above. The workers told the factory inspector: “We work with more spirit, we have the reward ever before us of getting away sooner at night, and one active and cheerful spirit pervades the whole mill, from the youngest piecer to the oldest hand, and we can greatly help each other.” (Cited above, p. 21.)

<!-- ch15-p077 -->
To begin with, the shortening of the hours of labor creates the subjective conditions for condensing labor, by enabling the worker to exert more strength in a given time. As soon as that shortening becomes compulsory, machinery becomes, in the hands of capital, the objective means, systematically used, for squeezing out more labor in a given time. This is done in two ways: by increasing the speed of the machinery, and by giving the worker more machinery to tend. Improved construction of the machinery is necessary, partly because without it greater pressure cannot be put on the worker, and partly because the shortened hours of labor force the capitalist to keep the strictest watch over the cost of production. The improvements in the steam-engine have increased the piston speed. At the same time, by means of a greater economy of power, they have made it possible to drive more machinery with the same engine, with the same or even a smaller consumption of coal. The improvements in the transmitting mechanism have lessened friction. They have also reduced the diameter and weight of the shafting to a constantly decreasing minimum, and this is what so strikingly distinguishes modern machinery from the older kind. Finally, the improvements in the working machines have had several effects. In some cases they have reduced the machines' size while increasing their speed and efficiency, as in the modern power-loom. In others they have enlarged the framework and at the same time increased the extent and number of the working parts, as in spinning-mules. Or they have added to the speed of these working parts by imperceptible changes of detail, such as those which ten years ago increased the speed of the spindles in self-acting mules by one-fifth.

<!-- ch15-p078 -->
In England the reduction of the working day to 12 hours dates from 1832. In 1836 a manufacturer stated:

<!-- ch15-p079 -->
> “The labour now undergone in the factories is much greater than it used to be ... compared with thirty or forty years ago ... owing to the greater attention and activity required by the greatly increased speed which is given to the machinery.”[^82]

[^82]: John Fielden, cited above, p. 32.

<!-- ch15-p080 -->
In 1844 Lord Ashley, now Lord Shaftesbury, made the following statements in the House of Commons, backed by documentary evidence:

<!-- ch15-p081 -->
> “The labour performed by those engaged in the processes of manufacture, is three times as great as in the beginning of such operations. Machinery has executed, no doubt, the work that would demand the sinews of millions of men; but it has also prodigiously multiplied the labour of those who are governed by its fearful movements.... In 1815, the labour of following a pair of mules spinning cotton of No. 40 – reckoning 12 hours to the working day – involved a necessity of walking 8 miles. In 1832, the distance travelled in following a pair of mules, spinning cotton yarn of the same number, was 20 miles, and frequently more. In 1835” (query – 1815 or 1825?) “the spinner put up daily, on each of these mules, 820 stretches, making a total of 1,640 stretches in the course of the day. In 1832, the spinner put up on each mule 2,200 stretches, making a total of 4,400. In 1844, 2,400 stretches, making a total of 4,800; and in some cases the amount of labour required is even still greater.... I have another document sent to me in 1842, stating that the labour is progressively increasing ‒ increasing not only because the distance to be travelled is greater, but because the quantity of goods produced is multiplied, while the hands are fewer in proportion than before; and, moreover, because an inferior species of cotton is now often spun, which it is more difficult to work.... In the carding-room there has also been a great increase of labour. One person there does the work formerly divided between two. In the weaving-room, where a vast number of persons are employed, and principally females ... the labour has increased within the last few years fully 10 per cent., owing to the increased speed of the machinery in spinning. In 1838, the number of hanks spun per week was 18,000, in 1843 it amounted to 21,000. In 1819, the number of picks in power-loom-weaving per minute was 60 – in 1842 it was 140, showing a vast increase of labour.”[^83]

[^83]: Lord Ashley, cited above, pp. 6-9, passim.

<!-- ch15-p082 -->
At that time the English manufacturers asserted that any further progress in that direction was impossible, and therefore that every further reduction of the hours of labor meant less production. In the face of this remarkable intensity of labor, already reached in 1844 under the Twelve Hours' Act, there appeared to be a justification for this assertion. The apparent correctness of their reasons will be best shown by the following statement made at the time by Leonard Horner, the factory inspector, their ever watchful censor.

<!-- ch15-p083 -->
> “Now, as the quantity produced must, in the main, be regulated by the speed of the machinery, it must be the interest of the mill-owner to drive it at the utmost rate of speed consistent with these following conditions, viz., the preservation of the machinery from too rapid deterioration; the preservation of the quality of the article manufactured; and the capability of the workman to follow the motion without a greater exertion than he can sustain for a constancy [continuously]. One of the most important problems, therefore, which the owner of a factory has to solve is to find out the maximum speed at which he can run, with a due regard to the above conditions. It frequently happens that he finds he has gone too fast, that breakages and bad work more than counterbalance the increased speed, and that he is obliged to slacken his pace. I therefore concluded, that as an active and intelligent mill-owner would find out the safe maximum, it would not be possible to produce as much in eleven hours as in twelve. I further assumed that the operative paid by piecework, would exert himself to the utmost consistent with the power of continuing at the same rate.”[^84]

[^84]: Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for Quarter ending 30th September, 1844, and from 1st October, 1844, to 30th April, 1845, p. 20.

<!-- ch15-p084 -->
Horner therefore concluded that reducing the working hours below twelve would necessarily reduce production.[^85] Ten years later he himself cites his opinion of 1845 as proof of how much he had underestimated, in that year, the elasticity of machinery and of human labor-power, both of which are stretched to an extreme at the same time by the compulsory shortening of the working day.

[^85]: Cited above, p. 22.

<!-- ch15-p085 -->
We now come to the period that follows the introduction of the Ten Hours' Act in 1847 into the English cotton, woolen, silk, and flax mills.

<!-- ch15-p086 -->
> “The speed of the spindles has increased upon throstles 500, and upon mules 1,000 revolutions a minute, i.e., the speed of the throstle spindle, which in 1839 was 4,500 times a minute, is now (1862) 5,000; and of the mule spindle, that was 5,000, is now 6,000 times a minute, amounting in the former case to one-tenth, and in the second case to one-fifth additional increase.”[^86]

[^86]: “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st October, 1862,” p. 62.

<!-- ch15-p087 -->
James Nasmyth, the eminent civil engineer of Patricroft, near Manchester, explained in a letter to Leonard Horner, written in 1852, the nature of the improvements in the steam-engine made between 1848 and 1852. He first remarks that the horse-power of steam-engines, which the official returns always estimate according to the power of similar engines in 1828[^87], is only nominal, and can serve only as an index of their real power. Then he goes on to say:

[^87]: This was changed in the “Parliamentary Return” of 1862. There the actual horse-power of the modern steam engines and water wheels appears instead of the nominal horse-power. The doubling spindles, too, are no longer included among the spinning spindles (as they were in the “Returns” of 1839, 1850, and 1856). Further, for woolen mills the number of “gigs” [machines for raising the nap on cloth] is added; a distinction is made between jute and hemp mills on the one hand and flax mills on the other; and finally stocking-weaving is included in the report for the first time.

<!-- ch15-p088 -->
> “I am confident that from the same weight of steam-engine machinery, we are now obtaining at least 50 per cent. more duty or work performed on the average, and that in many cases the identical steam-engines which in the days of the restricted speed of 220 feet per minute, yielded 50 horsepower, are now yielding upwards of 100...” “The modern steam-engine of 100 horse-power is capable of being driven at a much greater force than formerly, arising from improvements in its construction, the capacity and construction of the boilers, &c....” “Although the same number of hands are employed in proportion to the horse-power as at former periods, there are fewer hands employed in proportion to the machinery.”[^88] “In the year 1850, the factories of the United Kingdom employed 134,217 nominal horse-power to give motion to 25,638,716 spindles and 301,445 looms. The number of spindles and looms in 1856 was respectively 33,503,580 of the former, and 369,205 of the latter, which, reckoning the force of the nominal horse-power required to be the same as in 1850, would require a force equal to 175,000 horses, but the actual power given in the return for 1856 is 161,435, less by above 10,000 horses than, calculating upon the basis of the return of 1850, the factories ought to have required in 1856.”[^89] “The facts thus brought out by the Return (of 1856) appear to be that the factory system is increasing rapidly; that although the same number of hands are employed in proportion to the horse-power as at former periods, there are fewer hands employed in proportion to the machinery; that the steam-engine is enabled to drive an increased weight of machinery by economy of force and other methods, and that an increased quantity of work can be turned off by improvements in machinery, and in methods of manufacture, by increase of speed of the machinery, and by a variety of other causes.”[^90]

[^88]: “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st October, 1856,” pp. 13-14, 20 and 1852, p. 23.

[^89]: Cited above, pp. 14-15.

[^90]: Cited above, p. 20.

<!-- ch15-p089 -->
> “The great improvements made in machines of every kind have raised their productive power very much. Without any doubt, the shortening of the hours of labour... gave the impulse to these improvements. The latter, combined with the more intense strain on the workman, have had the effect, that at least as much is produced in the shortened (by two hours or one-sixth) working day as was previously produced during the longer one.”[^91]

[^91]: “Reports, &c., for 31st October, 1858,” pp. 9-10. Compare “Reports, &c., for 30th April, 1860,” p. 30 and following.

<!-- ch15-p090 -->
One fact is enough to show how greatly the wealth of the manufacturers grew along with the more intense exploitation of labor-power. From 1838 to 1850 the average proportional increase in English cotton and other factories was 32%, while from 1850 to 1856 it amounted to 86%.

<!-- ch15-p091 -->
But however great the progress of English industry had been during the 8 years from 1848 to 1856 under a working day of 10 hours, it was far surpassed during the next period of 6 years, from 1856 to 1862. In silk factories, for instance, there were 1,093,799 spindles in 1856 and 1,388,544 in 1862; there were 9,260 looms in 1856 and 10,709 in 1862. But the number of factory workers was 56,131 in 1856 and 52,429 in 1862. So the spindles increased by 26.9% and the looms by 15.6%, while the number of factory workers fell by 7%. In 1850 worsted mills employed 875,830 spindles; in 1856, 1,324,549 (an increase of 51.2%); and in 1862, 1,289,172 (a decrease of 2.7%). But if we deduct the doubling spindles, which figure in the numbers for 1856 but not in those for 1862, we find that after 1856 the number of spindles stayed nearly stationary. On the other hand, after 1850 the speed of the spindles and looms was in many cases doubled. The number of power-looms in worsted mills was 32,617 in 1850, 38,956 in 1856, and 43,048 in 1862. The number of factory workers was 79,737 in 1850, 87,794 in 1856, and 86,063 in 1862. Included in these, however, were the children under 14 years of age: 9,956 in 1850, 11,228 in 1856, and 13,178 in 1862. So, despite the greatly increased number of looms in 1862 compared with 1856, the total number of workers employed fell, and the number of children exploited rose.[^92]

[^92]: “Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1862,” pp. 100 and 130.

<!-- ch15-p092 -->
On the 27th April, 1863, Mr. Ferrand said in the House of Commons:

<!-- ch15-p093 -->
> “I have been informed by delegates from 16 districts of Lancashire and Cheshire, in whose behalf I speak, that the work in the factories is, in consequence of the improvements in machinery, constantly on the increase. Instead of as formerly one person with two helps tenting [tending] two looms, one person now tents three looms without helps, and it is no uncommon thing for one person to tent four. Twelve hours’ work, as is evident from the facts adduced, is now compressed into less than 10 hours. It is therefore self-evident, to what an enormous extent the toil of the factory operative has increased during the last 10 years.”[^93]

[^93]: On 2 modern power-looms a weaver now makes 26 pieces of a certain quality, length, and breadth in a week of 60 hours, while on the old power-looms he could make no more than 4 such pieces. Soon after 1850, the cost of weaving a piece of such cloth had already fallen from 2s. 9d. to 5 1/8d.

    “Thirty years ago (1841) one spinner with three placers was not required to attend to more than one pair of mules with 300-324 spindles. At the present time (1871) he has to mind with the help of 5 piecers 2,200 spindles, and produces not less than seven times as much yarn as in 1841.” (Alex. Redgrave, Factory Inspector – in the *Journal of Arts*, 5th January, 1872.)

So, although the Factory Inspectors constantly and rightly praise the results of the Acts of 1844 and 1850, they admit that the shortening of the hours of labor has already called forth an intensification of labor so great that it injures the worker's health and his capacity for work.

<!-- ch15-p095 -->
> “In most of the cotton, worsted, and silk mills, an exhausting state of excitement necessary to enable the workers satisfactorily to mind the machinery, the motion of which has been greatly accelerated within the last few years, seems to me not unlikely to be one of the causes of that excess of mortality from lung disease, which Dr. Greenhow has pointed out in his recent report on this subject.”[^94]

[^94]: “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1861,” pp. 25, 26.

<!-- ch15-p096 -->
There cannot be the slightest doubt about the following. As soon as a lengthening of the hours of labor is forbidden once and for all, a tendency drives capital to make up for it by systematically raising the intensity of labor, and to turn every improvement in machinery into a more perfect means of exhausting the worker. This tendency must soon lead to a state of things in which a reduction of the hours of labor will again be inevitable.[^95] On the other hand, the rapid advance of English industry from 1848 to the present day, under a day of 10 hours, surpasses the advance made from 1833 to 1847, when the day was 12 hours long, by far more than that advance surpasses the advance made in the half century after the factory system was first introduced, when the working day had no limits.[^96]
