### Section 5: Illustrations of the General Law of Capitalist Accumulation

#### A. England from 1846-1866

<!-- ch25-p078 -->
No period of modern society is as good for studying capitalist accumulation as the last 20 years. It is as if this period had found Fortunatus' purse (the magic purse of the old tale, which never runs empty). But of all countries, England again provides the classic example. This is because it holds first place in the world market, because capitalist production is fully developed here and nowhere else, and finally because the arrival of the Free-trade millennium since 1846 has cut off the last line of retreat of vulgar economics (economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations). The giant advance of production, with the second half of the 20-year period again far outstripping the first, has already been shown well enough in Part IV.

Now let us look, on the other hand, at the increase of wealth. Here the movement of profit, rent of land, etc., which come under the income tax, gives the surest basis. In Great Britain from 1853 to 1864, profits liable to income tax (not counting farmers and some other categories) increased by 50.47%, or 4.58% as the yearly average,[^31] while the population increased by about 12% over the same period. The rise in the taxable rent of land (including houses, railways, mines, fisheries, etc.) from 1853 to 1864 came to 38%, or 3 5/12% a year. If we divide the years from 1853 to 1864 into three sets of four consecutive years each, the rate at which income grows rises steadily.[^32] For income from profits, for example, it is 1.73% a year from 1853 to 1857; 2.74% from 1857-1861; and 9.30% a year for 1861-64. The total of the incomes of the United Kingdom that come under the income tax was £307,068,898 in 1856; £328,127,416 in 1859; £351,745,241 in 1862; £359,142,897 in 1863; £362,462,279 in 1864; and £385,530,020 in 1865.[^33]

[^32]: Same source.

[^33]: These figures are good enough for comparison, but taken in absolute terms they are false, since perhaps £100,000,000 of income goes undeclared every year. The Inland Revenue Commissioners complain in every one of their reports of systematic fraud, especially by the commercial and industrial classes. So, for example: “A Joint-stock company returns £6,000 as assessable profits, the surveyor raises the amount to £88,000, and upon that sum duty is ultimately paid. Another company which returns £190,000 is finally compelled to admit that the true return should be £250,000.” (Same source, p. 42.)

The accumulation of capital went hand in hand with its concentration and centralization. No official statistics of agriculture existed for England (they did for Ireland), but 10 counties supplied them voluntarily. These statistics showed that from 1851 to 1861 the number of farms of less than 100 acres had fallen from 31,583 to 26,597, so that 5,016 had been merged into larger farms.[^34] From 1815 to 1825 no personal estate worth more than £1,000,000 came under the succession duty (the tax on inheritances). From 1825 to 1855, however, 8 did, and 4 did from 1856 to June, 1859, that is, in 4½ years.[^35] But centralization is best seen from a short analysis of Income Tax Schedule D (profits, not counting farms, etc.) for the years 1864 and 1865. I note first that incomes from this source pay income tax on everything over £60. These taxable incomes in England, Wales and Scotland came to £95,844,222 in 1864, and to £105,435,579 in 1865.[^36] The number of persons taxed was 308,416 in 1864, out of a population of 23,891,009; in 1865 it was 332,431, out of a population of 24,127,003. Let us now turn to the direct agents of this industry, the producers of this wealth: the working class.

<!-- ch25-p097 -->
> “It is one of the most melancholy features in the social state of this country,” says Gladstone, “that while there was a decrease in the consuming powers of the people, and while there was an increase in the privations and distress of the labouring class and operatives, there was at the same time a constant accumulation of wealth in the upper classes, and a constant increase of capital.”[^39]

[^39]: Gladstone in the House of Commons, Feb. 13th, 1843. *Times,* Feb. 14th, 1843 — “It is one of the most melancholy features in the social state of this country that we see, beyond the possibility of denial, that while there is at this moment a decrease in the consuming powers of the people, an increase of the pressure of privations and distress; there is at the same time a constant accumulation of wealth in the upper classes, an increase of the luxuriousness of their habits, and of their means of enjoyment.” (Hansard, 13th Feb.)

<!-- ch25-p098 -->
So spoke this oily minister in the House of Commons on February 13th, 1843. On April 16th, 1863, 20 years later, in the speech introducing his Budget, he said:

<!-- ch25-p099 -->
> “From 1842 to 1852 the taxable income of the country increased by 6 per cent.... In the 8 years from 1853 to 1861 it had increased from the basis taken in 1853 by 20 per cent.! The fact is so astonishing as to be almost incredible ... this intoxicating augmentation of wealth and power ... entirely confined to classes of property ... must be of indirect benefit to the labouring population, because it cheapens the commodities of general consumption. While the rich have been growing richer, the poor have been growing less poor. At any rate, whether the extremes of poverty are less, I do not presume to say.”[^40]

[^40]: Gladstone in the House of Commons, April 16th, 1863. *Morning Star,* April 17th.

<!-- ch25-p100 -->
What a lame anticlimax! If the working class has stayed “poor,” only “less poor,” in proportion as it produces “an intoxicating augmentation of wealth and power” for the wealthy class, then it has stayed relatively just as poor. If the extremes of poverty have not lessened, they have increased, because the extremes of wealth have. As for the cheapening of the means of subsistence, the official statistics, for example the accounts of the London Orphan Asylum, show a 20% rise in prices for the average of the three years 1860-1862, compared with 1851-1853. In the following three years, 1863-1865, the prices of meat, butter, milk, sugar, salt, coals, and a number of other necessary means of subsistence rose steadily.[^41] Gladstone's next Budget speech, of April 7th, 1864, is a Pindaric dithyramb (an ecstatic hymn of praise) on the advance of surplus-value-making and on the happiness of the people “tempered by poverty.” He speaks of masses “on the border” of pauperism, and of branches of trade in which “wages have not increased.” Finally he sums up the happiness of the working class in these words:

Professor Fawcett, who is not bound by official considerations as Gladstone is, declares flatly:

> “I do not, of course, deny that money wages have been augmented by this increase of capital (in the last ten years), but this apparent advantage is to a great extent lost, because many of the necessaries of life are becoming dearer” (he thinks this is because of the fall in value of the precious metals)... “the rich grow rapidly richer, whilst there is no perceptible advance in the comfort enjoyed by the industrial classes.... <!-- ch25-p104 -->
In the chapters on the “working day” and “machinery,” the reader has seen under what conditions the British working class created an “intoxicating augmentation of wealth and power” for the propertied classes. There we were mainly concerned with the worker in his social function. But to make the law of accumulation fully clear, we must also look at his condition outside the workshop: his food and his housing. The limits of this book force us to deal mainly with the worst-paid part of the industrial proletariat and with the agricultural workers, who together make up the majority of the working class.

<!-- ch25-p105 -->
But first, a word on official pauperism, that is, on the part of the working class that has lost the condition of its existence (the sale of labor-power) and vegetates on public charity. The official list of paupers in England[^44] numbered 851,369 persons; in 1856, 877,767; in 1865, 971,433. Because of the cotton famine, it grew in the years 1863 and 1864 to 1,079,382 and 1,014,978. The crisis of 1866 hit London hardest. In this center of the world market, which has more people than the kingdom of Scotland, it caused pauperism to rise in 1866 by 19.5% compared with 1865 and by 24.4% compared with 1864, and to rise still more in the first months of 1867 compared with 1866. Two points stand out from an analysis of the statistics of pauperism. On the one hand, the rise and fall in the number of paupers reflects the periodic changes of the industrial cycle. On the other hand, the official statistics become more and more misleading about the real extent of pauperism, in proportion as the class struggle, and therefore the class consciousness of the workers, develop along with the accumulation of capital. For example, the brutal treatment of paupers, about which the English Press (*The Times, Pall Mall Gazette,* etc.) has cried out so loudly in the last two years, is nothing new. In 1844 F. Engels showed exactly the same horrors, and exactly the same short-lived, hypocritical outcries of “sensational literature.” But the frightful increase of “deaths by starvation” in London during the last ten years proves beyond doubt the growing horror with which working people regard the slavery of the workhouse, that place of punishment for misery.[^45]

[^45]: A peculiar light is thrown on the progress made since the time of Adam Smith by the fact that he still sometimes uses the word “workhouse” to mean the same as “manufactory” (a manufacturing workshop).
