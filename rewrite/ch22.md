## Chapter 22: National Differences of Wages

<!-- ch22-p001 -->
In the 17th chapter we dealt with the many combinations that may bring about a change in the magnitude of the value of labor-power. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker (and to raise the next generation of workers) able to work; means of subsistence are the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live. We considered this magnitude either absolutely or relatively, that is, compared with surplus-value (the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it). On the other hand, the amount of the means of subsistence in which the price of labor is realized might in turn fluctuate independently of the changes in this price, or differently from them.[^1] As we have already said, simply translating the value of labor-power, or its price, as the case may be, into the outward form of wages turns all these laws into laws of the fluctuations of wages. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) What appears in these fluctuations of wages within a single country as a series of changing combinations may appear in different countries as differences between national wages that exist at the same time. So when we compare wages in different nations, we must take into account all the factors that determine changes in the amount of the value of labor-power: the price and the extent of the prime necessities of life, as they have developed naturally and historically; the cost of training the workers; the part played by the labor of women and children; the productivity of labor; and its extensive and intensive magnitude. Even the most superficial comparison requires first reducing the average day-wage for the same trades, in different countries, to a uniform working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor). After the day-wages have been reduced to the same terms in this way, the time wage must again be translated into the piece wage. (Time wages are wages paid by the hour, day, or week; piece wages are wages paid per piece of work done.) For only the piece wage can be a measure both of the productivity of labor (how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time) and of the intensity of labor (how much labor is packed into a given time, that is, how hard and fast people must work).

[^1]: “It is not accurate to say that wages” (here he is dealing with wages as expressed in money) “are increased, because they purchase more of a cheaper article.” (David Buchanan in his edition of Adam Smith’s “Wealth of Nations,” 1814, Vol. 1, p. 417, note.)

<!-- ch22-p002 -->
In every country there is a certain average intensity of labor. Below it, the labor spent producing a commodity (a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use) takes more than the socially necessary time, and so does not count as labor of normal quality. The socially necessary labor-time is the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor. In a given country, only a degree of intensity above the national average affects the measuring of value by the mere length of the working time. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) This is not the case on the world market, whose component parts are the individual countries. The average intensity of labor changes from country to country: here it is greater, there less. These national averages form a scale whose unit of measure is the average unit of universal labor. So the more intense national labor, compared with the less intense, produces more value in the same time, and this value is expressed in more money.

<!-- ch22-p003 -->
But in its international application, the law of value is modified still further by the fact that on the world market the more productive national labor also counts as the more intense, so long as competition does not force the more productive nation to lower the selling price of its commodities to the level of their value.

<!-- ch22-p004 -->
The more capitalist production develops in a country, the more the national intensity and productivity of labor there rise above the international level.[^2] (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) So the different quantities of commodities of the same kind that different countries produce in the same working time have unequal international values. These values are expressed in different prices, that is, in sums of money that vary according to the international values. (A price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) The relative value of money will therefore be lower in the nation with a more developed capitalist mode of production than in the nation with a less developed one. It follows, then, that nominal wages, the equivalent of labor-power expressed in money, will also be higher in the first nation than in the second. But this does not at all prove that the same holds for real wages, that is, for the means of subsistence placed at the disposal of the worker.

[^2]: We shall inquire elsewhere into the circumstances, connected with productivity, that may modify this law for individual branches of industry.

<!-- ch22-p005 -->
But even apart from these relative differences in the value of money in different countries, it will often be found that the daily or weekly, etc., wage in the first nation is higher than in the second, while the relative price of labor, that is, the price of labor compared both with surplus-value and with the value of the product, stands higher in the second than in the first.[^3]

[^3]: In his polemic against Adam Smith, James Anderson remarks: “It deserves, likewise, to be remarked, that although the apparent price of Labour is usually lower in poor countries, where the produce of the soil, and grain in general, is cheap; yet it is in fact for the most part really higher than in other countries. For it is not the wages that is given to the labourer per day that constitutes the real price of labour, although it is its apparent price. The real price is that which a certain quantity of work performed actually costs the employer; and considered in this light, labour is in almost all cases cheaper in rich countries than in those that are poorer, although the price of grain and other provisions is usually much lower in the last than in the first.... Labour estimated by the day is much lower in Scotland than in England.... Labour by the piece is generally cheaper in England.” (James Anderson, “Observations on the Means of Exciting a Spirit of National Industry,” etc., Edin. 1777, pp. 350, 351.) On the contrary, low wages in their turn make labor dear. “Labour being dearer in Ireland than it is in England ... because the wages are so much lower.” (N. 2079 in “Royal Commission on Railways, Minutes,” 1867.)

J. W. Cowell, a member of the Factory Commission of 1833, made a careful investigation of the spinning trade and came to the conclusion that

<!-- ch22-p007 -->
> “in England wages are virtually lower to the capitalist, though higher to the operative than on the Continent of Europe.”[^4]

[^4]: (Ure, op. cit., p. 314.)

<!-- ch22-p008 -->
The English Factory Inspector Alexander Redgrave, in his report of Oct. 31st, 1866, uses comparative statistics on continental states to prove that, in spite of lower wages and much longer working time, continental labor is dearer than English labor in proportion to the product. An English manager of a cotton factory in Oldenburg declares that the working time there lasted from 5:30 a.m. to 8 p.m., Saturdays included. He also declares that the workers there, when under English overseers, did not supply quite as much product in this time as the English did in 10 hours, and under German overseers much less. Wages are much lower than in England, in many cases 50%, but the number of workers in proportion to the machinery was much greater, in certain departments in the proportion of 5:3.

<!-- ch22-p009 -->
Mr. Redgrave gives very full details on the Russian cotton factories. The data were given to him by an English manager who had until recently been employed there. On this Russian soil, so fruitful of every infamy, the old horrors of the early days of English factories are in full swing. The managers are, of course, English, since the native Russian capitalist is of no use in the factory business. Despite all the overwork, continued day and night, and despite the most shameful underpayment of the workers, Russian manufacture manages to vegetate only because foreign competition is prohibited.

“This comparison,” says Mr. Redgrave, “is yet more unfavorable to Great Britain, inasmuch as there is so large a number of factories in which weaving by power is carried on in conjunction with spinning” (while in the table the weavers are not subtracted), “and the factories abroad are chiefly spinning factories; if it were possible to compare like with like, strictly, I could find many cotton spinning factories in my district in which mules containing 2,200 spindles are minded [tended] by one man (the minder) and two assistants only, turning off [producing] daily 220 lbs. of yarn, measuring 400 miles in length.”[^5]

It is well known that English companies have undertaken the building of railways in Eastern Europe as well as in Asia, and that in building them they have employed a certain number of English workers side by side with the native workers. Practical necessity has thus forced them to take into account the national difference in the intensity of labor, but this has brought them no loss. Their experience shows that even if the level of wages corresponds more or less to the average intensity of labor, the relative price of labor generally moves in the opposite direction.

<!-- ch22-p016 -->
In an “Essay on the Rate of Wages,”[^6] one of his first economic writings, H. Carey tries to prove that the wages of the different nations are directly proportional to the degree of productivity of the national working days. His aim is to draw from this international relation the conclusion that wages everywhere rise and fall in proportion to the productivity of labor. Our whole analysis of the production of surplus-value shows that this conclusion is absurd. It would be absurd even if Carey himself had proved his premises, instead of shuffling a confused mass of statistical material back and forth in his usual uncritical and superficial fashion. The best of it is that he does not claim that things actually are as they ought to be according to his theory. For, he says, State intervention has falsified the natural economic relations. Therefore, according to him, the different national wages must be reckoned as if the part of each that goes to the State in the form of taxes came to the worker himself. Ought not Mr. Carey to consider further whether those “State expenses” are not the “natural” fruits of capitalist development? The reasoning is quite worthy of the man who first declared the relations of capitalist production to be eternal laws of nature and reason, whose free, harmonious working is disturbed only by the intervention of the State, and who did so in order to discover afterwards that the diabolical influence of England on the world market (an influence which, it appears, does not spring from the natural laws of capitalist production) makes State intervention necessary, that is, the protection of those laws of nature and reason by the State, otherwise known as the System of Protection (protective tariffs). He discovered further that the theorems of Ricardo and others, in which existing social antagonisms and contradictions are formulated, are not the ideal product (the product in the realm of ideas) of the real economic movement. On the contrary, he discovered that the real antagonisms of capitalist production in England and elsewhere are the result of the theories of Ricardo and others! Finally he discovered that it is, in the last resort, trade that destroys the inborn beauties and harmonies of the capitalist mode of production. One step further and he will, perhaps, discover that the one evil in capitalist production is capital itself. Only a man with such an atrocious lack of the critical faculty and such fake learning deserved, in spite of his Protectionist heresy, to become the secret source of the harmonious wisdom of a Bastiat, and of all the other Free-trade optimists of today.
