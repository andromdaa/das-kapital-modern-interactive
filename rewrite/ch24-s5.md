### Section 5: The So-Called Labor Fund

<!-- ch24-p103 -->
We have shown in the course of this inquiry that capital is not a fixed magnitude. It is a part of social wealth that is elastic and constantly fluctuates with the division of fresh surplus-value into revenue and additional capital. We have also seen that, even when the magnitude of the functioning capital is given, the labor-power, the science, and the land (by which, in economic terms, we are to understand all the conditions of labor that Nature supplies independently of man) embodied in it form elastic powers of capital. Within certain limits, these allow capital a field of action independent of its own magnitude. In this inquiry we have left aside all effects of the process of circulation, effects which may give the same mass of capital very different degrees of efficiency. And since we assumed the limits set by capitalist production, that is, assumed the process of social production in a form that has developed by purely spontaneous growth, we left aside any more rational combination that could be carried out directly and systematically with the means of production and the mass of labor-power now available. Classical economics always loved to think of social capital as a fixed magnitude with a fixed degree of efficiency. But it was the arch-Philistine Jeremy Bentham, that dull, pedantic, leather-tongued oracle of the ordinary bourgeois intelligence of the 19th century, who first set up this prejudice as a dogma.[^49] Bentham is among philosophers what Martin Tupper (author of the popular verse book *Proverbial Philosophy*) is among poets. Both could only have been manufactured in England.[^50] In the light of his dogma, the commonest phenomena of the process of production, for example its sudden expansions and contractions, and indeed even accumulation itself, become completely inconceivable.[^51] Bentham himself, as well as Malthus, James Mill, MacCulloch, etc., used the dogma for an apologetic purpose, that is, in defense of the system. Above all they used it to represent one part of capital as a fixed magnitude, namely variable capital, the part that can be converted into labor-power. The material of variable capital, that is, the mass of means of subsistence it represents for the worker, or the so-called labor fund, was presented in a fable as a separate part of social wealth, fixed by natural laws and unchangeable. To set in motion the part of social wealth that is to function as constant capital, or, to put it in material form, as means of production, a definite mass of living labor is required. This mass is given by technology. But the number of workers required to set this mass of labor-power flowing is not given (it changes with the degree of exploitation of the individual labor-power). Nor is the price of this labor-power given. Only its minimum limit is given, and even that is very variable. The facts that lie at the bottom of this dogma are these. On the one hand, the worker has no right to interfere in the division of social wealth into means of enjoyment for the non-worker and means of production.[^52] On the other hand, only in favorable and exceptional cases does the worker have the power to enlarge the so-called labor fund at the expense of the “revenue” of the wealthy.

[^49]: Compare, among others, Jeremy Bentham: “Théorie des Peines et des Récompenses,” traduct. d’Et. Dumont, 3ème édit. Paris, 1826, t. II, L. IV., ch. II.

[^50]: Bentham is a purely English phenomenon. Not even excepting our own philosopher, Christian Wolff, never in any age or any country has the most homespun commonplace strutted about so self-satisfied. The principle of utility was not Bentham's discovery. He simply reproduced, in his dull way, what Helvétius and other Frenchmen had said with wit in the 18th century. To know what is useful for a dog, one must study dog-nature. This nature itself cannot be deduced from the principle of utility. Applying this to man: whoever wants to criticize all human acts, movements, relations, etc., by the principle of utility must first deal with human nature in general, and then with human nature as modified in each historical epoch. Bentham makes short work of it. With the driest naïveté he takes the modern shopkeeper, especially the English shopkeeper, as the normal man. Whatever is useful to this odd normal man, and to his world, is absolutely useful. This yard-measure, then, he applies to past, present, and future. The Christian religion, for example, is “useful,” “because it forbids in the name of religion the same faults that the penal code condemns in the name of the law.” Artistic criticism is “harmful,” because it disturbs worthy people in their enjoyment of Martin Tupper, etc. With such rubbish the brave fellow, whose motto was “nuila dies sine line!” [No day without a line!], has piled up mountains of books. If I had the courage of my friend Heinrich Heine, I would call Mr. Jeremy a genius in the way of bourgeois stupidity.

[^51]: “Political economists are too apt to consider a certain quantity of capital and a certain number of labourers as productive instruments of uniform power, or operating with a certain uniform intensity.... Those... who maintain ... that commodities are the sole agents of production ... prove that production could never be enlarged, for it requires as an indispensable condition to such an enlargement that food, raw materials, and tools should be previously augmented; which is in fact maintaining that no increase of production can take place without a previous increase, or, in other words, that an increase is impossible.” (S. Bailey: “Money and its Vicissitudes,” pp. 58 and 70.) Bailey criticizes the dogma mainly from the point of view of the process of circulation.

[^52]: John Stuart Mill, in his “Principles of Political Economy,” says: “The really exhausting and the really repulsive labours instead of being better paid than others, are almost invariably paid the worst of all.... The more revolting the occupation, the more certain it is to receive the minimum of remuneration.... The hardships and the earnings, instead of being directly proportional, as in any just arrangements of society they would be, are generally in an inverse ratio to one another.” To avoid misunderstanding, let me say this: although men like John Stuart Mill are to blame for the contradiction between their traditional economic dogmas and their modern tendencies, it would be very wrong to class them with the herd of vulgar economic apologists.

What silly tautology results from trying to present the capitalist limits of the labor fund as its natural and social limits can be seen, for example, in Professor Fawcett.[^53]

[^53]: H. Fawcett, Professor of Political Economy at Cambridge. “The Economic position of the British labourer.” London, 1865, p. 120.

<!-- ch24-p105 -->
> “The circulating capital of a country,” he says, “is its wage-fund. Hence, if we desire to calculate the average money wages received by each labourer, we have simply to divide the amount of this capital by the number of the labouring population.”[^54]

[^54]: I must remind the reader here that I was the first to use the categories “variable and constant capital.” Since the time of Adam Smith, political economy has confusedly mixed up the essential distinctions involved in these categories with the merely formal differences between fixed and circulating capital, which arise out of the process of circulation. For further details on this point, see Book II., Part II.

<!-- ch24-p106 -->
That is to say, we first add together the individual wages actually paid. Then we declare that the sum we get forms the total value of the “labor fund” fixed and granted to us by God and Nature. Lastly, we divide the sum thus obtained by the number of workers, to find out again how much may come to each on average. An uncommonly clever dodge, this. It did not stop Mr. Fawcett from saying in the same breath:

<!-- ch24-p107 -->
“The aggregate wealth which is annually saved in England, is divided into two portions; one portion is employed as capital to maintain our industry, and the other portion is exported to foreign countries... Only a portion, and perhaps, not a large portion of the wealth which is annually saved in this country, is invested in our own industry.[^55]

[^55]: Fawcett, cited above, pp. 122, 123.

<!-- ch24-p108 -->
The greater part of the surplus-product that accrues each year is embezzled from the English worker, because it is taken from him without any equivalent being returned. This greater part is thus used as capital, not in England, but in foreign countries. But along with the additional capital exported in this way, a part of the “labor fund” invented by God and Bentham is also exported.[^56]

[^56]: It might be said that not only capital, but also workers, in the form of emigrants, are exported from England every year. In the text, however, there is no question of the *peculium* [the small property a slave was allowed to keep] of the emigrants, a great part of whom are not workers. Farmers' sons make up a great part of them. The additional capital transported abroad each year to be put out at interest is in much greater proportion to the annual accumulation than the yearly emigration is to the yearly increase of population.
