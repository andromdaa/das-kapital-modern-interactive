### Section 2: The Value Transferred by Machinery to the Product

<!-- ch15-p025 -->
We saw that the productive forces that come from cooperation and division of labor cost capital nothing. They are natural forces of social labor. In the same way, physical forces like steam, water, etc., cost nothing when they are taken over for productive processes. But just as a man needs lungs to breathe with, he needs something made by human hands in order to consume physical forces productively. A water-wheel is needed to exploit the force of water, and a steam-engine to exploit the elasticity of steam. Once discovered, the law of the deviation of the magnetic needle in the field of an electric current, or the law of the magnetization of iron around which an electric current circulates, never costs a penny.[^23] But exploiting these laws for telegraphy, etc., requires a costly and extensive apparatus. The tool, as we have seen, is not wiped out by the machine. From being a dwarf implement of the human organism, it expands and multiplies into the implement of a mechanism created by man. Capital now sets the worker to work, not with a hand tool, but with a machine that itself handles the tools. So it is clear at first glance that modern industry, by bringing both enormous physical forces and the natural sciences into the process of production, raises the productivity of labor to an extraordinary degree. But it is by no means equally clear that this increased productive force is not, on the other hand, bought with an increased expenditure of labor. Machinery, like every other component of constant capital (the part of capital spent on means of production; its value is passed on to the product but does not grow), creates no new value, but gives up its own value to the product that it helps to produce. Insofar as the machine has value, and so passes value on to the product, it forms an element in the value of that product. Instead of being made cheaper, the product is made dearer in proportion to the value of the machine. And it is as clear as noonday that machines and systems of machinery, the characteristic instruments of labor of modern industry, are incomparably more loaded with value than the implements used in handicrafts and in manufacture.

[^23]: Science, generally speaking, costs the capitalist nothing, a fact that by no means stops him from exploiting it. Capital annexes the science of others just as it annexes the labor of others. However, capitalist appropriation and personal appropriation, whether of science or of material wealth, are totally different things. Dr. Ure himself laments the gross ignorance of mechanical science among his dear machinery-exploiting manufacturers, and Liebig has a tale to tell about the astounding ignorance of chemistry shown by English chemical manufacturers.

<!-- ch15-p026 -->
In the first place, we must note that the machinery always enters the labor process as a whole, but enters the value-creating process only bit by bit. It never adds more value than it loses, on average, by wear and tear. So there is a great difference between the value of a machine and the value that machine transfers to the product in a given time. The longer the machine's life in the labor process, the greater that difference. It is true, no doubt, as we have already seen, that every instrument of labor enters the labor process as a whole, and enters the value-creating process only piecemeal, in proportion to its average daily loss by wear and tear. But this difference between the instrument as a whole and its daily wear and tear is much greater in a machine than in a tool. There are three reasons. First, the machine is made of more durable material, so it lasts longer. Second, its use is regulated by strictly scientific laws, which allows greater economy in the wear and tear of its parts and in the materials it consumes. And lastly, its field of production is incomparably larger than that of a tool. Once we allow, for both the machine and the tool, for their average daily cost, that is, for the value they pass on to the product through their average daily wear and tear, and also for their consumption of auxiliary substances such as oil, coal, and so on, each of them does its work free of charge, just like the forces that Nature supplies without the help of man. The greater the productive power of the machinery compared with that of the tool, the greater the extent of its free service compared with that of the tool. In modern industry, human beings succeeded for the first time in making the product of their past labor work on a large scale free of charge, like the forces of Nature.[^24]

[^24]: Ricardo puts such stress on this effect of machinery that he sometimes loses sight of the value that machines give up to the product, and puts machines on the same footing as natural forces. (In other connections he takes no more notice of this effect than he does of the general distinction between the labor process and the process of creating surplus-value.) Thus: “Adam Smith nowhere undervalues the services which the natural agents and machinery perform for us, but he very justly distinguishes the nature of the value which they add to commodities... as they perform their work gratuitously, the assistance which they afford us, adds nothing to value in exchange.” (Ric., cited above, pp. 336, 337.) This remark of Ricardo's is of course correct insofar as it is aimed at J. B. Say, who imagines that machines render the “service” of creating value, which forms a part of “profits.”

<!-- ch15-p027 -->
When we discussed cooperation and manufacture, we showed that certain general factors of production, such as buildings, are economized by being consumed in common, compared with the scattered means of production of the isolated worker, and that they therefore make the product cheaper. In a system of machinery, not only is the framework of the machine consumed in common by its many operating implements, but the prime mover, together with part of the transmitting mechanism, is consumed in common by the many working machines.

<!-- ch15-p028 -->
Take as given the difference between the value of the machinery and the value it transfers to the product in a day. Then how far this transferred value makes the product dearer depends, in the first place, on the size of the product, on its area, so to speak. Mr. Baynes, of Blackburn, in a lecture published in 1858, estimates that

<!-- ch15-p029 -->
> “each real mechanical horse-power[^25] will drive 450 self-acting mule spindles, with preparation, or 200 throstle spindles, or 15 looms for 40 inch cloth with the appliances for warping, sizing, &c.”

[^25]: A horse-power equals a force of 33,000 foot-pounds per minute, that is, a force that raises 33,000 pounds one foot in a minute, or one pound 33,000 feet. This is the horse-power meant in the text. In ordinary language, and also here and there in quotations in this work, a distinction is drawn between the “nominal” and the “commercial” or “indicated” horse-power of the same engine. The old or nominal horse-power is calculated only from the length of the piston-stroke and the diameter of the cylinder, and leaves steam pressure and piston speed out of account. In practice it says this: This engine would be one of 50 horse-power if it were driven with the same low steam pressure and the same slow piston speed as in the days of Boulton and Watt. But these two factors have increased enormously since those days. To measure the mechanical force an engine exerts today, an indicator has been invented which shows the pressure of the steam in the cylinder. The piston speed is easy to find out. So the “indicated” or “commercial” horse-power of an engine is expressed by a mathematical formula that takes in the diameter of the cylinder, the length of the stroke, the piston speed, and the steam pressure all at once, and shows what multiple of 33,000 pounds the engine really raises in a minute. Hence one “nominal” horse-power may exert three, four, or even five “indicated” or “real” horse-powers. This remark is made to explain various quotations in the following pages. — *F. E*.

<!-- ch15-p030 -->
In the first case, the daily cost of one horse-power, and the wear and tear of the machinery that power sets in motion, are spread over the day's output of 450 mule spindles; in the second, of 200 throstle spindles; in the third, of 15 power-looms. So only a very tiny value is transferred by such wear and tear to a pound of yarn or a yard of cloth. The same is true of the steam-hammer mentioned above. Its daily wear and tear, its coal consumption, etc., are spread over the enormous masses of iron it hammers in a day, so only a small value is added to a hundred weight of iron. But that value would be very great if this giant instrument were used for driving in nails.

<!-- ch15-p031 -->
Take as given a machine's capacity for work, that is, the number of its operating tools, or, where force is in question, their mass. Then the amount of its product will depend on the speed of its working parts: for instance, on the speed of the spindles, or on the number of blows the hammer gives in a minute. Many of these colossal hammers strike seventy times a minute, and Ryder's patent machine for forging spindles with small hammers gives as many as 700 strokes per minute.

<!-- ch15-p032 -->
Take as given the rate at which machinery transfers its value to the product. Then the amount of value so transferred depends on the total value of the machinery.[^26] The less labor it contains, the less value it passes on to the product. The less value it gives up, the more productive it is, and the more its services come close to those of natural forces. But the production of machinery by machinery lowers its value in relation to its extent and its effectiveness.

[^26]: The reader steeped in capitalist notions will naturally miss here the “interest” that the machine adds to the product in proportion to its capital value. But it is easy to see that a machine no more creates new value than any other part of constant capital does, so it cannot add any value under the name of “interest.” It is also clear that here, where we are dealing with the production of surplus-value, we cannot assume in advance that any part of that value exists under the name of interest. The capitalist way of calculating, which at first sight seems absurd and contrary to the laws of the creation of value, will be explained in the third book of this work.

<!-- ch15-p033 -->
If we analyze and compare the prices (a price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity) of commodities produced by handicrafts or manufacture with the prices of the same commodities produced by machinery, this generally shows that in the product of machinery, the value due to the instruments of labor increases relatively but decreases absolutely. In other words, its absolute amount decreases, but its amount relative to the total value of the product, of a pound of yarn, for instance, increases.[^27]

[^27]: This portion of value added by the machinery decreases both absolutely and relatively when the machinery does away with horses and other animals that are used as mere moving forces, and not as machines for changing the form of matter. We may remark here in passing that Descartes, in defining animals as mere machines, saw with the eyes of the manufacturing period, while to the eyes of the middle ages animals were man's assistants, as they were later to Von Haller in his “Restauration der Staatswissenschaften.” Descartes, like Bacon, expected that the altered methods of thought would bring about a change in the form of production and the practical mastery of Nature by Man. His “Discours de la Méthode” makes this plain. He says there: “Il est possible (by the methods he introduced in philosophy) de parvenir à des connaissances fort utiles à la vie, et qu’au lieu de cette philosophie spéculative qu’on enseigne dans les écoles, on en peut trouver une pratique, par laquelle, connaissant la force et les actions du feu, de l’eau, de l’air, des astres, et de tous les autres corps qui nous environnent, aussi distinctement que nous connaissons les divers métiers de nos artisans, nous les pourrions employer en même façon à tous les usages auxquels ils sont propres, et ainsi nous rendre comme maîtres et possesseurs de la nature” and thus “contribuer au perfectionnement de la vie humaine.” [It is possible to arrive at knowledge that is very useful in life. In place of the speculative philosophy taught in the schools, we can find a practical one. Through it, knowing the force and the actions of fire, water, air, the stars, and all the other bodies around us as clearly as we know the various trades of our craftsmen, we could use them in the same way for all the purposes they are suited to, and so make ourselves, as it were, masters and owners of nature; and thus “contribute to the perfecting of human life.”] The preface to Sir Dudley North's “Discourses upon Trade” (1691) states that Descartes' method had begun to free political economy from the old fables and superstitious notions about gold, trade, etc. On the whole, however, the early English economists sided with Bacon and Hobbes as their philosophers, while at a later period the philosopher [...] of political economy in England, France, and Italy was Locke.

Clearly, whenever it costs as much labor to produce a machine as is saved by using that machine, there is nothing but a shifting of labor from one place to another. So the total labor needed to produce a commodity is not reduced, and the productivity of labor is not increased. It is clear, however, that the difference between the labor a machine costs and the labor it saves, in other words, the degree of its productivity, does not depend on the difference between its own value and the value of the implement it replaces. As long as the labor spent on a machine, and so the portion of its value added to the product, stays smaller than the value the worker adds to the product with his tool, there is always a difference of labor saved in favor of the machine. The productivity of a machine is therefore measured by the human labor-power it replaces. According to Mr. Baynes, 2 factory workers are needed for the 450 mule spindles, including the preparation machinery,[^28] that are driven by one-horse power. Each self-acting mule spindle, working ten hours, produces 13 ounces of yarn (average number of thickness). So 2½ factory workers spin 365 5/8 lbs. of yarn a week. Hence, leaving waste aside, 366 lbs. of cotton absorb only 150 hours' labor, or fifteen days' labor of ten hours each, while being turned into yarn. But with a spinning-wheel, supposing the hand-spinner produces thirteen ounces of yarn in sixty hours, the same weight of cotton would absorb 2,700 days' labor of ten hours each, or 27,000 hours' labor.[^29] Where block-printing, the old method of printing calico by hand, has been replaced by machine printing, a single machine, with the help of one man or boy, prints as much four-color calico in one hour as it used to take 200 men to print.[^30] Before Eli Whitney invented the cotton gin in 1793, separating the seed from a pound of cotton cost an average day's labor. With his invention, one Black woman was able to clean 100 lbs. a day, and since then the effectiveness of the gin has increased considerably. A pound of cotton wool, which had previously cost 50 cents to produce, contained more unpaid labor after that invention, and so was sold at 10 cents with greater profit. In India they use an instrument, half machine, half tool, called a churka, for separating the wool from the seed. With it, one man and a woman can clean 28 lbs. a day. With the churka invented some years ago by Dr. Forbes, one man and a boy produce 250 lbs. a day. If oxen, steam, or water are used to drive it, only a few boys and girls are needed as feeders. Sixteen of these machines driven by oxen do as much work in a day as 750 people used to do on average.[^31]

[^28]: According to the annual report (1863) of the Essen chamber of commerce, the Krupp cast-steel works produced thirteen million pounds of cast steel in 1862. The works had 161 furnaces, thirty-two steam-engines (in the year 1800 this was about the number of all the steam-engines working in Manchester), and fourteen steam-hammers (representing in all 1,236 horse-power), forty-nine forges, 203 tool-machines, and about 2,400 workers. Here there are not two workers to each horse-power.

[^29]: Babbage estimates that in Java the spinning labor alone adds 117% to the value of the cotton. At the same period (1832), the total value added to the cotton by machinery and labor in the fine-spinning industry came to about 33% of the value of the cotton. (“On the Economy of Machinery,” pp. 165, 166.)

[^30]: Machine printing also saves color.

[^31]: See the paper read by Dr. Watson, Reporter on Products to the Government of India, before the Society of Arts, 17th April, 1860.

As already stated, a steam-plough does as much work in one hour, at a cost of three-pence, as 66 men at a cost of 15 shillings. I come back to this example to clear up a mistaken notion. The 15 shillings are by no means the expression in money of all the labor the 66 men spend in one hour. Suppose the ratio of surplus labor (the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist) to necessary labor (the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages) were 100%. Then these 66 men would produce a value of 30 shillings in one hour, although their wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself), 15 shillings, represent only their labor for half an hour. Suppose, then, that a machine costs as much as a year's wages of the 150 men it displaces, say £3,000. This £3,000 is by no means the expression in money of the labor these 150 men added to the object produced before the machine was introduced. It expresses only the portion of their year's labor that they spent for themselves, which their wages represented. On the other hand, the £3,000, the money-value of the machine, expresses all the labor spent on producing it, no matter in what proportion this labor forms wages for the worker and surplus-value for the capitalist. Therefore, even if a machine costs as much as the labor-power it displaces, the labor materialized in it is even then much less than the living labor (the labor of workers now at work) it replaces.[^32]

[^32]: “These mute agents (machines) are always the produce of much less labour than that which they displace, even when they are of the same money-value.” (Ricardo, cited above, p. 40.)

Using machinery for the sole purpose of making the product cheaper is limited in this way: less labor must be spent in producing the machinery than is displaced by using it. For the capitalist, however, this use is still more limited. He does not pay for the labor; he pays only the value of the labor-power employed (the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work). So the limit to his using a machine is fixed by the difference between the value of the machine and the value of the labor-power it replaces. The division of the day's work into necessary and surplus labor differs in different countries, and even in the same country at different periods, or in different branches of industry. Further, the worker's actual wage sometimes sinks below the value of his labor-power and sometimes rises above it. So the difference between the price of the machinery and the price of the labor-power it replaces may vary a great deal, even if the difference between the quantity of labor needed to produce the machine and the total quantity of labor it replaces stays constant.[^33] But it is the first difference alone that determines what it costs the capitalist to produce a commodity, and, through the pressure of competition, influences what he does. Hence machines are invented in England nowadays that are used only in North America; just as in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries machines were invented in Germany to be used only in Holland, and just as many a French invention of the eighteenth century was exploited in England alone. In the older countries, when machinery is used in some branches of industry, it creates such an excess of labor in other branches that in these other branches wages fall below the value of labor-power. This fall hinders the use of machinery there. From the standpoint of the capitalist, whose profit comes not from reducing the labor employed but from reducing the labor paid for, it makes that use unnecessary and often impossible. In some branches of the English woolen manufacture, the employment of children has been considerably reduced in recent years, and in some cases entirely abolished. Why? Because the Factory Acts made two sets of children necessary, one working six hours and the other four, or each working five hours. But the parents refused to sell the “half-timers” cheaper than the “full-timers.” Hence the replacement of the “half-timers” by machinery.[^34] Before the labor of women and of children under 10 years of age was forbidden in mines, capitalists considered that employing naked women and girls, often together with men, was so far sanctioned by their moral code, and especially by their ledgers, that they turned to machinery only after the Act was passed. The Yankees have invented a stone-breaking machine. The English do not use it, because the “wretch”[^35] who does this work gets paid for such a small portion of his labor that machinery would increase the capitalist's cost of production.[^36] In England women are still sometimes used instead of horses for hauling canal boats,[^37] because the labor needed to produce horses and machines is an accurately known quantity, while the labor needed to maintain the women of the surplus population (the workers made “surplus” to capital's needs for the time being, who serve as a reserve of available labor and press down on the employed) is below all calculation. Hence nowhere do we find a more shameful squandering of human labor-power for the most despicable purposes than in England, the land of machinery.

[^33]: Hence in a communist society there would be a very different scope for using machinery than there can be in a bourgeois society (one shaped by the capitalist class).

They have introduced improved and new machinery of various kinds, which altogether supersedes the employment of children (i.e., under 13 years); f. i.
