## Chapter 20: Time-Wages

<!-- ch20-p001 -->
Wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself) in turn take many forms. This fact cannot be seen in the ordinary economic treatises, which care only about the material side of the question and ignore every difference of form. However, an account of all these forms belongs to the special study of wage labor, and therefore not to this work. Still, the two basic forms must be briefly worked out here.

<!-- ch20-p002 -->
As the reader will remember, labor-power (a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything; it is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist) is sold for a definite period of time. Hence the converted form in which the daily, weekly, etc., value of labor-power presents itself is that of time wages, and so of day wages, etc. (Here value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work.)

<!-- ch20-p003 -->
Next we should note that the laws set out in the 17th chapter, on the changes in the relative magnitudes of the price of labor-power and of surplus-value, turn into laws of wages by a simple change of form. Here price means the money name of the value (the labor) embodied in a commodity, and surplus-value means the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. In the same way, the distinction between the exchange-value of labor-power (the proportion in which it, like any commodity, trades for others) and the sum of the necessaries of life into which this value is converted now reappears as the distinction between nominal and real wages. It would be useless to repeat here, for the form of appearance, what has already been worked out for the substance underlying it. So we limit ourselves to a few points that are characteristic of time wages.

<!-- ch20-p004 -->
The sum of money[^1] that the worker receives for his daily or weekly labor makes up the amount of his nominal wages, or of his wages estimated in value. But clearly, depending on the length of the working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor), that is, depending on the amount of actual labor supplied each day, the same daily or weekly wage may represent very different prices of labor, that is, very different sums of money for the same quantity of labor.[^2] So in considering time wages we must again distinguish between the total of the daily or weekly wages, etc., and the price of labor. How, then, are we to find this price, that is, the money-value of a given quantity of labor? The average price of labor is found by dividing the average daily value of labor-power by the average number of hours in the working day. If, for example, the daily value of labor-power is 3 shillings, the value of the product of 6 working hours, and if the working day is 12 hours, then the price of 1 working hour is 3/12 shillings = 3d. The price of the working hour found in this way serves as the unit of measure for the price of labor.

[^1]: The value of money itself is always assumed here to be constant.

[^2]: “The price of labour is the sum paid for a given quantity of labour.” (Sir Edward West, “Price of Corn and Wages of Labour,” London, 1836, p. 67.) West is the author of the anonymous “Essay on the Application of Capital to Land.” by a Fellow of the University College of Oxford, London, 1815. It is an epoch-making work in the history of political economy (the science of economics as it was then called).

<!-- ch20-p005 -->
It follows, therefore, that the daily and weekly wages, etc., may stay the same although the price of labor falls constantly. If, for example, the usual working day is 10 hours and the daily value of labor-power is 3s., the price of the working hour is 3 3/5d. It falls to 3d. as soon as the working day rises to 12 hours, and to 2 2/5d as soon as it rises to 15 hours. Despite all this, daily or weekly wages remain unchanged. Conversely, the daily or weekly wages may rise although the price of labor stays constant or even falls. If, for example, the working day is 10 hours and the daily value of labor-power is 3 shillings, the price of one working hour is 3 3/5d. If, because trade has increased, the worker works 12 hours, with the price of labor staying the same, his daily wage now rises to 3 shillings 7 1/5 d. without any change in the price of labor. The same result might follow if it were the intensive amount of labor that increased, instead of its extensive amount.[^3] So a rise in the nominal daily or weekly wages may go along with a price of labor that stays put or falls. The same holds for the income of the worker's family, as soon as the quantity of labor spent by the head of the family is increased by the labor of the members of his family. There are, therefore, ways of lowering the price of labor that do not depend on reducing the nominal daily or weekly wages.[^4]

[^3]: “The wages of labour depend upon the price of labour and the quantity of labour performed.... An increase in the wages of labour does not necessarily imply an enhancement of the price of labour. From fuller employment, and greater exertions, the wages of labour may be considerably increased, while the price of labour may continue the same.” (West, op. cit., pp. 67, 68, 112.) West, however, brushes off the main question, “How is the price of labour determined?”, with mere commonplaces.

[^4]: This is seen by the author of the “Essay on Trade and Commerce”, whom we have often quoted, the fanatical spokesman of the industrial bourgeoisie (the capitalist class) of the 18th century, although he puts the matter in a confused way: “It is the quantity of labour and not the price of it” (he means by this the nominal daily or weekly wages) “that is determined by the price of provisions and other necessaries: reduce the price of necessaries very low, and of course you reduce the quantity of labour in proportion. Master manufacturers know that there are various ways of raising and felling the price of labour, besides that of altering its nominal amount.” (op. cit., pp. 48, 61.) In his “Three Lectures on the Rate of Wages,” London, 1830, in which N. W. Senior makes use of West's work without naming it, Senior says: “The labourer is principally interested in the amount of wages” (p. 14). That is to say, the worker is principally interested in what he receives, the nominal sum of his wages, not in what he gives, the amount of labor!

<!-- ch20-p006 -->
As a general law it follows that, if the amount of daily or weekly labor, etc., is given, the daily or weekly wages depend on the price of labor. That price itself varies either with the value of labor-power or with the gap between its price and its value. If, on the other hand, the price of labor is given, the daily or weekly wages depend on the quantity of daily or weekly labor.

<!-- ch20-p007 -->
The unit of measure for time wages, the price of the working hour, is the value of a day's labor-power divided by the number of hours in the average working day. Let the average working day be 12 hours, and the daily value of labor-power 3 shillings, the value of the product of 6 hours of labor. Under these conditions the price of a working hour is 3d., and the value produced in it is 6d. If the worker is now employed for less than 12 hours (or less than 6 days in the week), for example only 6 or 8 hours, then at this price of labor he receives only 2s. or 1s. 6d. a day.[^5] On our assumption he must work 6 hours a day on average just to produce a day's wage that matches the value of his labor-power. On the same assumption he works only half of every hour for himself and half for the capitalist. So clearly he cannot get for himself the value of the product of 6 hours if he is employed for less than 12 hours. In earlier chapters we saw the destructive results of overwork. Here we find the sources of the suffering that comes to the worker from not being employed enough.

[^5]: The effect of such an abnormal cut in employment is quite different from that of a general shortening of the working day enforced by law. The former has nothing to do with the absolute length of the working day, and can occur just as well in a working day of 15 hours as in one of 6 hours. In the first case the normal price of labor is calculated on the worker working 15 hours a day on average, in the second on his working 6 hours a day on average. So the result is the same if in the one case he is employed for only 7½ hours, and in the other for only 3 hours.

Suppose the hourly wage is fixed in such a way that the capitalist does not bind himself to pay a day's or a week's wage, but only to pay wages for the hours during which he chooses to employ the worker. Then he can employ him for less time than the time originally used as the basis for calculating the hourly wage, the unit of measure of the price of labor. Since this unit is fixed by the ratio

it naturally loses all meaning as soon as the working day stops containing a definite number of hours. The connection between paid and unpaid labor is destroyed. The capitalist can now squeeze a certain quantity of surplus labor (the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist) out of the worker without allowing him the labor-time necessary for his own subsistence. He can wipe out all regularity of employment. To suit his own convenience, whims, and interest of the moment, he can make the most enormous overwork alternate with a partial or total stop of work. On the pretense of paying “the normal price of labour,” he can lengthen the working day abnormally without any matching compensation to the worker. Hence the perfectly rational revolt in 1860 of the London workers in the building trades against the capitalists' attempt to impose this sort of hourly wage on them. The legal limitation of the working day puts an end to such mischief. It does not, of course, put an end to the reduced employment caused by the competition of machinery, by changes in the quality of the workers employed, and by crises, partial or general.

the denominator increases, the numerator increases still faster. The value of labor-power, as it depends on its wear and tear, increases with the length of time that labor-power is at work, and it increases faster than that time does. In many branches of industry where time wages are the general rule and there are no legal limits to working time, a habit has therefore grown up on its own of treating the working day as normal only up to a certain point, for example up to the end of the tenth hour (“normal working day,” “the day's work,” “the regular hours of work”). Beyond this limit the working time is overtime, and, taking the hour as the unit of measure, it is paid better (“extra pay”), although often in a ridiculously small proportion.[^6] Here the normal working day exists as a fraction of the actual working day, and the actual working day, often throughout the whole year, lasts longer than the normal one.[^7] In various British industries, the increase in the price of labor when the working day is extended beyond a certain normal limit takes such a shape that the low price of labor during the so-called normal time forces the worker to work during the better-paid overtime, if he wants to earn an adequate wage at all.[^8] Legal limitation of the working day puts an end to these amenities.[^9]

It is a generally known fact that, in any branch of industry, the longer the working days, the lower the wages.[^10] A. Redgrave, factory inspector, illustrates this with a comparative review of the 20 years from 1839-1859. According to it, wages rose in the factories under the 10 Hours Law, while they fell in the factories where work lasted 14 to 15 hours a day.[^11]

[^10]: “It is a very notable thing, too, that where long hours are the rule, small wages are also so.” (“Report of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st. Oct., 1863, p. 9.) “The work which obtains the scanty pittance of food, is, for the most part, excessively prolonged.” (“Public Health, Sixth Report,” 1864, p. 15.)

[^11]: “Report of Inspectors of Fact.,” 30th April, 1860, pp. 31, 32.

From the law, “the price of labour being given, the daily or weekly wage depends on the quantity of labour expended,” it follows, first of all, that the lower the price of labor, the greater the quantity of labor must be, or the longer the working day must be, for the worker to secure even a miserable average wage. Here the low price of labor acts as a spur to the extension of labor-time.[^12]

[^12]: The hand nail-makers in England, for example, have to work 15 hours a day because of the low price of labor, in order to hammer out their miserable weekly wage. “It’s a great many hours in a day (6 a.m. to 8 p.m.), and he has to work hard all the time to get 11 d. or 1s., and there is the wear of the tools, the cost of firing, and something for waste iron to go out of this, which takes off altogether 2½d. or 3d.” (“Children’s Employment Com., III. Report,” p. 136, n. 671.) In the same working time the women earn a week's wage of only 5 shillings. (Cited above, p. 137, n. 674.)

On the other hand, the extension of working time in its turn produces a fall in the price of labor, and with it a fall in the day's or week's wages.

shows that merely lengthening the working day lowers the price of labor, if no compensation steps in. But the same circumstances that allow the capitalist to lengthen the working day in the long run also allow him at first, and force him in the end, to lower the price of labor nominally, until the total price of the increased number of hours falls, and therefore the daily or weekly wage too. It is enough here to point to two circumstances. If one man does the work of 1½ or 2 men, the supply of labor increases, although the supply of labor-power on the market stays constant. The competition this creates among the workers allows the capitalist to beat down the price of labor, while the falling price of labor, on the other hand, allows him to screw up working time still further.[^13] Soon, however, this command over abnormal quantities of unpaid labor, that is, quantities beyond the average social amount, becomes a source of competition among the capitalists themselves. Part of the price of the commodity (a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use) consists of the price of labor. The unpaid part of the labor-price need not be counted in the price of the commodity. It can be given away to the buyer. This is the first step competition leads to. The second step it drives to is to leave out of the selling price of the commodity at least part of the abnormal surplus-value created by lengthening the working day as well. In this way an abnormally low selling price of the commodity arises, at first here and there, and becomes fixed by degrees. From then on this lower selling price becomes the constant basis of a miserable wage for an excessive working time, just as it was originally the product of these very circumstances. This movement is only pointed out here, since the analysis of competition does not belong to this part of our subject. Nevertheless, the capitalist may, for a moment, speak for himself. “In Birmingham there is so much competition of masters one against another that many are obliged to do things as employers that they would otherwise be ashamed of; and yet no more money is made, but only the public gets the benefit.”[^14] The reader will remember the two sorts of London bakers. One sort sold bread at its full price (the “full-priced” bakers), the other below its normal price (“the under-priced,” “the undersellers”). The “full-priced” bakers denounced their rivals before the Parliamentary Committee of Inquiry: “They only exist now by first defrauding the public, and next getting 18 hours’ work out of their men for 12 hours’ wages.... The unpaid labour of the men was made ... the source whereby the competition was carried on, and continues so to this day.... The competition among the master bakers is the cause of the difficulty in getting rid of night-work. An underseller, who sells his bread below the cost-price according to the price of flour, must make it up by getting more out of the labour of the men.... If I got only 12 hours’ work out of my men, and my neighbor got 18 or 20, he must beat me in the selling price. If the men could insist on payment for over-work, this would be set right.... A large number of those employed by the undersellers are foreigners and youths, who are obliged to accept almost any wages they can obtain.”[^15]

This lament is also interesting because it shows how only the appearance of the relations of production is mirrored in the capitalist's brain. The capitalist does not know that the normal price of labor also includes a definite quantity of unpaid labor, and that this very unpaid labor is the normal source of his gain. The category of surplus labor-time does not exist for him at all, since it is included in the normal working day, which he thinks he has paid for in the day's wages. But overtime does exist for him: the lengthening of the working day beyond the limits that correspond to the usual price of labor. Face to face with his underselling competitor, he even insists on extra pay for this overtime. Again he does not know that this extra pay includes unpaid labor, just as the price of the customary hour of labor does. For example, the price of one hour of the 12 hours' working day is 3d., say the value-product (the new value created by labor in a given period, as distinct from the old value transferred from the means of production) of half a working hour, while the price of the overtime working hour is 4d., or the value-product of 2/3 of a working hour. In the first case the capitalist takes for himself one-half of the working hour without paying for it, in the second case one-third.
