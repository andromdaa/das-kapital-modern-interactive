### Section 2: Erroneous Conception, by Political Economy, of Reproduction on a Progressively Increasing Scale

<!-- ch24-p037 -->
Before we investigate accumulation further, that is, the turning of surplus-value back into capital, we must brush aside an ambiguity that the classical economists brought in. (Classical political economy means the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production.)

<!-- ch24-p038 -->
The commodities that the capitalist buys with a part of the surplus-value for his own consumption do not serve the purpose of production or of creating value. In just the same way, the labor that he buys to satisfy his natural and social needs is not productive labor. By buying those commodities and that labor, he does not convert the surplus-value into capital. On the contrary, he consumes it, or spends it, as revenue. The old feudal nobility had a habitual way of life which, as Hegel rightly says, “consists in consuming what is in hand,” and which showed itself above all in the luxury of personal attendants. Against this way of life it was extremely important for bourgeois economics to proclaim the doctrine that accumulation of capital is the first duty of every citizen. It had to preach without ceasing that a man cannot accumulate if he eats up all his revenue, instead of spending a good part of it on acquiring additional productive workers, who bring in more than they cost. On the other hand, the economists had to fight against the popular prejudice that confuses capitalist production with hoarding (building up a hoard: money withdrawn from circulation and kept as a store of value).[^12] This prejudice imagines that accumulated wealth is either wealth that is rescued from being destroyed in its existing form, that is, from being consumed, or wealth that is withdrawn from circulation. Shutting money out of circulation would also shut out completely its self-expansion as capital, while piling up a hoard in the form of commodities would be sheer tomfoolery.[^13] The accumulation of commodities in great masses is the result either of over-production or of a stoppage of circulation.[^14] It is true that the popular mind is impressed by two sights. On the one hand, there is the mass of goods stored up for gradual consumption by the rich.[^15] On the other hand, there is the building up of reserve stocks. The latter is a phenomenon common to all modes of production, and we shall dwell on it for a moment when we come to analyze circulation. Classical economics is therefore quite right when it maintains that the consumption of surplus-products by productive, instead of by unproductive, workers is a characteristic feature of the process of accumulation. But at this point the mistakes also begin. Adam Smith has made it the fashion to represent accumulation as nothing more than the consumption of surplus-products by productive workers. That amounts to saying that capitalizing surplus-value consists merely in turning surplus-value into labor-power.

[^12]: “No political economist of the present day can by saving mean mere hoarding: and beyond this contracted and insufficient proceeding, no use of the term in reference to the national wealth can well be imagined, but that which must arise from a different application of what is saved, founded upon a real distinction between the different kinds of labour maintained by it.” (Malthus, cited above, pp. 38, 39.)

[^13]: So, for example, Balzac, who studied every shade of greed so thoroughly, shows the old usurer Gobseck as being in his second childhood when he begins to heap up a hoard of commodities.

[^14]: “Accumulation of stocks ... non-exchange ... over-production.” (Th. Corbet, cited above, p. 104.)

[^15]: In this sense Necker speaks of the “objets de faste et de somptuosité,” [things of show and luxury] of which “le temps a grossi l’accummulation,” [time has swollen the accumulation] and which “les lois de propriété ont rassemblés dans une seule classe de la société.” [the laws of property have gathered together in a single class of society] (*Oeuvres de M. Necker*, Paris and Lausanne, 1789, t. ii., p. 291.)

Let us see what Ricardo, for example, says:

<!-- ch24-p040 -->
> “It must be understood that all the productions of a country are consumed; but it makes the greatest difference imaginable whether they are consumed by those who reproduce, or by those who do not reproduce another value. When we say that revenue is saved, and added to capital, what we mean is, that the portion of revenue, so said to be added to capital, is consumed by productive instead of unproductive labourers. There can be no greater error than in supposing that capital is increased by non-consumption.”[^16]

[^16]: Ricardo, cited above, p. 163, note.

<!-- ch24-p041 -->
There can be no greater error than the one that Ricardo and all later economists repeat after A. Smith, namely, that

<!-- ch24-p042 -->
> “the part of revenue, of which it is said, it has been added to capital, is consumed by productive labourers.”

<!-- ch24-p043 -->
According to this, all surplus-value that is changed into capital becomes variable capital (the part of capital spent on labor-power, which reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies). This is far from being the case. The surplus-value, like the original capital, divides into constant capital (the part of capital spent on means of production, whose value is passed on to the product but does not grow) and variable capital, into means of production and labor-power. Labor-power is the form in which variable capital exists during the process of production. In this process the labor-power is itself consumed by the capitalist, while the means of production are consumed by the labor-power as it performs its function, labor. At the same time, the money paid to buy the labor-power is converted into necessaries, which are consumed, not by “productive labour,” but by the “productive labourer.” Through a fundamentally perverted analysis, Adam Smith arrives at the absurd conclusion that, even though each individual capital is divided into a constant and a variable part, the capital of society breaks down only into variable capital, that is, it is laid out exclusively in paying wages. For instance, suppose a cloth manufacturer converts £2,000 into capital. He lays out one portion in buying weavers, the other in woollen yarn, machinery, etc. But the people from whom he buys the yarn and the machinery pay for labor with a part of the purchase money, and so on, until the whole £2,000 are spent in paying wages, that is, until the entire product represented by the £2,000 has been consumed by productive workers. Clearly, the whole point of this argument lies in the words “and so on,” which send us from pillar to post. In truth, Adam Smith breaks off his investigation just where its difficulties begin.[^17]

[^17]: In spite of his “Logic,” John St. Mill never detects even a faulty analysis like this one when his predecessors make it, an analysis that, even from the bourgeois standpoint of the science, cries out to be corrected. In every case he records the confusion of his master’s thoughts with the dogmatism of a disciple. So here: “The capital itself in the long run becomes entirely wages, and when replaced by the sale of produce becomes wages again.”

<!-- ch24-p044 -->
The annual process of reproduction is easy to understand, so long as we look only at the sum total of the year’s production. But every single component of this product must be brought onto the market as a commodity, and there the difficulty begins. The movements of the individual capitals and of the personal revenues cross and mix with each other, and they are lost in the general change of places, in the circulation of society’s wealth. This dazzles the eye and sets very complicated problems to be solved. In the third part of Book II I shall give an analysis of how the facts really hang together. It is one of the great merits of the Physiocrats (a school of 18th-century French economists) that in their *Tableau économique* [Economic Table] they were the first to try to depict the year’s production in the shape in which it presents itself to us after passing through the process of circulation.[^18]

[^18]: In his description of the process of reproduction and of accumulation, Adam Smith in many ways not only made no advance, but even lost considerable ground, compared with his predecessors, especially the Physiocrats. Connected with the illusion mentioned in the text is the really wonderful dogma that he left as an inheritance to political economy: the dogma that the price of commodities is made up of wages, profit (interest) and rent, that is, of wages and surplus-value. Starting from this basis, Storch naively confesses, “Il est impossible de résoudre le prix nécessaire dans ses éléments les plus simples.” [It is impossible to break down the necessary price into its simplest elements.] (Storch, cited above, Petersb. Edit., 1815, t. ii., p. 141, note.) A fine science of economics this is, which declares it impossible to break down the price of a commodity into its simplest elements! This point will be investigated further in the seventh part of Book iii.

<!-- ch24-p045 -->
For the rest, it goes without saying that political economy, acting in the interests of the capitalist class, has not failed to exploit Adam Smith’s doctrine, namely, that the whole of that part of the surplus-product which is converted into capital is consumed by the working class.
