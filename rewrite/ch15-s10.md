### Section 10: Modern Industry and Agriculture

<!-- ch15-p312 -->
The revolution that modern industry has brought about in agriculture, and in the social relations of agricultural producers, will be investigated later on. Here we shall only point out a few results in advance. If the use of machinery in agriculture is for the most part free from the harmful physical effects it has on the factory worker, its action in pushing out the workers is more intense, and meets less resistance, as we shall see later in detail. In the counties of Cambridge and Suffolk, for example, the area of cultivated land has grown very much within the last 20 years (up to 1868). In the same period the rural population has shrunk, not only relatively, but absolutely. In the United States agricultural machines as yet replace workers only virtually. In other words, they allow the farmer to cultivate a larger area, but do not actually drive out the workers employed. In 1861 the number of persons employed in England and Wales in the manufacture of agricultural machines was 1,034, while the number of agricultural workers employed in using agricultural machines and steam-engines was no more than 1,205.

In agriculture, modern industry has a more revolutionary effect than anywhere else, for this reason: it wipes out the peasant, that bulwark of the old society, and puts the wage worker in his place. So the desire for social change and the class antagonisms are brought to the same level in the country as in the towns. The irrational, old-fashioned methods of farming are replaced by scientific ones. Capitalist production completely tears apart the old bond that held agriculture and manufacture together in their infancy. But at the same time it creates the material conditions for a higher synthesis in the future, namely the union of agriculture and industry on the basis of the more perfected forms that each has acquired during their temporary separation. Capitalist production gathers the population in great centers and makes the town population ever more dominant. In this way, on the one hand, it concentrates the historical motive power of society. On the other hand, it disturbs the circulation of matter between man and the soil, that is, it prevents the return to the soil of those of its elements that man consumes in the form of food and clothing. So it violates the conditions necessary for the lasting fertility of the soil. By this action it destroys, at the same time, the health of the town worker and the intellectual life of the rural worker.[^245] But while it upsets the conditions that grew up naturally for maintaining that circulation of matter, it imperiously demands that this circulation be restored as a system, as a regulating law of social production, and in a form fit for the full development of the human race. In agriculture as in manufacture, the transformation of production under the rule of capital means, at the same time, the martyrdom of the producer. The instrument of labor becomes the means of enslaving, exploiting, and impoverishing the worker. The social combination and organization of labor processes is turned into an organized way of crushing out the worker's individual vitality, freedom, and independence. The scattering of the rural workers over larger areas breaks their power of resistance, while concentration increases the power of resistance of the town factory workers. In modern agriculture, as in the urban industries, the increased productivity and quantity of the labor set in motion are bought at the cost of laying waste labor-power itself and consuming it by disease. Moreover, all progress in capitalist agriculture is progress in the art not only of robbing the worker, but of robbing the soil. All progress in increasing the fertility of the soil for a given time is progress towards ruining the lasting sources of that fertility. The more a country, like the United States, for example, starts its development on the foundation of modern industry, the more rapid is this process of destruction.[^246] Capitalist production, therefore, develops technology, and the combining of various processes into a social whole, only by sapping the original sources of all wealth – the soil and the worker.

To have developed, from the point of view of natural science, the negative, that is, destructive, side of modern agriculture is one of Liebig’s immortal merits. His summary of the history of agriculture, too, although not free from gross errors, contains flashes of light. It is a pity, however, that he ventures on such haphazard assertions as the following: “By greater pulverising and more frequent ploughing, the circulation of air in the interior of porous soil is aided, and the surface exposed to the action of the atmosphere is increased and renewed; but it is easily seen that the increased yield of the land cannot be proportional to the labour spent on that land, but increases in a much smaller proportion. This law,” adds Liebig, “was first enunciated by John Stuart Mill in his ‘Principles of Pol. Econ.,’ Vol. 1, p. 17, as follows: ‘That the produce of land increases, *caeteris paribus* [other things being equal], in a diminishing ratio to the increase of the labourers employed’ (Mill here introduces in an erroneous form the law enunciated by Ricardo’s school, for since the ‘decrease of the labourers employed,’ kept even pace in England with the advance of agriculture, the law discovered in, and applied to, England, could have no application to that country, at all events), ‘is the universal law of agricultural industry.’ This is very remarkable, since Mill was ignorant of the reason for this law.” (Liebig, cited above, Bd. I., p. 143 and Note.) Leaving aside Liebig’s wrong interpretation of the word “labour,” by which he understands something quite different from what political economy means by it, it is in any case “very remarkable” that he should make Mr. John Stuart Mill the first to put forward a theory that was first published by James Anderson in A. Smith’s day, and was repeated in various works down to the beginning of the 19th century. It is a theory that Malthus, that master of plagiarism (the whole of his population theory is a shameless plagiarism), took over as his own in 1815; that West developed at the same time as Anderson and independently of him; that Ricardo, in the year 1817, connected with the general theory of value, after which it went around the world as Ricardo’s theory; that James Mill, the father of John Stuart Mill, vulgarized in 1820; and that, finally, John Stuart Mill and others reproduced as a dogma already quite commonplace and known to every schoolboy. It cannot be denied that John Stuart Mill owes his authority, which is at all events “remarkable,” almost entirely to such mix-ups.
