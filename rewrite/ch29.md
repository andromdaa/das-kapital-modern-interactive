## Chapter 29: Genesis of the Capitalist Farmer

<!-- ch29-p001 -->
We have now considered the forcible creation of a class of outlawed proletarians (the proletariat is the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power). We have considered the bloody discipline that turned them into wage workers, and the disgraceful action of the State, which used the police to speed up the accumulation of capital by raising the degree of exploitation of labor. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. The accumulation of capital is turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows.) The question remains: where did the capitalists come from in the first place? For the expropriation of the agricultural population (the taking away of their property, here their land) directly creates nothing but the greatest landed proprietors. As far as the genesis of the farmer is concerned, however, we can, so to speak, put our hand on it, because it is a slow process that unfolded over many centuries. The serfs, and the free small proprietors too, held land on very different terms of tenure, and so they were freed under very different economic conditions. In England the first form of the farmer is the bailiff, who was himself a serf. His position is similar to that of the old Roman *villicus* [estate overseer], only with a narrower field of action. During the second half of the 14th century he is replaced by a farmer whom the landlord provided with seed, cattle and implements. This farmer's condition is not very different from the peasant's. Only he exploits more wage labor. Soon he becomes a metayer, a half-farmer. He advances one part of the farm's stock, and the landlord advances the other. The two divide the total product in proportions fixed by contract. In England this form quickly disappears and gives way to the farmer proper. He makes his own capital breed by employing wage workers, and he pays a part of the surplus-product to the landlord as rent, in money or in kind. During the 15th century, as long as the independent peasant, and the farm-laborer who worked for himself as well as for wages, grew rich by their own labor, the farmer's circumstances and his field of production stayed equally middling. The agricultural revolution began in the last third of the 15th century and went on through almost the whole of the 16th (except, however, its last decade). It made the farmer rich just as fast as it made the mass of the agricultural people poor.[^1]

The seizure of the common lands let him greatly increase his stock of cattle, almost without cost, while they also gave him a richer supply of manure for tilling the soil. In the 16th century a very important element was added to this. At that time contracts for farms ran for a long time, often for 99 years. The steady fall in the value of the precious metals, and so of money, brought the farmers golden fruit. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) Apart from all the other circumstances discussed above, this fall lowered wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself). Part of the wages was now added to the profits of the farm. The continuous rise in the price of grain, wool, meat, in a word of all agricultural produce, swelled the money capital of the farm without any action on his part. (Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) Meanwhile the rent he paid, which was calculated on the old value of money, really shrank.[^2] So the farmers grew rich at the expense both of their laborers and of their landlords. No wonder, then, that by the end of the 16th century England had a class of capitalist farmers who were rich, considering the circumstances of the time.[^3]

[^2]: On how the depreciation of money in the 16th century affected the different classes of society, see “A Compendium of Briefe Examination of Certayne Ordinary Complaints of Divers of our Countrymen in these our Days,” by W. S. Gentleman (London 1581). Because this work is written as a dialogue, people for a long time believed Shakespeare wrote it, and even in 1751 it was published under his name. Its author is William Stafford. In one place the knight reasons as follows: *Knight*: You, my neighbor, the husbandman [farmer], you Maister Mercer [cloth merchant], and you Goodman Cooper [barrel-maker], with other artificers [craftsmen], may save yourselves metely [fairly] well. For as much as all things are dearer than they were, so much do you arise in the pryce of your wares and occupations that ye sell agayne. But we have nothing to sell whereby we might advance ye price there of, to countervaile [offset] those things that we must buy agayne.” In another place, the knight asks the doctor: “I pray you, what be those sorts that ye meane. And first, of those that ye thinke should have no losse thereby? *Doctor*: I mean all those that live by buying and selling, for as they buy deare, they sell thereafter. *Knight*: What is the next sort that ye say would win by it? *Doctor*: Marry [why], all such as have takings of fearmes [farms] in their owne manurance [cultivation] at the old rent, for where they pay after the olde rate they sell after the newe — that is, they paye for theire lande good cheape, and sell all things growing thereof deare. *Knight*: What sorte is that which, ye sayde should have greater losse hereby, than these men had profit? *Doctor*: It is all noblemen, gentlemen, and all other that live either by a stinted [fixed] rent or stypend, or do not manure [cultivate] the ground, or doe occupy no buying and selling.”

[^3]: In France, the régisseur, the steward and collector of dues for the feudal lords during the earlier part of the middle ages, soon became a man of business, who swindled his way into being a capitalist by extortion, cheating, etc. These régisseurs were sometimes noblemen themselves. For example, “C'est li compte que messire Jacques de Thoraine, chevalier chastelain sor Besançon rent és-seigneur tenant les comptes à Dijon pour monseigneur le duc et comte de Bourgoigne, des rentes appartenant à la dite chastellenie, depuis xxve jour de décembre MCCCLIX jusqu'au xxviiie jour de décembre MCCCLX.” [This is the account given by M. Jacques de Thoraisse, knight, and Lord of a manor near Besançon, to the lord who keeps the accounts at Dijon for his highness the Duke and Count of Burgundy, of the rents belonging to the said manor, from the 25th day of December 1359 to the 28th day of December 1360] (Alexis Monteil: “Traité de Matériaux Manuscrits etc.,” pp. 234, 235.) Here already it is clear how, in every sphere of social life, the lion's share falls to the middleman. In the economic sphere, for example, financiers, stock-exchange speculators, merchants and shopkeepers skim the cream. In civil matters, the lawyer fleeces his clients. In politics the representative counts for more than the voters, and the minister for more than the sovereign. In religion, God is pushed into the background by the “Mediator,” and the Mediator in turn is shoved back by the priests, the inevitable middlemen between the good shepherd and his sheep. In France, as in England, the great feudal territories were divided into countless small homesteads, but under conditions incomparably more favorable for the people. During the 14th century the farms, or *terriers*, arose. Their number grew constantly, to far beyond 100,000. They paid rents of from 1/12 to 1/5 of the product, in money or in kind. These farms were fiefs, sub-fiefs, etc. (lands held from a lord), according to the value and extent of the domains, and many of them contained only a few acres. But these farmers had, to some degree, rights of jurisdiction over the people living on the land; there were four grades. One can understand how the agricultural population was oppressed under all these petty tyrants. Monteil says that France once had 160,000 judges, where today 4,000 courts, including justices of the peace, are enough.
