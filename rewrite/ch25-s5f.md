#### F. Ireland

<!-- ch25-p247 -->
To finish this section, we must travel for a moment to Ireland. First, the main facts of the case.

<!-- ch25-p248 -->
In 1841 the population of Ireland had reached 8,222,664. By 1851 it had dwindled to 6,623,985; by 1861, to 5,850,309; by 1866, to 5½ millions, nearly its level in 1801. The decline began with the famine year, 1846, so that in less than twenty years Ireland lost more than 5/16ths of its people.[^119] Its total emigration from May, 1851, to July, 1865, numbered 1,591,487; the emigration during the years 1861-1865 was more than half-a-million. From 1851-1861 the number of inhabited houses fell by 52,990. From 1851-1861 the number of holdings of 15 to 30 acres rose by 61,000, and the number of holdings over 30 acres by 109,000, while the total number of all farms fell by 120,000. This fall, therefore, was due solely to the suppression of farms under 15 acres, that is, to their centralization.

England, a country with fully developed capitalist production, and above all an industrial country, would have bled to death from such a drain of population as Ireland has suffered. But Ireland is at present only an agricultural district of England, separated by a wide channel from the country to which it supplies grain, wool, cattle, and industrial and military recruits.

<!-- ch25-p273 -->
The depopulation of Ireland has thrown much of the land out of cultivation and has greatly reduced the produce of the soil.[^123] And in spite of the greater area given over to cattle breeding, the depopulation has brought about an absolute decline in some branches of cattle breeding, and in others an advance hardly worth mentioning and constantly interrupted by setbacks. Nevertheless, as the population fell in numbers, rents and farmers’ profits rose, although profits did not rise as steadily as rents. The reason for this is easy to understand. On the one hand, as small holdings were thrown together into large ones and arable land was changed into pasture, a larger part of the whole produce was turned into surplus-produce. The surplus-produce increased, although the total produce, of which it formed a fraction, decreased. On the other hand, the money value of this surplus-produce increased even faster than its mass, because of the rise in the English market price of meat, wool, etc., during the last 20 years, and especially during the last 10.

[^123]: If the product per acre also falls relatively, it must not be forgotten that for a century and a half England has indirectly exported the soil of Ireland, without even allowing its cultivators the means to replace the elements of the soil that had been exhausted.

<!-- ch25-p274 -->
The scattered means of production that serve the producers themselves as means of employment and of subsistence, without increasing their own value by absorbing the labor of others, are no more capital than a product consumed by its own producer is a commodity. If the mass of the means of production employed in agriculture fell along with the mass of the population, the mass of the capital employed in agriculture still increased. This is because a part of the means of production that were formerly scattered was concentrated and turned into capital.

<!-- ch25-p275 -->
The total capital of Ireland outside agriculture, employed in industry and trade, accumulated slowly during the last two decades, with great and constantly recurring fluctuations. All the more rapidly did the concentration of its individual parts develop. And however small its absolute increase, it had increased greatly in proportion to the shrinking population.

<!-- ch25-p276 -->
Here, then, before our own eyes and on a large scale, a process is revealed that orthodox economics could not have wished better for the support of its dogma: that misery springs from absolute surplus population, and that balance is restored by depopulation. This is a far more important experiment than the plague in the middle of the 14th century, which the Malthusians have praised so highly. Note further: only the naïveté of the schoolmaster could apply the standard of the 14th century to the conditions of production and population of the nineteenth century. But on top of that, this naïveté overlooked a fact. After the plague and the decimation that went with it, on this side of the Channel, in England, the agricultural population was freed and grew richer; on that side, in France, there followed greater servitude and more misery.[^124]

[^124]: Because Ireland is regarded as the promised land of the “principle of population,” Th. Sadler, before publishing his work on population, issued his famous book, “Ireland, its Evils and their Remedies.” 2nd edition, London, 1829. In it he compares the statistics of the individual provinces, and of the individual counties in each province, and proves that the misery there is not, as Malthus would have it, in proportion to the size of the population, but in inverse proportion to it.

<!-- ch25-p277 -->
The Irish famine of 1846 killed more than 1,000,000 people, but it killed poor devils only. It did not do the slightest damage to the wealth of the country. The exodus of the next 20 years, an exodus still constantly growing, did not decimate their means of production along with the human beings, as, for example, the Thirty Years’ War did. Irish genius discovered an altogether new way of spiriting a poor people thousands of miles away from the scene of its misery. The exiles transplanted to the United States send sums of money home every year as traveling expenses for those left behind. Every troop that emigrates one year draws another after it the next. So, instead of costing Ireland anything, emigration forms one of the most profitable branches of its export trade. Finally, it is a systematic process. It does not simply make a passing gap in the population, but every year sucks out of it more people than births replace, so that the absolute level of the population falls year by year.[^125]

[^125]: Between 1851 and 1874, the total number of emigrants came to 2,325,922.

<!-- ch25-p278 -->
What were the consequences for the Irish workers left behind and freed from the surplus population? That the relative surplus population is as great today as before 1846; that wages are just as low; that the oppression of the workers has increased; that misery is forcing the country toward a new crisis. The facts are simple. The revolution in agriculture has kept pace with emigration. The production of relative surplus population has more than kept pace with the absolute depopulation. A glance at table C. shows that the change of arable land to pasture must work even more sharply in Ireland than in England. In England the growing of green crops increases with the breeding of cattle; in Ireland, it decreases. While a large number of acres that were formerly tilled lie idle or are turned permanently into grass-land, a great part of the waste land and peat bogs that were formerly unused now serve to extend cattle-breeding. The smaller and medium farmers – I count among these all who do not farm more than 100 acres – still make up about 8/10ths of the whole number.[^126] One after another, and with a force unknown before, they are crushed by the competition of an agriculture run by capital, and so they continually supply new recruits to the class of wage workers. The one great industry of Ireland, linen manufacture, needs relatively few adult men. In spite of its expansion since the price of cotton rose in 1861-1866, it employs altogether only a comparatively insignificant part of the population. Like all other great modern industries, it constantly produces, through incessant fluctuations, a relative surplus population within its own sphere, even when the mass of human beings it absorbs increases absolutely. The misery of the agricultural population forms the pedestal for gigantic shirt-factories, whose armies of workers are, for the most part, scattered over the country. Here we meet again the system of domestic industry described above, which, in underpayment and overwork, possesses its own systematic means of creating superfluous workers. Finally, although the depopulation does not have such destructive consequences as it would have in a country with fully developed capitalist production, it does not go on without constantly reacting on the home market. The gap that emigration causes here limits not only the local demand for labor, but also the incomes of small shopkeepers, artisans, and tradespeople generally. Hence the fall in incomes between £60 and £100 in Table E.

[^126]: According to a table in Murphy’s “Ireland Industrial, Political and Social,” 1870, 94.6 percent of the holdings do not reach 100 acres, and 5.4 exceed 100 acres.

In spite of this, however, they do not let their government lull itself with illusions. According to them, the rate of wages in the country, still very low, has risen 50-60 percent within the last 20 years, and now stands, on the average, at 6s. to 9s. per week. In fact, the agricultural workers used to be simply the smallest of the small farmers, and for the most part they formed a kind of rear-guard of the medium and large farms on which they found work. Only since the catastrophe of 1846 have they begun to form a section of the class of purely wage workers, a special class, connected with its employers only by money relations.

<!-- ch25-p285 -->
We know what their housing conditions were in 1846. Since then they have grown even worse. A part of the agricultural workers, though a part that grows smaller day by day, still lives on the farmers’ holdings in over-crowded huts, whose hideousness far surpasses the worst that the English agricultural workers showed us in this line. And this holds generally, with the exception of certain tracts of Ulster; in the south, in the counties of Cork, Limerick, Kilkenny, etc.; in the east, in Wicklow, Wexford, etc.; in the center of Ireland, in King’s and Queen’s County, Dublin, etc.; in the west, in Sligo, Roscommon, Mayo, Galway, etc.

The first act of the agricultural revolution was to sweep away the huts standing on the field of labor. This was done on the largest scale, and as if in obedience to a command from on high. Thus many workers were forced to look for shelter in villages and towns. There they were thrown like refuse into garrets, holes, cellars, and corners, in the worst back slums. Thousands of Irish families found themselves suddenly transplanted into hotbeds of vice – families who, according to the testimony of the English (eaten up as the English are with national prejudice), are notable for their rare attachment to hearth and home, for their cheerfulness, and for the purity of their home life. The men now have to look for work from the neighboring farmers and are hired only by the day, and so under the most precarious form of wage. Hence

These results of the agricultural revolution – that is, of the change of arable land into pasture, the use of machinery, the strictest saving of labor, etc. – are made still worse by the model landlords, who, instead of spending their rents in other countries, condescend to live in Ireland on their demesnes. The uncertainty and irregularity of employment, the constant return and long duration of gluts of labor – all these symptoms of a relative surplus population therefore appear in the reports of the Poor Law administration as so many hardships of the agricultural proletariat. It will be remembered that we met a similar spectacle among the English agricultural proletariat. But the difference is this. In England, an industrial country, the industrial reserve recruits itself from the country districts. In Ireland, an agricultural country, the agricultural reserve recruits itself from the towns, the cities of refuge of the agricultural workers who have been driven out. In England, the people agriculture has left over are turned into factory workers. In Ireland, those forced into the towns press on wages in the towns, but at the same time they remain agricultural workers, and are constantly sent back to the country districts in search of work.

<!-- ch25-p295 -->
The official inspectors sum up the material condition of the agricultural worker as follows:

<!-- ch25-p296 -->
> “Though living with the strictest frugality, his own wages are barely sufficient to provide food for an ordinary family and pay his rent” and he depends upon other sources for the means of clothing himself, his wife, and children.... The atmosphere of these cabins, combined with the other privations they are subjected to, has made this class particularly susceptible to low fever and pulmonary consumption [tuberculosis of the lungs].”[^138]

After this, it is no wonder that, according to the unanimous testimony of the inspectors, a gloomy discontent runs through the ranks of this class; that they long for the return of the past, loathe the present, despair of the future, give themselves up “to the evil influence of agitators,” and have only one fixed idea, to emigrate to America. This is the land of Cockaigne, the fabled land of plenty, into which the great Malthusian cure-all, depopulation, has transformed green Erin.

<!-- ch25-p298 -->
One example will show what a happy life the Irish factory worker leads:

<!-- ch25-p299 -->
> “On my recent visit to the North of Ireland,” says the English Factory Inspector, Robert Baker, “I met with the following evidence of effort in an Irish skilled workman to afford education to his children; and I give his evidence verbatim, as I took it from his mouth. That he was a skilled factory hand, may be understood when I say that he was employed on goods for the Manchester market. ‘Johnson. – I am a beetler [a worker who pounds linen cloth to finish it] and work from 6 in the morning till 11 at night, from Monday to Friday. Saturday we leave off at 6 p. m., and get three hours of it (for meals and rest). I have five children in all. For this work I get 10s. 6d. a week; my wife works here also, and gets 5s. a week. The oldest girl who is 12, minds the house. She is also cook, and all the servant we have. She gets the young ones ready for school. A girl going past the house wakes me at half past five in the morning. My wife gets up and goes along with me. We get nothing (to eat) before we come to work. The child of 12 takes care of the little children all the day, and we get nothing till breakfast at eight. At eight we go home. We get tea once a week; at other times we get stirabout [porridge], sometimes of oat-meal, sometimes of Indian meal [cornmeal], as we are able to get it. In the winter we get a little sugar and water to our Indian meal. In the summer we get a few potatoes, planting a small patch ourselves; and when they are done we get back to stirabout. Sometimes we get a little milk as it may be. So we go on from day to day, Sunday and week day, always the same the year round. I am always very much tired when I have done at night. We may see a bit of flesh meat sometimes, but very seldom. Three of our children attend school, for whom we pay 1d. a week a head. Our rent is 9d. a week. Peat for firing costs 1s. 6d. a fortnight at the very lowest.’”[^139]

But the lion’s share of the yearly national rental that an inconceivably small number of land magnates in England, Scotland and Ireland swallow up is so monstrous that the wisdom of the English State does not think fit to supply the same statistical materials about the distribution of rents as about the distribution of profits. Lord Dufferin is one of those land magnates. That rent-rolls and profits can ever be “excessive,” or that their plethora is in any way connected with a plethora of the people’s misery, is, of course, an idea as “disreputable” as it is “unsound.” He keeps to facts. The fact, as he sees it, is that as the Irish population shrinks, the Irish rent-rolls swell; that depopulation benefits the landlords, and therefore also benefits the soil, and therefore the people, that mere accessory of the soil. He declares, therefore, that Ireland is still over-populated, and that the stream of emigration still flows too lazily. To be perfectly happy, Ireland must get rid of at least one-third of a million working men. Let no one imagine that this lord, a poet into the bargain, is a physician of the school of Sangrado, who, whenever he did not find his patient better, ordered bloodletting and more bloodletting, until the patient lost his sickness at the same time as his blood. Lord Dufferin demands a new bloodletting of only one-third of a million, instead of about two millions; in fact, without getting rid of these, the millennium in Erin cannot come. The proof is easily given.

From 1851 to 1861 centralization destroyed mainly farms of the first three categories, under 1 and not over 15 acres. These above all must disappear. This gives 307,058 “supernumerary” farmers, and, counting the families at the low average of 4 persons, 1,228,232 persons. On the extravagant supposition that one-fourth of these can be absorbed again after the agricultural revolution is complete, there remain 921,174 persons for emigration. Categories 4, 5, 6, of over 15 and not over 100 acres, are, as has long been known in England, too small for the capitalist cultivation of grain, and for sheep-breeding they are almost vanishing quantities. On the same supposition as before, therefore, there are a further 788,761 persons to emigrate; total, 1,709,532. And as l’appétit vient en mangeant [appetite comes with eating], Rentroll’s eyes will soon discover that Ireland, with 3½ millions, is still miserable, and miserable because she is over-populated. Therefore her depopulation must go still further, so that she may fulfill her true destiny, that of an English sheep-walk and cattle-pasture.”[^141]

There I also return to the condition of the small farmers and the agricultural workers. For now, only one quotation. Nassau W. Senior says, among other things, in his posthumous work, “Journals, Conversations and Essays relating to Ireland.” 2 vols. London, 1868; Vol. II., p. 282: “Well,” said Dr. G., “we have got our Poor Law and it is a great instrument for giving the victory to the landlords. Another, and a still more powerful instrument is emigration.... No friend to Ireland can wish the war to be prolonged [between the landlords and the small Celtic farmers] — still less, that it should end by the victory of the tenants. The sooner it is over — the sooner Ireland becomes a grazing country, with the comparatively thin population which a grazing country requires, the better for all classes.” The English Corn Laws of 1815 secured Ireland the monopoly of the free import of grain into Great Britain. So they artificially favored the growing of grain. With the abolition of the Corn Laws in 1846, this monopoly was suddenly removed. Apart from all other circumstances, this event alone was enough to give a great push to the turning of Irish arable land into pasture, to the concentration of farms, and to the eviction of small cultivators. From 1815 to 1846 the fruitfulness of the Irish soil had been praised, and loudly proclaimed as destined by Nature herself for the growing of wheat. After that, English agronomists, economists, and politicians suddenly discover that it is good for nothing but producing forage. M. Léonce de Lavergne has hurried to repeat this on the other side of the Channel. It takes a “serious” man, in Lavergne’s style, to be caught by such childishness.
