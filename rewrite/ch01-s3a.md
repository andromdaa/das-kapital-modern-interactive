### Section 3: The Form of Value or Exchange-Value

<!-- ch01-p037 -->
Commodities come into the world in the shape of use-values, articles, or goods, such as iron, linen, grain, etc. This is their plain, homely, bodily form. They are commodities, however, only because they are something twofold: both useful objects and, at the same time, containers of value. So they show themselves as commodities, or have the form of commodities, only insofar as they have two forms: a physical or natural form, and a form of value (the way the value of one commodity is expressed in another commodity).

<!-- ch01-p038 -->
The reality of the value of commodities differs in one respect from Dame Quickly (the hostess in Shakespeare's *Henry IV*): we don't know “where to have it” [how to get hold of it]. The value of commodities is the exact opposite of the coarse material stuff they are made of. Not an atom of matter goes into it. We can turn a single commodity over and examine it by itself as much as we like, yet insofar as it remains an object of value, it seems impossible to grasp. But suppose we bear in mind that the value of commodities has a purely social reality. They acquire this reality only insofar as they are expressions or embodiments of one identical social substance, namely, human labor. Then it follows as a matter of course that value can show itself only in the social relation of commodity to commodity. In fact we started from exchange-value, or the exchange relation of commodities, in order to get at the value that lies hidden behind it. We must now return to this form in which value first appeared to us.

<!-- ch01-p039 -->
Everyone knows, if he knows nothing else, that commodities have a form of value common to them all, which stands in marked contrast with the varied bodily forms of their use-values. I mean their money form (the form of value in which one commodity, gold, has become the universal equivalent). Here, however, we are set a task that *bourgeois* economics has never yet even attempted. The task is to trace the origin of this money form. That means developing the expression of value contained in the value relation of commodities, from its simplest, almost invisible outline, up to the dazzling money form. By doing this we shall, at the same time, solve the riddle that money presents.

<!-- ch01-p040 -->
The simplest value relation is clearly that of one commodity to some one other commodity of a different kind. So the relation between the values of two commodities gives us the simplest expression of the value of a single commodity.

#### A. Elementary or Accidental Form Of Value

<!-- ch01-p041 -->
x commodity A = y commodity B, or\
x commodity A is worth y commodity B.\
20 yards of linen = 1 coat, or\
20 Yards of linen are worth 1 coat.

##### 1. The two poles of the expression of value. Relative form and Equivalent form

<!-- ch01-p042 -->
The whole mystery of the form of value lies hidden in this elementary form. So analyzing it is our real difficulty.

<!-- ch01-p043 -->
Here two different kinds of commodities (in our example the linen and the coat) clearly play two different parts. The linen expresses its value in the coat. The coat serves as the material in which that value is expressed. The linen plays an active part, the coat a passive one. The value of the linen is represented as relative value, or appears in relative form. The coat acts as equivalent, or appears in equivalent form.

<!-- ch01-p044 -->
The relative form and the equivalent form are two closely connected elements of the expression of value. They depend on each other and cannot be separated. But at the same time they are mutually exclusive, opposed extremes, that is, poles of the same expression. They are assigned to the two different commodities that the expression brings into relation, one to each. It is not possible to express the value of linen in linen. 20 yards of linen = 20 yards of linen is no expression of value. On the contrary, such an equation says only that 20 yards of linen are nothing other than 20 yards of linen, a definite quantity of the use-value linen. So the value of the linen can be expressed only relatively, that is, in some other commodity. The relative form of value of the linen therefore presupposes that some other commodity, here the coat, is present in the form of an equivalent. On the other hand, the commodity that figures as the equivalent cannot at the same time take on the relative form. That second commodity is not the one whose value is expressed. Its function is only to serve as the material in which the value of the first commodity is expressed.

<!-- ch01-p045 -->
No doubt the expression 20 yards of linen = 1 coat, or 20 yards of linen are worth 1 coat, implies the opposite relation: 1 coat = 20 yards of linen, or 1 coat is worth 20 yards of linen. But in that case I must reverse the equation in order to express the value of the coat relatively. And as soon as I do that, the linen becomes the equivalent instead of the coat. So a single commodity cannot take on both forms at once in the same expression of value. The very polarity of these forms makes them mutually exclusive.

<!-- ch01-p046 -->
Whether a commodity takes on the relative form or the opposite, equivalent form, then, depends entirely on its accidental position in the expression of value. That is, it depends on whether it is the commodity whose value is being expressed, or the commodity in which value is being expressed.

##### 2. The Relative Form of value

<!-- ch01-p047 -->
(a.) The nature and meaning of this form

<!-- ch01-p048 -->
We want to discover how the elementary expression of the value of a commodity lies hidden in the value relation of two commodities. To do so, we must first consider that relation entirely apart from its quantitative side. The usual procedure is generally the reverse: in the value relation people see nothing but the proportion between definite quantities of two different sorts of commodities that are counted as equal to each other. It is easily forgotten that the magnitudes of different things can be compared in quantity only when those magnitudes are expressed in terms of the same unit. Only as expressions of such a unit are they of the same denomination, and therefore measurable against each other.[^17]

[^17]: The few economists, among them S. Bailey, who have busied themselves with analyzing the form of value have been unable to reach any result. First, because they confuse the form of value with value itself. Second, because, under the coarse influence of the practical bourgeois, they pay attention only to the quantitative side of the question. “The command of quantity ... constitutes value.” (“Money and its Vicissitudes.” London, 1837, p. 11. By S. Bailey.)

<!-- ch01-p049 -->
Whether 20 yards of linen = 1 coat or = 20 coats or = x coats, that is, whether a given quantity of linen is worth few coats or many, every such statement implies that the linen and the coats, as magnitudes of value, are expressions of the same unit, things of the same kind. Linen = coat is the basis of the equation.

<!-- ch01-p050 -->
But the two commodities whose sameness of quality is assumed in this way do not play the same part. Only the value of the linen is expressed. And how? By its reference to the coat as its equivalent, as something that can be exchanged for it. In this relation the coat is the form in which value exists, it is value embodied, for only as such is it the same as the linen. On the other hand, the linen's own value comes to the front and receives an independent expression. For it is only as value that the linen is comparable with the coat as a thing of equal value, or exchangeable with the coat. To borrow an illustration from chemistry: butyric acid is a different substance from propyl formate. Yet both are made up of the same chemical substances, carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O), and in the same proportions too, namely C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>2</sub>. Now suppose we equate butyric acid to propyl formate. Then, in the first place, propyl formate would be, in this relation, merely a form of existence of C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>2</sub>. And in the second place, we would be stating that butyric acid also consists of C<sub>4</sub>H<sub>8</sub>O<sub>2</sub>. So by equating the two substances in this way, we would express their chemical composition, while their different physical forms would be left out of account.

<!-- ch01-p051 -->
If we say that, as values, commodities are mere congealed amounts of human labor, our analysis does, it is true, reduce them to the abstraction, value. But we give this value no form apart from their bodily form. It is different in the value relation of one commodity to another. There, the one stands out in its character of value because of its relation to the other.

<!-- ch01-p052 -->
By making the coat the equivalent of the linen, we equate the labor embodied in the coat to the labor in the linen. Now, it is true that the tailoring that makes the coat is concrete labor of a different sort from the weaving that makes the linen. But the act of equating tailoring to weaving reduces the tailoring to what is really equal in the two kinds of labor, to their common character as human labor. In this roundabout way, then, the fact is expressed that weaving too, insofar as it weaves value, has nothing to distinguish it from tailoring, and so is abstract human labor. Only the expression of equivalence between different sorts of commodities brings out the specific character of value-creating labor. It does this by actually reducing the different kinds of labor embodied in the different kinds of commodities to their common quality of abstract human labor.[^18]

[^18]: The famous Franklin, one of the first economists after Wm. Petty to see through the nature of value, says: “Trade in general being nothing else but the exchange of labour for labour, the value of all things is ... most justly measured by labour.” (“The works of B. Franklin, &c.,” edited by Sparks. Boston, 1836, Vol. II., p. 267.) Franklin is not aware that, by estimating the value of everything in labor, he sets aside any difference in the sorts of labor exchanged, and so reduces them all to equal human labor. But although he does not know it, he says it. He speaks first of “the one labour,” then of “the other labour,” and finally of “labour,” with no further qualification, as the substance of the value of everything.

<!-- ch01-p053 -->
But something more is needed beyond expressing the specific character of the labor that the value of the linen consists of. Human labor-power in motion, or human labor, creates value, but is not itself value. It becomes value only in its congealed state, when it is embodied in the form of some object. To express the value of the linen as congealed human labor, that value must be expressed as something with an objective existence. It must be something materially different from the linen itself, and yet something common to the linen and all other commodities. The problem is already solved.

<!-- ch01-p054 -->
When the coat occupies the position of equivalent in the equation of value, it ranks in quality as the equal of the linen, as something of the same kind, because it is value. In this position it is a thing in which we see nothing but value, or whose tangible bodily form represents value. Yet the coat itself, the body of the commodity coat, is a mere use-value. A coat as such no more tells us that it is value than the first piece of linen we pick up does. This shows that when the coat is placed in a value relation to the linen, it signifies more than it does outside that relation. In the same way, many a man strutting about in a gorgeous uniform counts for more than he does in plain clothes.

<!-- ch01-p055 -->
In the production of the coat, human labor-power, in the shape of tailoring, must actually have been spent. So human labor has piled up in it. In this respect the coat is a container of value, but even when worn threadbare, it does not let this fact show through. And as the equivalent of the linen in the value equation, it exists in this respect alone, and so counts as embodied value, as a body that is value. A, for instance, cannot be “your majesty” to B unless, at the same time, majesty in B's eyes takes on the bodily form of A. What is more, majesty changes its features, hair, and many other things besides, with every new father of the people.

<!-- ch01-p056 -->
So in the value equation in which the coat is the equivalent of the linen, the coat serves as the form of value. The value of the commodity linen is expressed by the bodily form of the commodity coat, the value of one by the use-value of the other. As a use-value, the linen is something tangibly different from the coat. As value, it is the same as the coat, and now looks like a coat. In this way the linen acquires a form of value different from its physical form. The fact that it is value is made visible by its equality with the coat, just as the sheep's nature of a Christian is shown in his resemblance to the Lamb of God.

<!-- ch01-p057 -->
We see, then, that everything our analysis of the value of commodities has already told us is told us by the linen itself, as soon as it enters into dealings with another commodity, the coat. Only it gives away its thoughts in the one language it knows, the language of commodities. To tell us that its own value is created by labor in its abstract character as human labor, it says that the coat, insofar as it is worth as much as the linen, and therefore is value, consists of the same labor as the linen. To let us know that its sublime reality as value is not the same as its buckram body (its body of stiff cloth), it says that value looks like a coat, and so that, insofar as the linen is value, it and the coat are as like as two peas. We may remark here that the language of commodities has, besides Hebrew, many other more or less correct dialects. The German “Wertsein,” to be worth, for instance, expresses less strikingly than the Romance verbs “valere,” “valer,” “valoir” [each: to be worth] that the equating of commodity B to commodity A is commodity A's own way of expressing its value. *Paris vaut bien une messe*. [Paris is well worth a mass]

<!-- ch01-p058 -->
So, by means of the value relation expressed in our equation, the bodily form of commodity B becomes the form of value of commodity A. In other words, the body of commodity B acts as a mirror to the value of commodity A.[^19] Commodity A relates itself to commodity B as value in person, as the matter that human labor is made of. By doing so, commodity A turns the use-value B into the substance in which it expresses its own, A's, value. The value of A, expressed in this way in the use-value of B, has taken the form of relative value.

[^19]: In a way, it is with man as with commodities. Man does not come into the world with a looking glass in his hand, nor as a Fichtean philosopher, for whom “I am I” is enough. So he first sees and recognizes himself in other men. Peter establishes his own identity as a man only by first comparing himself with Paul as a being of the same kind. And in this way Paul, just as he stands, in his Pauline personality, becomes for Peter the type of the genus homo [the human kind].

<!-- ch01-p059 -->
(b.) How the quantity of relative value is determined

<!-- ch01-p060 -->
Every commodity whose value is to be expressed is a useful object of a given quantity, such as 15 bushels of grain, or 100 lbs of coffee. And a given quantity of any commodity contains a definite quantity of human labor. So the form of value must express not only value in general, but also value in a definite quantity. Therefore, in the value relation of commodity A to commodity B, of the linen to the coat, not only is the coat, as value in general, made the equal of the linen in quality, but a definite quantity of coat (1 coat) is made the equivalent of a definite quantity (20 yards) of linen.

<!-- ch01-p061 -->
The equation, 20 yards of linen = 1 coat, or 20 yards of linen are worth one coat, implies that the same quantity of value substance (congealed labor) is embodied in both. It implies that the two commodities have each cost the same amount of labor, of the same quantity of labor-time. But the labor-time necessary to produce 20 yards of linen or 1 coat changes with every change in the productivity of weaving or tailoring. We now have to consider how such changes affect the quantitative side of the relative expression of value.

<!-- ch01-p062 -->
I. Let the value of the linen vary,[^20] while that of the coat stays constant. Say the labor-time necessary to produce the linen is doubled, as a result, perhaps, of the exhaustion of the soil that grows flax. Then the value of the linen will also be doubled. Instead of the equation 20 yards of linen = 1 coat, we would have 20 yards of linen = 2 coats, since 1 coat would now contain only half the labor-time embodied in 20 yards of linen. If, on the other hand, this labor-time is cut by one-half, as a result, say, of improved looms, the value of the linen would fall by one-half. So we would have 20 yards of linen = ½ coat. The relative value of commodity A, that is, its value expressed in commodity B, rises and falls directly with the value of A, the value of B being assumed constant.

[^20]: Here, as now and then in the preceding pages, value is used in the sense of value determined as to quantity, or of magnitude of value.

<!-- ch01-p063 -->
II. Let the value of the linen stay constant, while the value of the coat varies. Under these conditions, suppose the labor-time necessary to produce a coat doubles, as a result, for instance, of a poor crop of wool. Then instead of 20 yards of linen = 1 coat, we have 20 yards of linen = ½ coat. If, on the other hand, the value of the coat sinks by one-half, then 20 yards of linen = 2 coats. So if the value of commodity A stays constant, its relative value expressed in commodity B rises and falls inversely with the value of B.

<!-- ch01-p064 -->
If we compare the different cases in I and II, we see that the same change in the magnitude of relative value may arise from completely opposite causes. Thus the equation 20 yards of linen = 1 coat becomes 20 yards of linen = 2 coats, either because the value of the linen has doubled, or because the value of the coat has fallen by one-half. And it becomes 20 yards of linen = ½ coat, either because the value of the linen has fallen by one-half, or because the value of the coat has doubled.

<!-- ch01-p065 -->
III. Let the quantities of labor-time necessary to produce the linen and the coat vary at the same time, in the same direction and in the same proportion. In this case 20 yards of linen continue to equal 1 coat, however much their values may have changed. Their change of value shows up as soon as they are compared with a third commodity whose value has stayed constant. If the values of all commodities rose or fell at the same time, and in the same proportion, their relative values would stay unchanged. Their real change of value would show in the smaller or larger quantity of commodities produced in a given time.

<!-- ch01-p066 -->
IV. The labor-time necessary to produce the linen and the coat, and therefore the value of these commodities, may vary at the same time in the same direction but at unequal rates, or in opposite directions, or in other ways. The effect of all these possible variations on the relative value of a commodity can be deduced from the results of I, II, and III.
