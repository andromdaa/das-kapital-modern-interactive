## Chapter 25: The General Law of Capitalist Accumulation

### Section 1: The Increased Demand for labor power that Accompanies Accumulation, the Composition of Capital Remaining the same

<!-- ch25-p001 -->
In this chapter we consider how the growth of capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger) affects the lot of the working class. The most important factor in this inquiry is the composition of capital and the changes it goes through in the course of the process of accumulation (turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows).

<!-- ch25-p002 -->
The composition of capital is to be understood in a two-fold sense. On the side of value, it is fixed by the proportion in which capital is divided into constant capital, or the value of the means of production, and variable capital, or the value of labor-power, the sum total of wages. Here value means what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. The means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work. Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself. On the side of material, as it functions in the process of production, all capital is divided into means of production and living labor-power. This second composition is fixed by the relation between the mass of the means of production employed, on the one hand, and the mass of labor needed to employ them, on the other. I call the first the *value-composition* of capital, and the second its *technical composition*.

<!-- ch25-p003 -->
There is a strict correlation between the two. To express it, I call the value composition of capital, insofar as it is determined by its technical composition and mirrors the changes in that technical composition, the *organic composition* of capital. Wherever I speak of the composition of capital without further qualification, its organic composition is always meant.

<!-- ch25-p004 -->
The many individual capitals invested in a particular branch of production have compositions that differ, more or less, from one another. The average of their individual compositions gives us the composition of the total capital in this branch of production. Lastly, the average of these averages, across all branches of production, gives us the composition of the total social capital of a country. In the last resort, it is with this alone that we are concerned in the investigation that follows.

<!-- ch25-p005 -->
Growth of capital involves growth of its variable part, the part invested in labor-power. A part of the surplus-value (the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it) that is turned into additional capital must always be turned back into variable capital, or additional labor fund. Let us suppose that, all other circumstances remaining the same, the composition of capital also remains constant (that is, that a definite mass of means of production always needs the same mass of labor-power to set it in motion). Then the demand for labor and the subsistence-fund of the workers clearly increase in the same proportion as the capital, and the faster the capital increases, the faster they increase. The capital produces a surplus-value every year, and one part of it is added to the original capital every year. This increment itself grows every year along with the growth of the capital already functioning. Lastly, under a special stimulus to getting rich, such as the opening of new markets, or of new spheres for investing capital as a result of newly developed social wants, etc., the scale of accumulation may be suddenly extended, merely by a change in the way the surplus-value or surplus-product is divided into capital and revenue. Because of all this, the requirements of accumulating capital may exceed the increase of labor-power or of the number of workers; the demand for workers may exceed the supply, and so wages may rise. Indeed, this must ultimately be the case if the conditions supposed above continue. For since more workers are employed in each year than in the year before, sooner or later a point must be reached at which the requirements of accumulation begin to outrun the customary supply of labor, and so a rise of wages takes place. A lament on this score was heard in England during the whole of the fifteenth century and the first half of the eighteenth. The more or less favorable circumstances in which the wage-working class supports and multiplies itself in no way alter the fundamental character of capitalist production. Simple reproduction (production repeated on the same scale, with the capitalist consuming all the surplus-value) constantly reproduces the capital relation itself, that is, the relation of capitalists on the one side and wage workers on the other. In the same way, reproduction on an extended scale, that is, accumulation, reproduces the capital relation on an extended scale: more capitalists, or larger capitalists, at this pole, more wage workers at that one. The reproduction of a mass of labor-power is, in fact, an essential part of the reproduction of capital itself. This labor-power must constantly re-incorporate itself into capital, for that capital's self-expansion. It cannot get free from capital, and its enslavement to capital is only concealed by the variety of individual capitalists to whom it sells itself. Accumulation of capital is, therefore, increase of the proletariat (the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power).[^1]

[^1]: Karl Marx, cited above. “A égalité d’oppression des masses, plus un pays a de prolétaires et plus il est riche.” [With the oppression of the masses being equal, the more proletarians a country has, the richer it is.] (Colins, “L’Economie Politique. Source des Révolutions et des Utopies, prétendues Socialistes.” Paris, 1857, t. III., p. 331.) Our “prolétarian” is, economically, none other than the wage worker who produces and increases capital, and who is thrown out on the streets as soon as he is superfluous to the needs of growth of “Monsieur capital,” as Pecqueur calls this person. “The sickly proletarian of the primitive forest” is a pretty Roscherian fancy. The primitive forester owns the primitive forest, and uses the primitive forest as his property with the freedom of an orangutan. So he is not a proletarian. He would only be one if the primitive forest exploited him, instead of being exploited by him. As far as his health is concerned, such a man would compare well, not only with the modern proletarian, but also with the syphilitic and scrofulous (tubercular) upper classes. But no doubt Herr Wilhelm Roscher means by “primitive forest” his native heath of Lüneburg.

Classical economics (the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production) grasped this fact so thoroughly that Adam Smith, Ricardo, etc., as mentioned earlier, inaccurately identified accumulation with the consumption of the whole capitalized part of the surplus-product by productive workers, or with its transformation into additional wage workers. As early as 1696 John Bellers says:

<!-- ch25-p007 -->
> “For if one had a hundred thousand acres of land and as many pounds in money, and as many cattle, without a labourer, what would the rich man be, but a labourer? And as the labourers make men rich, so the more labourers there will be, the more rich men ... the labour of the poor being the mines of the rich.”[^2]

[^2]: John Bellers, cited above, p. 2.

<!-- ch25-p008 -->
So too Bernard de Mandeville, at the beginning of the eighteenth century:

<!-- ch25-p009 -->
> “It would be easier, where property is well secured, to live without money than without poor; for who would do the work? ... As they [the poor] ought to be kept from starving, so they should receive nothing worth saving. If here and there one of the lowest class by uncommon industry, and pinching his belly, lifts himself above the condition he was brought up in, nobody ought to hinder him; nay, it is undeniably the wisest course for every person in the society, and for every private family to be frugal; but it is the interest of all rich nations, that the greatest part of the poor should almost never be idle, and yet continually spend what they get.... Those that get their living by their daily labour ... have nothing to stir them up to be serviceable but their wants which it is prudence to relieve, but folly to cure. The only thing then that can render the labouring man industrious, is a moderate quantity of money, for as too little will, according as his temper is, either dispirit or make him desperate, so too much will make him insolent and lazy.... From what has been said, it is manifest, that, in a free nation, where slaves are not allowed of, the surest wealth consists in a multitude of laborious poor; for besides, that they are the never-failing nursery of fleets and armies, without them there could be no enjoyment, and no product of any country could be valuable. “To make the society” [which of course consists of non-workers] “happy and people easier under the meanest circumstances, it is requisite that great numbers of them should be ignorant as well as poor; knowledge both enlarges and multiplies our desires, and the fewer things a man wishes for, the more easily his necessities may be supplied.”[^3]

[^3]: Bernard de Mandeville: “The Fable of the Bees,” 5th edition, London, 1728. Remarks, pp. 212, 213, 328. “Temperate living and constant employment is the direct road, for the poor, to rational happiness” [by which he most probably means long working days and scant means of subsistence], “and to riches and strength for the state” (namely, for the landlords, capitalists, and their political dignitaries and agents). (“An Essay on Trade and Commerce,” London, 1770, p. 54.)

<!-- ch25-p010 -->
What Mandeville, an honest, clear-headed man, had not yet seen is that the mechanism of the process of accumulation itself increases, along with the capital, the mass of the “labouring poor,” that is, the wage workers. They turn their labor-power into an increasing power of self-expansion of the growing capital, and even by doing so they must make eternal their relation of dependence on their own product, as personified in the capitalists. On this relation of dependence, Sir F. M. Eden, in his “The State of the Poor, an History of the Labouring Classes in England,” says,

<!-- ch25-p011 -->
> “the natural produce of our soil is certainly not fully adequate to our subsistence; we can neither be clothed, lodged nor fed but in consequence of some previous labour. A portion at least of the society must be indefatigably employed .... There are others who, though they ‘neither toil nor spin,’ can yet command the produce of industry, but who owe their exemption from labour solely to civilisation and order .... They are peculiarly the creatures of civil institutions,[^4] which have recognised that individuals may acquire property by various other means besides the exertion of labour.... Persons of independent fortune ... owe their superior advantages by no means to any superior abilities of their own, but almost entirely ... to the industry of others. It is not the possession of land, or of money, but the command of labour which distinguishes the opulent from the labouring part of the community .... This [scheme approved by Eden] would give the people of property sufficient (but by no means too much) influence and authority over those who ... work for them; and it would place such labourers, not in an abject or servile condition, but in such a state of easy and liberal dependence as all who know human nature, and its history, will allow to be necessary for their own comfort.”[^5]

[^4]: Eden should have asked: whose creatures, then, are “the civil institutions”? From his standpoint of legal illusion, he does not regard the law as a product of the material relations of production, but, the other way round, the relations of production as products of the law. Linguet overthrew Montesquieu’s illusory “Esprit des lois” with one word: “ L’esprit des lois, c’est la propriété.” [The spirit of the laws is property.]

[^5]: Eden, cited above, Vol. 1, book I., chapter 1, pp. 1, 2, and preface, p. xx.

Sir F. M. Eden, it may be noted in passing, is the only follower of Adam Smith during the eighteenth century who produced any work of importance.[^6]

[^6]: If the reader reminds me of Malthus, whose “Essay on Population” appeared in 1798, I remind him that this work, in its first form, is nothing more than a schoolboyish, superficial plagiarism of De Foe, Sir James Steuart, Townsend, Franklin, Wallace, etc., and does not contain a single sentence thought out by himself. The great sensation this pamphlet caused was due solely to party interest. The French Revolution had found passionate defenders in the United Kingdom. The “principle of population” had been slowly worked out in the eighteenth century. Then, in the midst of a great social crisis, it was proclaimed with drums and trumpets as the infallible antidote to the teachings of Condorcet, etc., and the English oligarchy greeted it with jubilation as the great destroyer of all hankerings after human development. Malthus, hugely astonished at his success, set about stuffing his book with materials superficially compiled, and adding to it new matter, not discovered but annexed by him. Note further: although Malthus was a parson of the English State Church, he had taken the monastic vow of celibacy — one of the conditions of holding a Fellowship in Protestant Cambridge University: “Socios collegiorum maritos esse non permittimus, sed statim postquam quis uxorem duxerit socius collegii desinat esse.” [We do not permit fellows of colleges to be married, but as soon as anyone has taken a wife, let him cease to be a fellow of the college.] (“Reports of Cambridge University Commission,” p. 172.) This circumstance favorably distinguishes Malthus from the other Protestant parsons. They have shrugged off the command of celibacy for the priesthood, and have taken “Be fruitful and multiply” as their special Biblical mission to such a degree that they generally contribute to the increase of population to a really unbecoming extent, while at the same time they preach the “principle of population” to the workers. It is characteristic that the economic fall of man, the Adam’s apple, the urgent appetite, “the checks which tend to blunt the shafts of Cupid,” as Parson Townsend waggishly puts it — that this delicate question was and is monopolized by the Reverends of Protestant Theology, or rather of the Protestant Church. With the exception of the Venetian monk Ortes, an original and clever writer, most of the teachers of population theory are Protestant parsons. For instance, Bruckner, “Théorie du Système animal,” Leyde, 1767, in which the whole subject of the modern population theory is exhausted, and to which the passing quarrel between Quesnay and his pupil, the elder Mirabeau, supplied ideas on the same topic; then Parson Wallace, Parson Townsend, Parson Malthus and his pupil, the arch-Parson Thomas Chalmers, to say nothing of lesser reverend scribblers in this line. Originally, political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) was studied by philosophers like Hobbes, Locke, Hume; by businessmen and statesmen like Thomas More, Temple, Sully, De Witt, North, Law, Vanderlint, Cantillon, Franklin; and especially, and with the greatest success, by medical men like Petty, Barbon, Mandeville, Quesnay. Even in the middle of the eighteenth century, the Rev. Mr. Tucker, a notable economist of his time, apologized for meddling with the things of Mammon. Later on, and in truth with this very “Principle of population,” the hour of the Protestant parsons struck. Petty regarded the population as the basis of wealth, and was, like Adam Smith, an outspoken enemy of parsons. As if he had a presentiment of their bungling interference, he says “that Religion best flourishes when the Priests are most mortified [held down by self-denial], as was before said of the Law, which best flourisheth when lawyers have least to do.” So he advises the Protestant priests, if they will not, once and for all, follow the Apostle Paul and “mortify” themselves by celibacy, “not to breed more Churchmen than the Benefices [paid church posts], as they now stand shared out, will receive, that is to say, if there be places for about twelve thousand in England and Wales, it will not be safe to breed up 24,000 ministers, for then the twelve thousand which are unprovided for, will seek ways how to get themselves a livelihood, which they cannot do more easily than by persuading the people that the twelve thousand incumbents do poison or starve their souls, and misguide them in their way to Heaven.” (Petty: “A Treatise of Taxes and Contributions,” London, 1667, p. 57.) Adam Smith’s position with the Protestant priesthood of his time is shown by the following. In “A Letter to A. Smith, L.L.D. On the Life, Death, and Philosophy of his Friend, David Hume. By one of the People called Christians,” 4th Edition, Oxford, 1784, Dr. Horne, Bishop of Norwich, reproves Adam Smith: because in a published letter to Mr. Strahan he “embalmed his friend David” (namely, Hume); because he told the world how “Hume amused himself on his deathbed with Lucian and Whist”; and because he even had the impudence to write of Hume: “I have always considered him, both in his life-time and since his death, as approaching as nearly to the idea of a perfectly wise and virtuous man, as, perhaps, the nature of human frailty will permit.” The bishop cries out in a passion: “Is it right in you, Sir, to hold up to our view as ‘perfectly wise and virtuous,’ the *character* and *conduct* of one, who seems to have been possessed with an incurable antipathy to all that is called *Religion;* and who strained every nerve to explode, suppress and extirpate the spirit of it among men, that its very name, if he could effect it, might no more be had in remembrance?” (cited above, p. 8.) “But let not the lovers of truth be discouraged. Atheism cannot be of long continuance.” (P. 17.) Adam Smith, “had the atrocious wickedness to propagate atheism through the land (viz., by his “Theory of Moral Sentiments”). Upon the whole, Doctor, your meaning is good; but I think you will not succeed this time. You would persuade us, by the example of *David Hume, Esq.,* that atheism is the only cordial for low spirits, and the proper antidote against the fear of death.... You may smile over *Babylon* in ruins and congratulate the hardened *Pharaoh* on his overthrow in the Red Sea.” (cited above, pp. 21, 22.) One orthodox individual among Adam Smith’s college friends writes after his death: “Smith’s well-placed affection for Hume ... hindered him from being a Christian.... When he met with honest men whom he liked ... he would believe almost anything they said. Parson Thomas Chalmers suspects that Adam Smith invented the category of “unproductive labourers” solely for the Protestant parsons, in spite of their blessed work in the vineyard of the Lord.

Under the conditions of accumulation supposed so far, which are the conditions most favorable to the workers, their relation of dependence on capital takes on a bearable form, or, as Eden says, an “easy and liberal” one. Instead of becoming more intensive as capital grows, this relation of dependence only becomes more extensive. That is, the sphere of capital's exploitation and rule merely extends along with its own dimensions and the number of its subjects. A larger part of the workers' own surplus-product, which is always increasing and is continually transformed into additional capital, comes back to them in the shape of means of payment. So they can widen the circle of their enjoyments, make some additions to their consumption-fund of clothes, furniture, etc., and lay by small reserve funds of money. But better clothing, food, and treatment, and a larger peculium [the small property a slave was allowed to keep], do away with the exploitation of the slave no more than they set aside the exploitation of the wage worker. A rise in the price of labor as a consequence of the accumulation of capital only means, in fact, that the length and weight of the golden chain the wage worker has already forged for himself allow it to be pulled less tight. In the controversies on this subject the chief fact has generally been overlooked, namely the defining characteristic of capitalist production. Labor-power is sold today not in order to satisfy the buyer's personal needs by its service or by its product. The buyer's aim is to increase his capital: to produce commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use) that contain more labor than he pays for, and so contain a portion of value that costs him nothing and that is nevertheless realized when the commodities are sold. Production of surplus-value is the absolute law of this mode of production. Labor-power can be sold only insofar as it preserves the means of production in their capacity as capital, reproduces its own value as capital, and yields, in unpaid labor, a source of additional capital.[^7] So the conditions of its sale, whether more or less favorable to the worker, include the necessity of its constant re-selling, and the constantly extended reproduction of all wealth in the shape of capital. Wages, as we have seen, by their very nature always imply that the worker performs a certain quantity of unpaid labor. Quite apart from the case of a rise of wages with a falling price of labor, etc., such an increase means at best only a quantitative reduction of the unpaid labor that the worker has to supply. This reduction can never reach the point at which it would threaten the system itself. Apart from violent conflicts over the rate of wages (and Adam Smith has already shown that in such a conflict, taken on the whole, the master is always master), a rise in the price of labor resulting from the accumulation of capital implies the following alternative:

[^7]: “The limit, however, to the employment of both the operative and the labourer is the same; namely, the possibility of the employer realising a *profit* on the produce of their industry. If the rate of wages is such as to reduce the master’s gains below the average profit of capital, he will cease to employ them, or he will only employ them on condition of submission to a reduction of wages.” (John Wade, cited above, p. 241.)

<!-- ch25-p014 -->
Either the price of labor keeps on rising, because its rise does not interfere with the progress of accumulation. There is nothing wonderful in this, for, says Adam Smith, “after these (profits) are diminished, stock may not only continue to increase, but to increase much faster than before.... A great stock, though with small profits, generally increases faster than a small stock with great profits.” (cited above, ii, p. 189.) In this case, clearly, a reduction in the unpaid labor in no way interferes with the extension of the domain of capital. – Or, on the other hand, accumulation slackens as a result of the rise in the price of labor, because the stimulus of gain is blunted. The rate of accumulation lessens; but as it lessens, the primary cause of its lessening vanishes, namely the disproportion between capital and exploitable labor-power. The mechanism of the capitalist production process removes the very obstacles that it temporarily creates. The price of labor falls again to a level corresponding to the needs of the self-expansion of capital, whether that level is below, the same as, or above the one that was normal before the rise of wages took place. So we see: in the first case, it is not a reduced rate of increase in labor-power, or in the working population, whether absolute or proportional, that makes capital excessive; on the contrary, it is the excess of capital that makes exploitable labor-power insufficient. In the second case, it is not an increased rate of increase in labor-power, or in the working population, whether absolute or proportional, that makes capital insufficient; on the contrary, it is the relative reduction of capital that makes exploitable labor-power, or rather its price, excessive. It is these absolute movements of the accumulation of capital that are reflected as relative movements of the mass of exploitable labor-power, and that therefore seem to be produced by that mass's own independent movement. To put it mathematically: the rate of accumulation is the independent, not the dependent, variable; the rate of wages is the dependent, not the independent, variable. Thus, when the industrial cycle is in its phase of crisis, a general fall in the price of commodities is expressed as a rise in the value of money; and in its phase of prosperity, a general rise in the price of commodities is expressed as a fall in the value of money. (Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) The so-called currency school concludes from this that when prices are high too much money, and when prices are low too little[^8] money, is in circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying). Their ignorance and complete misunderstanding of the facts[^9] are worthily matched by the economists who interpret the above phenomena of accumulation by saying that there are now too few, now too many wage workers.

[^8]: Note by the Institute of Marxism-Leninism to the Russian edition: The manuscript says “little” in the first case and “much” in the second; the correction has been made following the authorized French translation.

[^9]: Compare Karl Marx: “Zur Kritik der Politischen Oekonomie,” pp. 166, and following.

The law of capitalist production that lies at the bottom of the supposed “natural law of population” comes down simply to this. The correlation between accumulation of capital and rate of wages is nothing other than the correlation between the unpaid labor transformed into capital and the additional paid labor needed to set this additional capital in motion. So it is in no way a relation between two magnitudes independent of each other: on the one side, the magnitude of the capital; on the other, the number of the working population. It is rather, at bottom, only the relation between the unpaid and the paid labor of the same working population. If the quantity of unpaid labor supplied by the working class and accumulated by the capitalist class increases so rapidly that its conversion into capital requires an extraordinary addition of paid labor, then wages rise, and, all other circumstances remaining equal, the unpaid labor shrinks in proportion. But as soon as this shrinking touches the point at which the surplus labor (the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist) that nourishes capital is no longer supplied in normal quantity, a reaction sets in: a smaller part of revenue is capitalized, accumulation lags, and the upward movement of wages receives a check. So the rise of wages is confined within limits that not only leave the foundations of the capitalist system untouched, but also secure its reproduction on an extended scale. The law of capitalist accumulation, metamorphosed by economists into a supposed law of Nature, in reality merely states that the very nature of accumulation excludes every reduction in the degree of exploitation of labor, and every rise in the price of labor, which could seriously endanger the continual reproduction, on an ever-enlarging scale, of the capitalist relation. It cannot be otherwise in a mode of production in which the worker exists to satisfy the needs of self-expansion of existing values, instead of material wealth existing, the other way round, to satisfy the worker's needs of development. As in religion man is governed by the products of his own brain, so in capitalist production he is governed by the products of his own hand.[^10]
