# Part 1: Commodities and Money

## Chapter 1: Commodities

### Section 1: The Two Factors of a Commodity: Use-Value and Value (The Substance of Value and the Magnitude of Value)

<!-- ch01-p001 -->
In societies where the capitalist mode of production prevails, wealth presents itself as “an immense accumulation of commodities,”[^1] and its unit is a single commodity. Our investigation must therefore begin with the analysis of a commodity.

[^1]: Karl Marx, “Zur Kritik der Politischen Oekonomie.” Berlin, 1859, p. 3.

<!-- ch01-p002 -->
A commodity is, first of all, an object outside us, a thing that by its properties satisfies human wants of one sort or another. The nature of these wants makes no difference, for instance whether they spring from the stomach or from fancy.[^2] Nor are we concerned here with how the object satisfies these wants: whether directly, as means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), or indirectly, as means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things).

[^2]: “Desire implies want, it is the appetite of the mind, and as natural as hunger to the body... The greatest number (of things) have their value from supplying the wants of the mind.” Nicholas Barbon: “A Discourse Concerning Coining the New Money Lighter. In Answer to Mr. Locke’s Considerations, &c.”, London, 1696, pp. 2, 3.

<!-- ch01-p003 -->
Every useful thing, such as iron, paper, etc., may be looked at from two points of view: quality and quantity. Each is a collection of many properties, and so it may be useful in various ways. Discovering the various uses of things is the work of history.[^3] So is setting up socially recognized standards of measure for the quantities of these useful objects. These measures differ partly because the objects to be measured differ in nature, and partly because of convention.

[^3]: “Things have an intrinsick vertue” (this is Barbon’s special term for use-value) “which in all places have the same vertue; as the loadstone [magnet] to attract iron” (cited above, p. 6). The magnet’s property of attracting iron became useful only after the polarity of the magnet had been discovered by means of that property.

<!-- ch01-p004 -->
The usefulness of a thing makes it a use-value.[^4] But this usefulness is not something made of air. It is limited by the physical properties of the commodity, and it does not exist apart from that commodity. A commodity, such as iron, grain, or a diamond, is therefore, so far as it is a material thing, a use-value, something useful. This property of a commodity does not depend on how much labor is needed to make its useful qualities our own. When dealing with use-values, we always assume that we are dealing with definite quantities, such as dozens of watches, yards of linen, or tons of iron. The use-values of commodities provide the material for a special field of study, the trade knowledge of commodities.[^5] Use-values become real only by being used or consumed. They also make up the substance of all wealth, whatever the social form of that wealth may be. In the form of society we are about to examine, they are in addition the material bearers of exchange-value.

[^4]: “The natural worth of anything consists in its fitness to supply the necessities, or serve the conveniencies of human life.” (John Locke, “Some Considerations on the Consequences of the Lowering of Interest, 1691,” in Works Edit. London, 1777, Vol. II., p. 28.) In English writers of the 17th century we often find “worth” used in the sense of use-value, and “value” in the sense of exchange-value. This fits the spirit of a language that likes to use a Germanic word for the actual thing and a Romance word for its reflection.

[^5]: In bourgeois societies (societies shaped by the capitalist class and its way of thinking) the economic *fictio juris* [legal fiction] prevails that everyone, as a buyer, has an encyclopedic knowledge of commodities.

<!-- ch01-p005 -->
At first sight, exchange-value presents itself as a quantitative relation: the proportion in which use-values of one sort are exchanged for use-values of another sort,[^6] a relation that constantly changes with time and place. So exchange-value appears to be something accidental and purely relative. Consequently an intrinsic value, that is, an exchange-value that is inseparably connected with commodities and built into them, seems a contradiction in terms.[^7] Let us look at the matter a little more closely.

[^6]: “La valeur consiste dans le rapport d’échange qui se trouve entre telle chose et telle autre entre telle mesure d’une production et telle mesure d’une autre.” [“Value consists in the exchange relation between one thing and another, between a given amount of one product and a given amount of another”] (Le Trosne: “De l’Intérêt Social.” Physiocrates, Ed. Daire. Paris, 1846. p. 889.)

[^7]: “Nothing can have an intrinsick value.” (N. Barbon, cited above, p. 6); or as Butler says – “For what is worth in any thing, / But so much money as ’twill bring?” (from *Hudibras*).

<!-- ch01-p006 -->
A given commodity, for example a quarter of wheat, is exchanged for x blacking, y silk, or z gold, etc. In short, it is exchanged for other commodities in the most varied proportions. So the wheat has not one exchange-value but a great many. But x blacking, y silk, z gold, etc., each represents the exchange-value of one quarter of wheat. So x blacking, y silk, z gold, etc., must, as exchange-values, be able to replace each other, or be equal to each other. It follows, first, that the valid exchange-values of a given commodity express something equal. Second, exchange-value in general is only the way of expressing, the form of appearance of, something contained in it, yet distinguishable from it.

<!-- ch01-p007 -->
Let us take two commodities, for example grain and iron. Whatever the proportions in which they can be exchanged, those proportions can always be represented by an equation in which a given quantity of grain is set equal to some quantity of iron: for example, 1 quarter corn = x cwt. iron. What does this equation tell us? It tells us that in two different things, in 1 quarter of grain and in x cwt. of iron, there exists something common to both, in equal quantities. The two things must therefore be equal to a third thing, which in itself is neither the one nor the other. So each of them, so far as it is an exchange-value, must be reducible to this third thing.

<!-- ch01-p008 -->
A simple example from geometry will make this clear. To calculate and compare the areas of straight-sided figures, we break them down into triangles. But the area of the triangle itself is expressed by something totally different from its visible shape, namely by half the base multiplied by the height. In the same way, the exchange-values of commodities must be capable of being expressed in terms of something common to them all, of which they represent a greater or smaller quantity.

<!-- ch01-p009 -->
This common “something” cannot be a geometrical, a chemical, or any other natural property of commodities. Such properties concern us only to the extent that they affect the usefulness of those commodities, that is, make them use-values. But the exchange of commodities is clearly an act marked by a total abstraction from use-value: use-value is left out of account entirely. Then one use-value is just as good as another, provided only that there is enough of it. Or, as old Barbon says,

<!-- ch01-p010 -->
> “one sort of wares are as good as another, if the values be equal. There is no difference or distinction in things of equal value ... An hundred pounds’ worth of lead or iron, is of as great value as one hundred pounds’ worth of silver or gold.”[^8]

[^8]: N. Barbon, cited above, p. 53 and 7.

<!-- ch01-p011 -->
As use-values, commodities are above all of different qualities. But as exchange-values they are merely different quantities, and so they do not contain an atom of use-value.

<!-- ch01-p012 -->
If, then, we leave the use-value of commodities out of consideration, they have only one common property left: they are products of labor. But even the product of labor itself has been changed in our hands. If we set aside its use-value, we also set aside the material elements and shapes that make the product a use-value. We no longer see in it a table, a house, yarn, or any other useful thing. Its existence as a material thing is put out of sight. Nor can it any longer be regarded as the product of the labor of the joiner, the mason, the spinner, or any other definite kind of productive labor. Along with the useful qualities of the products themselves, we put out of sight both the useful character of the various kinds of labor embodied in them and the concrete forms of that labor. Nothing is left but what is common to them all. All are reduced to one and the same sort of labor, abstract human labor.

<!-- ch01-p013 -->
Let us now look at what remains of each of these products. In each it is the same insubstantial reality: a mere congealed mass of uniform human labor, of labor-power (a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything) spent without regard to the way it was spent. All that these things now tell us is that human labor-power has been spent in producing them, that human labor is embodied in them. Looked at as crystals of this social substance, which is common to them all, they are – Values.

<!-- ch01-p014 -->
We have seen that when commodities are exchanged, their exchange-value shows itself as something totally independent of their use-value. But if we abstract from their use-value, what remains is their Value as defined above. Therefore the common substance that shows itself in the exchange-value of commodities, whenever they are exchanged, is their value. As our investigation goes on, it will show that exchange-value is the only form in which the value of commodities can show itself or be expressed. For the present, however, we have to consider the nature of value independently of this, its form.

<!-- ch01-p015 -->
A use-value, or useful article, therefore has value only because abstract human labor has been embodied or materialized in it. How, then, is the magnitude of this value to be measured? Plainly, by the quantity of the value-creating substance, the labor, contained in the article. The quantity of labor, however, is measured by its duration, and labor-time in turn is measured in weeks, days, and hours.

<!-- ch01-p016 -->
Some people might think that if the value of a commodity is determined by the quantity of labor spent on it, then the lazier and less skillful the worker, the more valuable his commodity would be, because he would need more time to produce it. The labor, however, that forms the substance of value is uniform human labor, the spending of one and the same labor-power. The total labor-power of society, which is embodied in the sum total of the values of all the commodities that society produces, counts here as one uniform mass of human labor-power, even though it is made up of countless individual units. Each of these units is the same as any other, so far as it has the character of the average labor-power of society and works as such; that is, so far as it needs no more time to produce a commodity than is needed on average, no more than is socially necessary. The labor-time socially necessary is the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production, and with the average degree of skill and intensity usual at the time. In England the introduction of power-looms probably cut by one-half the labor needed to weave a given quantity of yarn into cloth. In fact, the hand-loom weavers went on needing the same time as before. But even so, after the change the product of one hour of their labor represented only half an hour’s social labor, and so it fell to one-half of its former value.

<!-- ch01-p017 -->
We see, then, that what determines the magnitude of the value of any article is the amount of labor socially necessary, or the socially necessary labor-time, for its production.[^9] In this connection each individual commodity is to be considered as an average sample of its kind.[^10] Commodities in which equal quantities of labor are embodied, or which can be produced in the same time, therefore have the same value. The value of one commodity is to the value of any other as the labor-time necessary to produce the one is to the labor-time necessary to produce the other. “As values, all commodities are only definite masses of congealed labour time.”[^11]

[^9]: “The value of them (the necessaries of life), when they are exchanged the one for another, is regulated by the quantity of labour necessarily required, and commonly taken in producing them.” (“Some Thoughts on the Interest of Money in General, and Particularly in the Publick Funds, &c.” London, p. 36) This remarkable anonymous work, written in the last century, bears no date. It is clear, however, from evidence inside the work that it appeared in the reign of George II, about 1739 or 1740.

[^10]: “Toutes les productions d’un même genre ne forment proprement qu’une masse, dont le prix se détermine en général et sans égard aux circonstances particulières.” [“Properly speaking, all products of the same kind form only one mass, whose price is determined in general and without regard to particular circumstances”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 893.)

[^11]: K. Marx. cited above, p.6

<!-- ch01-p018 -->
The value of a commodity would therefore stay constant if the labor-time required to produce it also stayed constant. But that labor-time changes with every change in the productivity of labor (how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time). This productivity is determined by various circumstances, among others: the average skill of the workers; the state of science and how far it is applied in practice; the social organization of production; the extent and capabilities of the means of production; and physical conditions. For example, the same amount of labor is embodied in 8 bushels of grain in good seasons, and in only four in bad ones. The same labor extracts more metal from rich mines than from poor ones. Diamonds are very rarely found on the earth’s surface, and so finding them costs, on average, a great deal of labor-time. Consequently much labor is represented in a small space. Jacob doubts whether gold has ever been paid for at its full value. This applies still more to diamonds. According to Eschwege, the total output of the Brazilian diamond mines for the eighty years ending in 1823 had not brought in the price of one-and-a-half years’ average output of the sugar and coffee plantations of the same country, although the diamonds cost much more labor and therefore represented more value. With richer mines, the same quantity of labor would embody itself in more diamonds, and their value would fall. If we could manage to turn carbon into diamonds with a small expenditure of labor, their value might fall below that of bricks. In general, the greater the productivity of labor, the less labor-time is needed to produce an article, the less labor is crystallized in that article, and the less its value. And the other way round: the less the productivity of labor, the more labor-time is needed to produce an article, and the greater its value. So the value of a commodity varies directly as the quantity, and inversely as the productivity, of the labor embodied in it.[^*1]

[^*1]: The following passage appeared only in the first edition. “Now we know the substance of value. It is labour. We know the measure of its magnitude. It is labour time. The form, which stamps value as exchange-value, remains to be analysed. But before this we need to develop the characteristics we have already found somewhat more fully.” Taken from the Penguin edition of “Capital,” translated by Ben Fowkes.

<!-- ch01-p019 -->
A thing can be a use-value without having value. This is the case whenever its usefulness to people is not due to labor. Examples are air, virgin soil, natural meadows, etc. A thing can be useful, and a product of human labor, without being a commodity. Whoever directly satisfies his wants with the product of his own labor does create use-values, but not commodities. To produce commodities, he must produce not only use-values, but use-values for others, social use-values. (And not simply for others, with nothing more. The medieval peasant produced quit-rent grain for his feudal lord and tithe grain for his parson. But neither the quit-rent grain nor the tithe grain became commodities just because they had been produced for others. To become a commodity, a product must be transferred by means of an exchange to someone else, for whom it will serve as a use-value.)[^12] Lastly, nothing can have value without being an object of utility. If the thing is useless, so is the labor contained in it; the labor does not count as labor, and so it creates no value.

[^12]: I am inserting the parenthesis because leaving it out has often led to the misunderstanding that, in Marx, every product consumed by someone other than its producer counts as a commodity. [Engels, 4th German Edition]
