# Part 6: Wages

## Chapter 19: The Transformation of the Value (and Respective Price) of Labor-Power into Wages

<!-- ch19-p001 -->
On the surface of bourgeois society (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it), the worker's wage appears as the price of labor: a certain amount of money paid for a certain amount of labor. So people speak of the value of labor, and they call its expression in money its necessary or natural price. (A price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) On the other hand, they speak of the market prices of labor, that is, prices that swing above or below its natural price.

<!-- ch19-p002 -->
But what is the value of a commodity? It is the objective form of the social labor spent in producing it. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use.) And how do we measure the size of this value? By the amount of labor it contains. How, then, is the value of, for example, a 12 hour working day to be fixed? By the 12 working hours contained in a working day of 12 hours. That is an absurd tautology.[^1] (The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor.)

[^1]: “Mr. Ricardo ingeniously enough avoids a difficulty which, on a first view, threatens to encumber his doctrine — that value depends on the quantity of labour employed in production. If this principle is rigidly adhered to, it follows that the value of labour depends on the quantity of labour employed in producing it — which is evidently absurd. By a dexterous turn, therefore, Mr. Ricardo makes the value of labour depend on the quantity of labour required to produce wages; or, to give him the benefit of his own language, he maintains, that the value of labour is to be estimated by the quantity of labour required to produce wages; by which he means the quantity of labour required to produce the money or commodities given to the labourer. This is similar to saying, that the value of cloth is estimated, not by the quantity of labour bestowed on its production, but by the quantity of labour bestowed on the production of the silver, for which the cloth is exchanged.” — “A Critical Dissertation on the Nature, &c., of Value,” pp. 50, 51.

<!-- ch19-p003 -->
To be sold as a commodity on the market, labor must in any case exist before it is sold. But if the worker could give it an independent, objective existence, he would be selling a commodity and not labor.[^2]

[^2]: “If you call labour a commodity, it is not like a commodity which is first produced in order to exchange, and then brought to market where it must exchange with other commodities according to the respective quantities of each which there may be in the market at the time; labour is created the moment it is brought to market; nay, it is brought to market before it is created.” — “Observations on Certain Verbal Disputes,” etc., pp. 75, 76.

<!-- ch19-p004 -->
Apart from these contradictions, a direct exchange of money, that is, of realized labor, for living labor (living labor: the labor of workers now at work) would do one of two things. Either it would abolish the law of value, which only begins to develop freely on the basis of capitalist production, or it would abolish capitalist production itself, which rests directly on wage labor. For example, the working day of 12 hours is embodied in a money-value of 6s. Either equivalents are exchanged, and then the worker receives 6s. for 12 hours' labor; the price of his labor would be equal to the price of his product. In this case he produces no surplus-value for the buyer of his labor. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) The 6s. are not turned into capital (capital: value that is used to make more value, money that is advanced in order to come back larger), and the basis of capitalist production vanishes. But it is on this very basis that he sells his labor, and that his labor is wage labor. Or else he receives less than 6s. for 12 hours' labor, that is, less than 12 hours' labor. Twelve hours' labor is exchanged for 10, 6, etc., hours' labor. This equating of unequal amounts does more than just abolish the way value is fixed. A self-destroying contradiction of this kind cannot in any way even be stated or formulated as a law.[^3]

[^3]: “Treating labour as a commodity, and capital, the produce of labour, as another, then, if the values of these two commodities were regulated by equal quantities of labour, a given amount of labour would ... exchange for that quantity of capital which had been produced by the same amount of labour; antecedent labour would ... exchange for the same amount as present labour. But the value of labour in relation to other commodities ... is determined not by equal quantities of labour.” — E. G. Wakefield in his edition of Adam Smith’s “Wealth of Nations,” Vol. I., London, 1836, p. 231, note.

<!-- ch19-p005 -->
It is no use trying to derive the exchange of more labor for less from their difference in form, the one being realized and the other living.[^4] This is all the more absurd because the value of a commodity is fixed not by the amount of labor actually realized in it, but by the amount of living labor necessary to produce it. Say a commodity represents 6 working hours. If an invention is made by which it can be produced in 3 hours, the value even of the commodity already produced falls by half. It now represents 3 hours of social labor instead of the 6 formerly necessary. It is the amount of labor required to produce a commodity, not the realized form of that labor, that fixes the amount of its value.

[^4]: “There has to be a new agreement” (a new edition of the social contract!) “that whenever there is an exchange of work done for work to be done, the latter” (the capitalist) “is to receive a higher value than the former” (the worker). — Simonde (de Sismondi), “De la Richesse Commerciale,” Geneva, 1803, Vol I, p. 37.

<!-- ch19-p006 -->
What comes directly face to face with the owner of money on the market is in fact not labor, but the worker. What the worker sells is his labor-power (a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything). As soon as his labor actually begins, it has already stopped belonging to him, so he can no longer sell it. Labor is the substance and the built-in measure of value, but it has no value itself.[^5]

[^5]: “Labour the exclusive standard of value ... the creator of all wealth, no commodity.” Thomas Hodgskin, “Popul. Polit. Econ.,” p. 186.

<!-- ch19-p007 -->
In the expression “value of labor,” the idea of value is not only completely wiped out, but actually turned upside down. It is an expression as imaginary as the value of the earth. These imaginary expressions, however, arise from the relations of production themselves. They are categories for the forms of appearance of essential relations. That things often present themselves upside down in their appearance is fairly well known in every science except political economy (the science of economics as it was then called).[^6]

[^6]: On the other hand, trying to explain such expressions as mere poetic license only shows how powerless the analysis is. That is why, in answer to Proudhon's phrase, “Labour is called value, not as being a commodity itself, but in view of the values supposed to be potentially embodied in it. The value of labour is a figurative expression,” etc., I remarked: “In labour, commodity, which is a frightful reality, he (Proudhon) sees nothing but a grammatical ellipsis. The whole of existing society, then, based upon labour commodity, is henceforth based upon a poetic license, on a figurative expression. Does society desire to eliminate all the inconveniences which trouble it, it has only to eliminate all the ill-sounding terms. Let it change the language, and for that it has only to address itself to the Academy and ask it for a new edition of its dictionary.” (Karl Marx, “Misère de la Philosophie,” pp. 34, 35.) It is, naturally, even more convenient to understand nothing at all by value. Then one can easily fit everything under this category. So, for example, J. B. Say: “What is value?” Answer: “That which a thing is worth”; and what is “price”? Answer: “The value of a thing expressed in money.” And why does agriculture have a value? Answer: “Because one sets a price on it.” So value is what a thing is worth, and the land has its “value” because its value is “expressed in money.” This is, anyhow, a very simple way of explaining the why and the wherefore of things.

<!-- ch19-p008 -->
Classical political economy (the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production) borrowed the category “price of labor” from everyday life without further criticism, and then simply asked: how is this price determined? It soon recognized that changes in the relation of demand and supply explained nothing about the price of labor, as about the price of all other commodities, except its changes, that is, the swings of the market price above or below a certain average. If demand and supply balance, then, all other conditions remaining the same, prices stop swinging. But then demand and supply also stop explaining anything. At the moment when demand and supply are in balance, the price of labor is its natural price, determined independently of the relation of demand and supply. And how this price is determined is precisely the question. Or else a longer period of swings in the market price is taken, for example a year, and the swings are found to cancel each other out, leaving a mean average quantity, a relatively constant magnitude. This, naturally, had to be determined by something other than its own variations, which cancel each other out. This price, which always wins out in the end over the accidental market prices of labor and regulates them, this “necessary price” (the Physiocrats) or “natural price” of labor (Adam Smith) can, as with all other commodities, be nothing else than its value expressed in money. In this way political economy expected to break through the accidental prices of labor to the value of labor. As with other commodities, this value was then determined by the cost of production. But what is the cost of production of the worker, that is, the cost of producing or reproducing the worker himself? Without noticing it, political economy put this question in place of the original one, because the search for the cost of production of labor as such went round in a circle and never got anywhere. So what economists call the value of labor is in fact the value of labor-power, as it exists in the worker's person, and labor-power is as different from its function, labor, as a machine is from the work it performs. (The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work.) Busy with the difference between the market price of labor and its so-called value, with the relation of this value to the rate of profit and to the values of the commodities produced by means of labor, etc., the economists never discovered that the course of the analysis had led not only from the market prices of labor to its presumed value, but had led to this value of labor itself being broken down into the value of labor-power. Classical economics never became aware of the results of its own analysis. It accepted the categories “value of labor,” “natural price of labor,” etc., uncritically, as final and adequate expressions for the value-relation under consideration. So, as will be seen later, it was led into hopeless confusion and contradiction, while it gave the vulgar economists (economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations) a secure base of operations for their shallowness, which on principle worships appearances only.

<!-- ch19-p009 -->
Let us next see how the value (and price) of labor-power present themselves in this transformed condition, as wages.

<!-- ch19-p010 -->
We know that the daily value of labor-power is calculated on a certain length of the worker's life, and that a certain length of working day, in turn, corresponds to this. Assume that the usual working day is 12 hours, and the daily value of labor-power is 3s., the expression in money of a value that embodies 6 hours of labor. If the worker receives 3s., then he receives the value of his labor-power functioning through 12 hours. Now, if this value of a day's labor-power is expressed as the value of a day's labor itself, we get the formula: Twelve hours' labor has a value of 3s. In this way the value of labor-power determines the value of labor, or, expressed in money, its necessary price. If, on the other hand, the price of labor-power differs from its value, then in the same way the price of labor differs from its so-called value.

<!-- ch19-p011 -->
Since the value of labor is only an irrational expression for the value of labor-power, it follows, of course, that the value of labor must always be less than the value it produces. For the capitalist always makes labor-power work longer than is necessary to reproduce its own value. In the example above, the value of the labor-power that functions through 12 hours is 3s., a value that takes 6 hours to reproduce. The value that the labor-power produces, on the other hand, is 6s., because it in fact functions for 12 hours, and the value it produces depends not on its own value but on the length of time it is in action. So we get a result that seems absurd at first sight: labor that creates a value of 6s. has a value of 3s.[^7]

[^7]: Compare “Zur Kritik &c.,” p. 40, where I state that this problem will be solved in the part of that work that deals with Capital: “How does production, on the basis of exchange-value [the proportion in which one commodity trades for others] determined simply by labour-time, lead to the result that the exchange-value of labour is less than the exchange-value of its product?”

<!-- ch19-p012 -->
We see, further: the value of 3s., which pays for only part of the working day, that is, 6 hours' labor, appears as the value or price of the whole working day of 12 hours, which therefore includes 6 hours that are not paid for. So the wage form wipes out every trace of the division of the working day into necessary labor and surplus labor, into paid and unpaid labor. (Necessary labor is the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages. Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.) All labor appears as paid labor. Under the corvée (forced unpaid labor that peasants had to perform for a lord), the worker's labor for himself and his forced labor for his lord are separated in space and time in the clearest possible way. In slave labor, even the part of the working day in which the slave is only replacing the value of his own means of existence, in which he therefore in fact works for himself alone, appears as labor for his master. All the slave's labor appears as unpaid labor.[^8] In wage labor, on the contrary, even surplus labor, or unpaid labor, appears as paid. There, the property relation hides the slave's labor for himself; here, the money relation hides the unrewarded labor of the wage worker.

[^8]: The “Morning Star,” a London Free-trade paper, naive to the point of silliness, protested again and again during the American Civil War, with all the moral indignation man is capable of, that the Black man in the “Confederate States” worked absolutely for nothing. It should have compared the daily cost of such a Black man with that of the free worker in the East End of London.

<!-- ch19-p013 -->
Hence we can understand the decisive importance of the transformation of the value and price of labor-power into the form of wages, or into the value and price of labor itself. This form of appearance makes the actual relation invisible, and indeed shows the direct opposite of that relation. It forms the basis of all the legal notions of both worker and capitalist, of all the mystifications of the capitalist mode of production, of all its illusions about freedom, and of all the apologetic dodges of the vulgar economists.

<!-- ch19-p014 -->
If history took a long time to get to the bottom of the mystery of wages, nothing, on the other hand, is easier to understand than the necessity, the reason for the existence, of this phenomenon.

<!-- ch19-p015 -->
At first, the exchange between capital and labor presents itself to the mind in the same guise as the buying and selling of all other commodities. The buyer gives a certain sum of money, the seller an article of a nature different from money. The legal mind sees in this, at most, a material difference, expressed in the legally equivalent formula: “Do ut des, do ut facias, facio ut des, facio ut facias.”[^9]

[^9]: I give so that you may give; I give so that you may do; I do so that you may give; I do so that you may do.

<!-- ch19-p016 -->
Furthermore, since exchange-value and use-value are in their very nature magnitudes that cannot be measured against each other, the expressions “value of labor” and “price of labor” do not seem more irrational than the expressions “value of cotton” and “price of cotton.” (Exchange-value is the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for. Use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties.) Moreover, the worker is paid after he has given his labor. In its function as means of payment (money used to settle a debt after goods have changed hands on credit), money realizes afterwards the value or price of the article supplied, that is, in this particular case, the value or price of the labor supplied. Finally, the use-value that the worker supplies to the capitalist is not in fact his labor-power, but its function, some definite useful labor, the work of tailoring, shoemaking, spinning, etc. (Useful labor is labor of a particular kind, such as tailoring or weaving, that makes a particular useful thing.) That this same labor is, on the other hand, the universal value-creating element, and so has a property by which it differs from all other commodities, is beyond the grasp of the ordinary mind.

<!-- ch19-p017 -->
Let us put ourselves in the place of the worker who receives, for 12 hours' labor, say, the value produced by 6 hours' labor, say 3s. For him, in fact, his 12 hours' labor is the means of buying the 3s. The value of his labor-power may vary with the value of his usual means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), from 3 to 4 shillings, or from 3 to 2 shillings. Or, if the value of his labor-power stays constant, its price may rise to 4s. or fall to 2s., as a result of changing relations of demand and supply. He always gives 12 hours of labor. So every change in the amount of the equivalent that he receives necessarily appears to him as a change in the value or price of his 12 hours' work. This circumstance misled Adam Smith, who treated the working day as a constant quantity,[^10] into asserting that the value of labor is constant, although the value of the means of subsistence may vary, and the same working day may therefore represent itself in more or less money for the worker.

[^10]: Adam Smith only incidentally mentions the variation of the working day, when he is referring to piece wages (wages paid per piece of work done).

<!-- ch19-p018 -->
Let us consider, on the other hand, the capitalist. He wants to receive as much labor as possible for as little money as possible. In practice, therefore, the only thing that interests him is the difference between the price of labor-power and the value that its function creates. But then, he tries to buy all commodities as cheaply as possible, and always explains his profit by simple cheating, by buying below value and selling above it. Hence he never comes to see that if such a thing as the value of labor really existed, and he really paid this value, no capital would exist: his money would not be turned into capital.

<!-- ch19-p019 -->
Moreover, the actual movement of wages shows phenomena which seem to prove that what is paid is not the value of labor-power, but the value of its function, of labor itself. We may reduce these phenomena to two great classes: 1.) Change of wages as the length of the working day changes. One might as well conclude that what is paid is not the value of a machine but the value of its working, because it costs more to hire a machine for a week than for a day. 2.) The individual difference in the wages of different workers who do the same kind of work. We find this individual difference, but are not deceived by it, in the system of slavery, where labor-power itself is sold frankly and openly, without any beating about the bush. Only, in the slave system, the advantage of a labor-power above the average, and the disadvantage of a labor-power below the average, affects the slave-owner; in the wage labor system, it affects the worker himself, because his labor-power is, in the one case, sold by himself, in the other, by a third person.

<!-- ch19-p020 -->
For the rest, between the form of appearance, “value and price of labor,” or “wages,” and the essential relation that shows itself in it, namely the value and price of labor-power, the same difference holds that holds between all phenomena and their hidden basis. The forms of appearance arise directly and spontaneously as current ways of thinking; the hidden basis must first be discovered by science. Classical political economy almost touches the true relation of things, but without consciously formulating it. It cannot do this as long as it sticks in its bourgeois skin.
