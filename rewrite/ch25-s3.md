### Section 3: Progressive Production of a Relative surplus population or Industrial Reserve Army

<!-- ch25-p033 -->
The accumulation of capital at first appears to be only its growth in quantity. But, as we have seen, it takes place through a progressive qualitative change in its composition, through a constant increase of its constant part at the expense of its variable part.[^13]

[^13]: *Note in the 3rd German edition.* — In Marx’s copy there is a marginal note here: “Here note for working out later; if the extension is only quantitative, then for a greater and a smaller capital in the same branch of business the profits are as the magnitudes of the capitals advanced. If the quantitative extension induces qualitative change, then the rate of profit on the larger capital rises simultaneously.” *F. E.*

<!-- ch25-p034 -->
The specifically capitalist mode of production, the development of the productive power of labor that corresponds to it, and the change in the organic composition of capital that results from it do not merely keep pace with the advance of accumulation, or with the growth of social wealth. They develop much faster. This is because mere accumulation, the absolute increase of the total social capital, is accompanied by the centralization of the individual capitals that make up that total. It is also because the change in the technological composition of the additional capital goes hand in hand with a similar change in the technological composition of the original capital. So, as accumulation advances, the proportion of constant to variable capital changes. If it was originally, say, 1:1, it now becomes successively 2:1, 3:1, 4:1, 5:1, 7:1, etc. As a result, as the capital grows, instead of ½ of its total value, only 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, 1/6, 1/8, etc., is turned into labor-power, and, on the other hand, 2/3, 3/4, 4/5, 5/6, 7/8 into means of production. The demand for labor is determined not by the amount of capital as a whole, but by its variable part alone. So that demand falls progressively as the total capital increases, instead of rising in proportion to it, as was previously assumed. It falls relative to the magnitude of the total capital, and at an accelerating rate as this magnitude increases. As the total capital grows, its variable part, or the labor incorporated in it, does increase too, but in a constantly diminishing proportion. The pauses in between, in which accumulation works as a simple extension of production on a given technical basis, become shorter. It is not merely that an accelerated accumulation of the total capital, accelerated in a constantly growing progression, is needed to absorb an additional number of workers, or even, because of the constant metamorphosis of old capital, to keep employed those already at work. In its turn, this growing accumulation and centralization itself becomes a source of new changes in the composition of capital, of a still faster decrease of its variable part compared with its constant part. This accelerated relative decrease of the variable part goes along with the accelerated increase of the total capital, and moves faster than this increase. At the other pole, it takes the opposite form: an apparently absolute increase of the working population, an increase always moving faster than that of the variable capital or the means of employment. But in fact it is capitalist accumulation itself that constantly produces a relatively redundant population of workers, and produces it in direct proportion to its own energy and extent. That is, it produces a population larger than suffices for the average needs of the self-expansion of capital, and therefore a surplus population.

<!-- ch25-p035 -->
If we consider the social capital in its totality, the movement of its accumulation at one time causes periodic changes that affect it more or less as a whole, and at another time distributes its various phases simultaneously over the different spheres of production. In some spheres, a change in the composition of capital takes place without any increase in its absolute magnitude, as a result of simple centralization. In others, the absolute growth of capital is connected with an absolute decrease of its variable part, or of the labor-power it absorbs. In still others, capital goes on growing for a time on its given technical basis, and attracts additional labor-power in proportion to its growth, while at other times it undergoes organic change and reduces its variable part. In all spheres, the increase of the variable part of capital, and therefore of the number of workers it employs, is always connected with violent fluctuations and with the temporary production of surplus population. This may take the more striking form of the repulsion, or pushing out, of workers already employed. Or it may take the less obvious, but no less real, form of the greater difficulty of absorbing the additional working population through the usual channels.[^14] With the magnitude of the social capital already functioning and the degree of its increase, with the extension of the scale of production and the mass of workers set in motion, with the development of the productivity of their labor, and with the greater breadth and fullness of all sources of wealth, there is also an extension of the scale on which greater attraction of workers by capital is accompanied by their greater repulsion. The speed of the change in the organic composition of capital, and in its technical form, increases, and a growing number of spheres of production are drawn into this change, sometimes at the same time, sometimes by turns. So the working population, along with the accumulation of capital that it produces, also produces the means by which it is itself made relatively superfluous, turned into a relative surplus population. And it does this to an ever-increasing extent.[^15] This is a law of population peculiar to the capitalist mode of production. In fact, every particular historical mode of production has its own particular laws of population, historically valid within its limits, and only insofar as man has not interfered with them.

But if a surplus working population is a necessary product of accumulation, or of the development of wealth on a capitalist basis, this surplus population in turn becomes the lever of capitalist accumulation, indeed even a condition of existence of the capitalist mode of production. It forms a disposable industrial reserve army, which belongs to capital just as absolutely as if capital had bred it at its own cost. Independently of the limits of the actual increase of population, it creates, for the changing needs of the self-expansion of capital, a mass of human material always ready for exploitation. With accumulation, and the development of the productivity of labor that goes with it, capital's power of sudden expansion grows too. It grows not merely because the elasticity of the capital already functioning increases; not merely because the absolute wealth of society expands, of which capital forms only an elastic part; not merely because credit, under every special stimulus, at once places an unusual part of this wealth at the disposal of production in the form of additional capital. It grows also because the technical conditions of the process of production themselves – machinery, means of transport, etc. – now allow masses of surplus-product to be turned into additional means of production at the greatest speed. The mass of social wealth, overflowing as accumulation advances and able to be turned into additional capital, throws itself frantically into old branches of production whose market suddenly expands, or into newly formed branches, such as railways, etc., the need for which grows out of the development of the old ones. In all such cases, it must be possible to throw great masses of men suddenly onto the decisive points without harming the scale of production in other spheres. Overpopulation supplies these masses. The course characteristic of modern industry, namely a ten-year cycle (interrupted by smaller oscillations) of periods of average activity, production at high pressure, crisis, and stagnation, depends on the constant formation, the greater or lesser absorption, and the re-formation of the industrial reserve army or surplus population. In their turn, the changing phases of the industrial cycle recruit the surplus population, and become one of the most energetic agents of its reproduction. This peculiar course of modern industry, which occurs in no earlier period of human history, was also impossible in the childhood of capitalist production. The composition of capital changed only very slowly. So, on the whole, its accumulation was matched by a corresponding growth in the demand for labor. Slow as the advance of accumulation was compared with that of more modern times, it ran up against the natural limits of the exploitable working population, limits that could be got rid of only by forcible means, which will be mentioned later. The expansion of the scale of production by fits and starts is the prelude to its equally sudden contraction. The contraction in turn calls forth the expansion, but the expansion is impossible without disposable human material, without an increase in the number of workers independent of the absolute growth of the population. This increase is brought about by the simple process that constantly “sets free” a part of the workers, by methods that reduce the number of workers employed in proportion to the increased production. The whole form of the movement of modern industry therefore depends on the constant transformation of a part of the working population into unemployed or half-employed workers. The superficiality of political economy shows itself in the fact that it looks on the expansion and contraction of credit, which is a mere symptom of the periodic changes of the industrial cycle, as their cause. Just as the heavenly bodies, once thrown into a certain definite motion, always repeat it, so it is with social production once it has been thrown into this movement of alternate expansion and contraction. Effects in their turn become causes, and the changing accidents of the whole process, which always reproduces its own conditions, take on the form of periodicity. Once this periodicity has become fixed, even political economy sees that the production of a relative surplus population – that is, surplus with regard to the average needs of the self-expansion of capital – is a necessary condition of modern industry.

Even Malthus recognizes overpopulation as a necessity of modern industry, although, in his narrow way, he explains it by the absolute overgrowth of the working population, not by its becoming relatively more than is needed. He says:

<!-- ch25-p042 -->
Capitalist production can by no means be content with the quantity of disposable labor-power that the natural increase of population yields. For its free play it requires an industrial reserve army independent of these natural limits.

<!-- ch25-p043 -->
Up to this point we have assumed that the increase or decrease of the variable capital corresponds rigidly to the increase or decrease of the number of workers employed.

<!-- ch25-p044 -->
The number of workers commanded by capital may stay the same, or even fall, while the variable capital increases. This is the case if the individual worker supplies more labor, and his wages therefore increase, and this even though the price of labor stays the same or even falls, only more slowly than the mass of labor rises. In this case, an increase of variable capital becomes an index of more labor, but not of more workers employed. It is the absolute interest of every capitalist to press a given quantity of labor out of a smaller rather than a greater number of workers, if the cost is about the same. With the greater number, the outlay of constant capital increases in proportion to the mass of labor set in motion; with the smaller number, that increase is much smaller. The larger the scale of production, the stronger this motive. Its force increases with the accumulation of capital.

<!-- ch25-p045 -->
We have seen that the development of the capitalist mode of production and of the productive power of labor – at once the cause and the effect of accumulation – enables the capitalist, with the same outlay of variable capital, to set more labor in motion by greater exploitation (extensive or intensive) of each individual labor-power. We have also seen that the capitalist buys a greater mass of labor-power with the same capital as he progressively replaces skilled workers by less skilled ones, mature labor-power by immature, male by female, and that of adults by that of young persons or children.

<!-- ch25-p046 -->
So, on the one hand, as accumulation progresses, a larger variable capital sets more labor in motion without enlisting more workers. On the other hand, a variable capital of the same magnitude sets more labor in motion with the same mass of labor-power. And finally, it sets in motion a greater number of inferior labor-powers by displacing higher ones.

<!-- ch25-p047 -->
The production of a relative surplus population, or the setting free of workers, therefore goes on even faster than the technical revolution of the process of production, which accompanies the advance of accumulation and is accelerated by it. It also goes on faster than the corresponding decrease of the variable part of capital compared with the constant part. If the means of production, as they grow in extent and effective power, become means of employment for workers to a lesser extent, this state of things is in turn modified by the fact that, as the productivity of labor increases, capital increases its supply of labor faster than its demand for workers. The overwork of the employed part of the working class swells the ranks of the reserve, while, the other way round, the greater pressure that the reserve exerts on the employed through its competition forces them to submit to overwork and to subjection under the dictates of capital. Condemning one part of the working class to enforced idleness through the overwork of the other part, and the other way round, becomes a means of enriching the individual capitalists,[^19] and at the same time speeds up the production of the industrial reserve army on a scale that corresponds to the advance of social accumulation. How important this element is in the formation of the relative surplus population is shown by the example of England. Her technical means for saving labor are colossal. Nevertheless, if tomorrow morning labor in general were reduced to a rational amount, and adjusted to the different sections of the working class according to age and sex, the working population at hand would be absolutely insufficient to carry on national production on its present scale. The great majority of the workers who are now “unproductive” would have to be turned into “productive” ones.

[^19]: Even during the cotton famine of 1863, we find fierce denunciations of overwork in a pamphlet by the cotton-spinning factory workers of Blackburn. Because of the Factory Acts, this overwork of course affected only adult male workers. “The adult operatives at this mill have been asked to work from 12 to 13 hours per day, while there are hundreds who are compelled to be idle who would willingly work partial time, in order to maintain their families and save their brethren from a premature grave through being overworked.... We,” the pamphlet goes on to say, “would ask if the practice of working overtime by a number of hands, is likely to create a good feeling between masters and servants. Those who are worked overtime feel the injustice equally with those who are condemned to forced idleness. The author of the “Essay on Trade and Commerce” grasps the effect of a relative surplus population on the employed workers with his usual unerring bourgeois instinct (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it). “Another cause of idleness in this kingdom is the want of a sufficient number of labouring hands .... Whenever from an extraordinary demand for manufactures, labour grows scarce, the labourers feel their own consequence [importance], and will make their masters feel it likewise — it is amazing; but so depraved are the dispositions of these people, that in such cases a set of workmen have combined to distress the employer by idling a whole day together.” (“Essay, &c.,” pp. 27, 28.) The fellows were in fact hankering after a rise in wages.

Taken as a whole, the general movements of wages are regulated exclusively by the expansion and contraction of the industrial reserve army, and these in turn correspond to the periodic changes of the industrial cycle. So these movements are not determined by the variations in the absolute number of the working population, but by the changing proportions in which the working class is divided into active army and reserve army, by the increase or decrease in the relative amount of the surplus population, and by the extent to which it is at one time absorbed, at another set free. Modern industry has its ten-year cycles and periodic phases, and as accumulation advances these are, moreover, complicated by irregular oscillations that follow each other more and more quickly. For such an industry, that would indeed be a beautiful law which pretends to make the action of capital depend on the absolute variation of the population, instead of regulating the demand and supply of labor by the alternate expansion and contraction of capital, with the labor-market now appearing relatively under-full because capital is expanding, now again over-full because it is contracting. Yet this is the dogma of the economists. According to them, wages rise as a result of the accumulation of capital. The higher wages stimulate the working population to multiply faster, and this goes on until the labor-market becomes too full, and capital therefore becomes insufficient relative to the supply of labor. Wages fall, and now we have the other side of the medal. As a result of the fall in wages, the working population is little by little decimated, so that capital is again in excess relative to them. Or, as others explain it, falling wages and the corresponding increase in the exploitation of the worker speed up accumulation again, while at the same time the lower wages hold the growth of the working class in check. Then the time comes again when the supply of labor is less than the demand, wages rise, and so on. A beautiful mode of motion, this, for developed capitalist production! Before any positive increase of the population really fit for work could occur as a result of the rise in wages, the time during which the industrial campaign had to be carried through, the battle fought and won, would have passed again and again.

<!-- ch25-p049 -->
Between 1849 and 1859, a rise of wages that was practically insignificant, though accompanied by falling prices of grain, took place in the English agricultural districts. In Wiltshire, for example, weekly wages rose from 7s. to 8s.; in Dorsetshire, from 7s. or 8s. to 9s., etc. This was the result of an unusual exodus of the agricultural surplus population, caused by the demands of war, the vast extension of railroads, factories, mines, etc. The lower the wages, the higher the proportion in which even the most insignificant rise in them expresses itself. If the weekly wage, for example, is 20s. and it rises to 22s., that is a rise of 10 percent; but if it is only 7s. and it rises to 9s., that is a rise of 28 4/7 per cent., which sounds very fine. Everywhere the farmers were howling, and the London *Economist,* referring to these starvation wages, prattled quite seriously about “a general and substantial advance.”[^20] What did the farmers do now? Did they wait until, as a result of this brilliant remuneration, the agricultural workers had so increased and multiplied that their wages had to fall again, as prescribed by the dogmatic economic brain? They introduced more machinery, and in a moment the workers were redundant again, in a proportion satisfactory even to the farmers. There was now “more capital” laid out in agriculture than before, and in a more productive form. With this, the demand for labor fell, not only relatively, but absolutely.

[^20]: *Economist,* Jan. 21. 1860.

<!-- ch25-p050 -->
The economic fiction described above confuses the laws that regulate the general movement of wages, or the ratio between the working class – that is, the total labor-power – and the total social capital, with the laws that distribute the working population over the different spheres of production. Suppose, for example, that favorable circumstances make accumulation especially active in a particular sphere of production, and that profits there, being greater than average profits, attract additional capital. Then, of course, the demand for labor rises and wages rise too. The higher wages draw a larger part of the working population into the more favored sphere, until it is glutted with labor-power, and wages at length fall again to their average level, or below it if the pressure is too great. Then the immigration of workers into the branch of industry in question not only stops; it gives way to their emigration. Here the political economist thinks he sees the why and wherefore of an absolute increase of workers accompanying a rise in wages, and of a fall in wages accompanying an absolute increase of workers. But what he really sees is only the local oscillation of the labor-market in a particular sphere of production. He sees only the phenomena that accompany the distribution of the working population among the different spheres where capital is laid out, according to its changing needs.

<!-- ch25-p051 -->
During periods of stagnation and average prosperity, the industrial reserve army weighs down the active labor-army; during periods of over-production and frenzy, it holds the active army's claims in check. Relative surplus population is therefore the pivot on which the law of demand and supply of labor works. It confines this law's field of action within limits that suit the activity of exploitation and the domination of capital absolutely.

<!-- ch25-p052 -->
This is the place to return to one of the grand exploits of economic apologetics, the defense of the system. It will be remembered that if, through the introduction of new machinery or the extension of old, a portion of variable capital is turned into constant capital, the economic apologist interprets this operation, which “fixes” capital and by that very act sets workers “free,” in exactly the opposite way: he pretends that it sets capital free for the workers. Only now can one fully understand the effrontery of these apologists. What are set free are not only the workers immediately turned out by the machines, but also their future replacements in the rising generation, and the additional contingent that the usual extension of trade on the old basis would regularly absorb. They are now all “set free,” and every new bit of capital looking for employment can dispose of them. Whether it attracts them or others, the effect on the general demand for labor will be nil, if this capital is just enough to take out of the market as many workers as the machines threw onto it. If it employs a smaller number, the number of surplus workers increases; if it employs a greater number, the general demand for labor increases only by the excess of those employed over those “set free.” So the impulse that additional capital seeking an outlet would otherwise have given to the general demand for labor is in every case neutralized to the extent of the workers thrown out of work by the machine. That is to say, the mechanism of capitalist production so manages matters that the absolute increase of capital is accompanied by no corresponding rise in the general demand for labor. And this the apologist calls a compensation for the misery, the suffering, the possible death of the displaced workers during the transition period that banishes them into the industrial reserve army! The demand for labor is not identical with the increase of capital, nor the supply of labor with the increase of the working class. It is not a case of two independent forces working on each other. Les dés sont pipés [The dice are loaded].

<!-- ch25-p053 -->
Capital works on both sides at the same time. If its accumulation, on the one hand, increases the demand for labor, on the other it increases the supply of workers by “setting free” some of them. At the same time, the pressure of the unemployed forces those who are employed to furnish more labor, and so makes the supply of labor, to a certain extent, independent of the supply of workers. The action of the law of supply and demand of labor on this basis completes the despotism of capital. So, as soon as the workers learn the secret of how it comes about that, in the same measure as they work more, produce more wealth for others, and as the productive power of their labor increases, in that same measure even their function as a means of the self-expansion of capital becomes more and more precarious for them; as soon as they discover that the degree of intensity of the competition among themselves depends wholly on the pressure of the relative surplus population; as soon as they try, through Trades’ Unions, etc., to organize regular cooperation between employed and unemployed, in order to destroy or weaken the ruinous effects of this natural law of capitalist production on their class – just as soon, capital and its sycophant, political economy, cry out at the violation of the “eternal” and, so to speak, “sacred” law of supply and demand. Every combination of employed and unemployed disturbs the “harmonious” action of this law. But on the other hand, as soon as adverse circumstances (in the colonies, for example) prevent the creation of an industrial reserve army, and with it the absolute dependence of the working class on the capitalist class, capital, along with its commonplace Sancho Panza (the down-to-earth squire of Don Quixote), rebels against the “sacred” law of supply and demand, and tries to check its inconvenient action by forcible means and State interference.
