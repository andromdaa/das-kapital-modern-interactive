### Section 3: Money

<!-- ch03-p071 -->
The commodity that functions as a measure of value, and also, either in its own person or through a representative, as the medium of circulation, is money. Gold (or silver) is therefore money. On the one hand, it functions as money when it has to be present in its own golden person. It is then the money-commodity. It is neither merely ideal, as in its function as a measure of value, nor able to be represented by something else, as in its function as the medium of circulation. On the other hand, it also functions as money when, by virtue of its function, whether that function is performed in person or by a representative, it solidifies into the sole form of value. It then becomes the only adequate form of existence of exchange-value, in opposition to use-value, represented by all other commodities.

#### A. Hoarding

<!-- ch03-p072 -->
The two opposed metamorphoses of commodities move continually in circuits: sale and purchase alternate without end. This is reflected in the restless currency of money, or in the function money performs as a *perpetuum mobile* [perpetual motion machine] of circulation. But as soon as the series of metamorphoses is interrupted, as soon as sales are not followed up by later purchases, money stops being mobile. It is transformed, as Boisguillebert says, from “meuble” into “immeuble,” from movable into immovable, from coin into money.

<!-- ch03-p073 -->
As soon as the circulation of commodities first develops, there also develops the need, and the passionate desire, to hold on to the product of the first metamorphosis. This product is the commodity in its transformed shape, or its gold-chrysalis.[^39] So commodities are now sold not in order to buy others, but in order to replace their commodity-form by their money-form. This change of form was merely the means of carrying out the circulation of commodities; now it becomes the end and aim. The changed form of the commodity is thus prevented from functioning as the form in which it can be parted with unconditionally, or as its merely passing money-form. The money turns to stone as a hoard, and the seller becomes a hoarder of money.

[^39]: “Une richesse en argent n’est que ... richesse en productions, converties en argent.” [“Wealth in money is nothing but ... wealth in products, changed into money.”] (Mercier de la Rivière, cited above.) “Une valeur en productions n’a fait que changer de forme.” [“A value in products has only changed its form.”] (Id., p. 486.)

<!-- ch03-p074 -->
In the early stages of the circulation of commodities, only the surplus use-values are turned into money. Gold and silver thus become, of themselves, the social expressions of superfluity, or wealth. This naïve form of hoarding becomes permanent in those communities where the traditional mode of production is carried on to supply a fixed and limited circle of home needs. This is the case with the people of Asia, and particularly of the East Indies. Vanderlint imagines that the prices of commodities in a country are determined by the quantity of gold and silver to be found in it, and he asks himself why Indian commodities are so cheap. Answer: Because the Hindus bury their money. From 1602 to 1734, he remarks, they buried 150 millions of pounds sterling of silver, which had originally come from America to Europe.[^40] In the 10 years from 1856 to 1866, England exported £120,000,000 in silver to India and China, silver it had received in exchange for Australian gold. Most of the silver exported to China makes its way to India.

[^40]: “’Tis by this practice’ they keep all their goods and manufactures at such low rates.” (Vanderlint, cited above, pp. 95, 96.)

<!-- ch03-p075 -->
As the production of commodities develops further, every producer of commodities is forced to make sure of the nexus rerum [the bond of things], or the social pledge.[^41] His needs constantly make themselves felt and require him to keep buying other people’s commodities, while the production and sale of his own goods take time and depend on circumstances. So in order to be able to buy without selling, he must previously have sold without buying. This operation, carried out on a general scale, appears to imply a contradiction. But at the sources where they are produced, the precious metals are exchanged directly for other commodities. And here we have sales (by the owners of commodities) without purchases (by the owners of gold or silver).[^42] And later sales by other producers, not followed by purchases, merely bring about the distribution of the newly produced precious metals among all the owners of commodities. In this way, hoards of gold and silver of varying size are accumulated all along the line of exchange. With the possibility of holding and storing up exchange-value in the shape of a particular commodity, there also arises the greed for gold. As circulation extends, the power of money increases, money being that absolutely social form of wealth that is always ready for use. “Gold is a wonderful thing! Whoever possesses it is lord of all he wants. By means of gold one can even get souls into Paradise.” (Columbus in his letter from Jamaica, 1503.) Since gold does not reveal what has been transformed into it, everything, commodity or not, can be converted into gold. Everything becomes salable and buyable. Circulation becomes the great social retort (a chemist’s distilling vessel) into which everything is thrown, to come out again as a gold-crystal. Not even the bones of saints can withstand this alchemy, and still less can more delicate res sacrosanctae, extra commercium hominum [sacred things, outside the commerce of men].[^43] Just as every qualitative difference between commodities is wiped out in money, so money, for its part, being the radical leveler that it is, does away with all distinctions.[^43a] But money itself is a commodity, an external object that can become the private property of any individual. Thus social power becomes the private power of private persons. The ancients therefore denounced money as subversive of the economic and moral order of things.[^43b] Modern society, soon after its birth, pulled Plutus (the Greek god of wealth) by the hair of his head out of the bowels of the earth.[^44] It greets gold as its Holy Grail, as the glittering incarnation of the very principle of its own life.

[^42]: A purchase, in a “categorical” sense, implies that gold and silver are already the converted form of commodities, or the product of a sale.

[^43]: Henry III, most Christian king of France, robbed cloisters of their relics and turned them into money. They were “sacred banks.” For the Phoenicians, a trading people above all others, money was the transformed shape of everything. So it was quite in order that the virgins who gave themselves up to strangers at the feast of the Goddess of Love should offer the goddess the piece of money they received.

A commodity, in its capacity as a use-value, satisfies a particular need and is a particular element of material wealth. But the value of a commodity measures how strongly it attracts all other elements of material wealth, and therefore measures the social wealth of its owner. To a barbarian owner of commodities, and even to a West-European peasant, value is the same as the form of value. So to him, an increase in his hoard of gold and silver is an increase in value. It is true that the value of money varies, at one time because its own value changes, at another because the values of commodities change. But on the one hand, this does not stop 200 ounces of gold from still containing more value than 100 ounces. On the other hand, it does not stop the actual metallic form of this article from remaining the universal equivalent form of all other commodities, and the immediate social incarnation of all human labor. The urge to hoard is by its very nature insatiable. In its qualitative aspect, or considered as to its form, money has no limits to its power; that is, it is the universal representative of material wealth, because it can be converted directly into any other commodity. But at the same time, every actual sum of money is limited in amount, and so, as a means of purchase, has only limited power. This antagonism between the quantitative limits of money and its qualitative boundlessness keeps spurring the hoarder on in his Sisyphus-like labor of accumulating. It is with him as with a conqueror who sees in every new country he annexes only a new frontier.

<!-- ch03-p077 -->
For gold to be held as money and made to form a hoard, it must be prevented from circulating, or from turning itself into a means of enjoyment. The hoarder therefore sacrifices the lusts of the flesh to his gold fetish. He lives up to the Gospel of abstention in earnest. On the other hand, he can withdraw from circulation no more than he has thrown into it in the shape of commodities. The more he produces, the more he can sell. Hard work, saving, and avarice are therefore his three cardinal virtues, and to sell much and buy little is the sum of his political economy.[^45]

[^45]: “Accrescere quanto più si può il numero de’venditori d’ogni merce, diminuere quanto più si puo il numero dei compratori, questi sono i cardini sui quali si raggirano tutte le operazioni di economia politica.” [“To increase as much as one can the number of sellers of every commodity, to reduce as much as one can the number of buyers: these are the hinges on which all the operations of political economy turn.”] (Verri, cited above, p. 52.)

<!-- ch03-p078 -->
Alongside the crude form of the hoard, we also find its aesthetic form, the possession of gold and silver articles. This grows with the wealth of civil society. “Soyons riches ou paraissons riches” [“Let us be rich or appear rich”] (Diderot).

<!-- ch03-p079 -->
In this way there is created, on the one hand, a constantly expanding market for gold and silver, unconnected with their functions as money. On the other hand, there is created a dormant source of supply, which is drawn on mainly in times of crisis and social upheaval.

<!-- ch03-p080 -->
Hoarding serves various purposes in the economy of metallic circulation. Its first function arises out of the conditions that govern the currency of gold and silver coins. We have seen how the quantity of money in circulation constantly ebbs and flows, along with the continual fluctuations in the extent and speed of the circulation of commodities and in their prices. This mass must therefore be able to expand and contract. At one time money must be drawn in to act as circulating coin; at another, circulating coin must be pushed out to act again as more or less stagnant money. The mass of money actually in circulation must always fill the absorbing capacity of circulation to the full. For this, the quantity of gold and silver in a country must be greater than the quantity needed to function as coin. This condition is met by money taking the form of hoards. These reserves serve as channels for supplying money to circulation or withdrawing it from circulation, which in this way never overflows its banks.[^46]

[^46]: “There is required for carrying on the trade of the nation a determinate sum of specifick money which varies, and is sometimes more, sometimes less, as the circumstances we are in require.... This ebbing and flowing of money supplies and accommodates itself, without any aid of Politicians.... The buckets work alternately; when money is scarce, bullion is coined; when bullion is scarce, money is melted.” (Sir D. North, cited above, Postscript, p. 3.) John Stuart Mill, who was for a long time an official of the East India Company, confirms the fact that in India silver ornaments still directly perform the functions of a hoard. The silver ornaments are brought out and coined when the rate of interest is high, and go back again when the rate of interest falls. (J. S. Mill’s Evidence “Reports on Bank Acts,” 1857, 2084.) According to a Parliamentary document of 1864 on the import and export of gold and silver in India, the import of gold and silver in 1863 exceeded the export by £19,367,764. During the 8 years just before 1864, imports of the precious metals exceeded exports by £109,652,917. During this century far more than £200,000,000 has been coined in India.

#### B. Means of Payment

<!-- ch03-p081 -->
In the simple form of the circulation of commodities that we have considered so far, a given value always appeared to us in a double shape: as a commodity at one pole, and as money at the opposite pole. So the owners of commodities came into contact as the representatives of things that were already equivalents. But as circulation develops, conditions arise under which the handing over of commodities is separated by an interval of time from the realization of their prices. It will be enough to point out the simplest of these conditions. One sort of article takes a longer time to produce, another a shorter time. Again, the production of different commodities depends on different seasons of the year. One sort of commodity may be born in its own market place; another has to make a long journey to market. So commodity-owner No. 1 may be ready to sell before No. 2 is ready to buy. When the same transactions are continually repeated between the same persons, the terms of sale are regulated to match the conditions of production. On the other hand, the use of a given commodity, of a house, for instance, is sold (in everyday language, let) for a definite period. Here the buyer has actually received the use-value of the commodity only at the end of the term. So he buys it before he pays for it. The seller sells an existing commodity; the buyer buys as the mere representative of money, or rather of future money. The seller becomes a creditor, the buyer becomes a debtor. Since the metamorphosis of commodities, or the development of their form of value, appears here in a new aspect, money also takes on a new function: it becomes the means of payment.

<!-- ch03-p082 -->
Here the roles of creditor and debtor arise out of simple circulation. The change in the form of that circulation stamps buyer and seller with this new die. At first, therefore, these new roles are just as passing and changeable as those of seller and buyer, and the same actors play them in turn. But this opposition is not nearly so pleasant, and it is far more capable of crystallizing.[^47] The same roles can, however, also arise independently of the circulation of commodities. The class struggles of the ancient world took the form chiefly of a contest between debtors and creditors. In Rome this ended in the ruin of the plebeian debtors. They were replaced by slaves. In the middle ages the contest ended with the ruin of the feudal debtors, who lost their political power together with the economic basis on which it rested. Nevertheless, the money relation of debtor and creditor that existed in these two periods only reflected the deeper-lying antagonism between the general economic conditions of existence of the classes in question.

[^47]: The following shows the debtor and creditor relations among English traders at the beginning of the 18th century. “Such a spirit of crudity reigns here in England among the men of trade, that is not to be met with in any other society of men, nor in any other kingdom of the world.” (“An Essay on Credit and the Bankrupt Act,” London, 1707, p. 2.)

<!-- ch03-p083 -->
Let us return to the circulation of commodities. The two equivalents, commodities and money, no longer appear at the same time at the two poles of the sale. Money now functions, first, as a measure of value in fixing the price of the commodity sold. The price fixed by the contract measures the debtor’s obligation, that is, the sum of money he has to pay on a fixed date. Secondly, money serves as a means of purchase that exists only ideally. Although it exists only in the buyer’s promise to pay, it makes the commodity change hands. Not until the day fixed for payment does the means of payment actually step into circulation and leave the buyer’s hand for the seller’s. The medium of circulation was turned into a hoard because the process stopped short after the first phase, because the converted shape of the commodity, namely the money, was withdrawn from circulation. The means of payment enters circulation, but only after the commodity has left it. Money is no longer the means that brings the process about. It only brings it to a close, by stepping in as the absolute form of existence of exchange-value, or as the universal commodity. The seller turned his commodity into money in order to satisfy some need with it; the hoarder did the same in order to keep his commodity in its money-shape; and the debtor did it in order to be able to pay. If he does not pay, the sheriff will sell his goods. So the form of value of commodities, money, is now the end and aim of a sale, and this owing to a social necessity springing out of the process of circulation itself.

<!-- ch03-p084 -->
The buyer turns money back into commodities before he has turned commodities into money. In other words, he carries out the second metamorphosis of commodities before the first. The seller’s commodity circulates and realizes its price, but only in the shape of a legal claim to money. It is turned into a use-value before it has been turned into money. Its first metamorphosis is completed only later.[^48]

[^48]: The following quotation from my book that appeared in 1859 will show why I take no notice in the text of an opposite form: “Contrariwise, in the process in M—C, the money can be alienated as a real means of purchase, and in that way, the price of the commodity can be realised before the use-value of the money is realised and the commodity actually delivered. This occurs constantly under the every-day form of prepayments. And it is under this form, that the English government purchases opium from the ryots [peasant farmers] of India.... In these cases, however, the money always acts as a means of purchase.... Of course capital also is advanced in the shape of money.... This point of view, however, does not fall within the horizon of simple circulation.” (“Zur Kritik, *&c*.,” pp. 119, 120.)

<!-- ch03-p085 -->
The obligations falling due within a given period represent the sum of the prices of the commodities whose sale gave rise to those obligations. The quantity of gold needed to realize this sum depends, in the first place, on the rapidity of currency of the means of payment. That quantity is conditioned by two circumstances. The first is that the relations between debtors and creditors form a sort of chain, so that A, when he receives money from his debtor B, at once hands it over to C, his creditor, and so on. The second circumstance is the length of the intervals between the different due dates of the obligations. The continuous chain of payments, or of delayed first metamorphoses, is essentially different from the interlacing of the series of metamorphoses that we considered earlier. The currency of the medium of circulation does not merely express the connection between buyers and sellers. This connection arises in circulation, and exists in it alone. By contrast, the movement of the means of payment expresses a social relation that was in existence long before.

<!-- ch03-p086 -->
The fact that a number of sales take place at the same time and side by side limits how far the rapidity of currency can make up for coin. On the other hand, this fact is a new lever for economizing on the means of payment. In proportion as payments are concentrated in one place, special institutions and methods develop for settling them. Such in the middle ages were the *virements* [transfers] at Lyons. The debts owed to A by B, to B by C, to C by A, and so on, only have to be set against each other in order to cancel each other out to a certain extent, like positive and negative quantities. Only a single balance then remains to be paid. The greater the amount of payments concentrated, the smaller this balance is relative to that amount, and the smaller is the mass of the means of payment in circulation.

<!-- ch03-p087 -->
The function of money as the means of payment implies a contradiction without a terminus medius [middle term]. To the extent that the payments balance one another, money functions only ideally, as money of account, as a measure of value. To the extent that actual payments have to be made, money does not serve as a medium of circulation, as a mere passing agent in the exchange of products. It serves as the individual incarnation of social labor, as the independent form of existence of exchange-value, as the universal commodity. This contradiction comes to a head in those phases of industrial and commercial crises that are known as monetary crises.[^49] Such a crisis occurs only where the ever-lengthening chain of payments, and an artificial system for settling them, have been fully developed. Whenever there is a general and extensive disturbance of this mechanism, no matter what its cause, money is suddenly and immediately transformed from its merely ideal shape of money of account into hard cash. Profane commodities can no longer take its place. The use-value of commodities becomes valueless, and their value vanishes in the presence of its own independent form. On the eve of the crisis, the bourgeois, with the self-satisfaction that springs from intoxicating prosperity, declares money to be an empty fancy: commodities alone are money. But now the cry is everywhere: money alone is a commodity! As the hart pants after fresh water, so his soul pants after money, the only wealth.[^50] In a crisis, the opposition between commodities and their form of value, money, is heightened into an absolute contradiction. Hence, in such events, the form in which money appears does not matter. The money famine continues, whether payments have to be made in gold or in credit money such as bank-notes.[^51]

If we now consider the total sum of money in circulation during a given period, we find the following. Given the rapidity of currency of the medium of circulation and of the means of payment, it is equal to the sum of the prices to be realized, plus the sum of the payments falling due, minus the payments that balance each other, minus finally the number of circuits in which the same piece of coin serves in turn as medium of circulation and as means of payment. Hence, even when prices, the rapidity of currency, and the extent of the economizing on payments are given, the quantity of money in circulation and the mass of commodities circulating during a given period, such as a day, no longer correspond. Money continues to circulate that represents commodities withdrawn from circulation long ago. Commodities circulate whose equivalent in money will not appear on the scene until some future day. Moreover, the debts contracted each day and the payments falling due on the same day are quantities that have no common measure at all.[^52]

[^52]: “The amount of purchases or contracts entered upon during the course of any given day, will not affect the quantity of money afloat on that particular day, but, in the vast majority of cases, will resolve themselves into multifarious drafts upon the quantity of money which may be afloat at subsequent dates more or less distant.... The bills granted or credits opened, to-day, need have no resemblance whatever, either in quantity, amount or duration, to those granted or entered upon to-morrow or next day, nay, many of today’s bills, and credits, when due, fall in with a mass of liabilities whose origins traverse a range of antecedent dates altogether indefinite, bills at 12, 6, 3 months or 1 often aggregating together to swell the common liabilities of one particular day....” (“The Currency Theory Reviewed; in a Letter to the Scottish People.” By a Banker in England. Edinburgh, 1845, pp. 29, 30 passim.)

Credit-money springs directly out of the function of money as a means of payment. Certificates of the debts owed for purchased commodities circulate in order to transfer those debts to others. On the other hand, the function of money as a means of payment extends to the same degree as the credit system extends. In that role it takes on various forms of its own, in which it makes itself at home in the sphere of large commercial transactions. Gold and silver coin, on the other hand, are mostly pushed off into the sphere of retail trade.[^53]

When the production of commodities has extended far enough, money begins to serve as the means of payment beyond the sphere of the circulation of commodities. It becomes the commodity that is the universal subject-matter of all contracts.[^54] Rents, taxes, and similar payments are turned from payments in kind into money payments. How far this transformation depends on the general conditions of production is shown, to take one example, by the fact that the Roman Empire twice failed in its attempt to levy all contributions in money. The unspeakable misery of the French farming population under Louis XIV, a misery so eloquently denounced by Boisguillebert, Marshal Vauban, and others, was due not only to the weight of the taxes, but also to the conversion of taxes in kind into money taxes.[^55] In Asia, on the other hand, state taxes consist chiefly of rents payable in kind, and this fact depends on conditions of production that are reproduced with the regularity of natural phenomena. And this mode of payment tends in its turn to maintain the ancient form of production. It is one of the secrets of the survival of the Ottoman Empire. If the foreign trade forced upon Japan by Europeans should lead to money rents replacing rents in kind, it will be all up with the model agriculture of that country. The narrow economic conditions under which that agriculture is carried on will be swept away.

In every country, certain days of the year become, by habit, recognized settling days for various large payments that recur. Apart from other turns in the wheel of reproduction, these dates depend on conditions closely connected with the seasons. They also govern the dates for payments that have no direct connection with the circulation of commodities, such as taxes, rents, and so on. The quantity of money needed for the payments falling due on those dates all over the country causes periodic, though merely superficial, disturbances in the economy of the medium of payment.[^56]

[^56]: “On Whitsuntide [Pentecost], 1824,” says Mr. Craig before the Commons’ Committee of 1826, “there was such an immense demand for notes upon the banks of Edinburgh, that by 11 o’clock they had not a note left in their custody. They sent round to all the different banks to borrow, but could not get them, and many of the transactions were adjusted by slips of paper only; yet by three o’clock the whole of the notes were returned into the banks from which they had issued! It was a mere transfer from hand to hand. “Although the average effective circulation of bank-notes in Scotland is less than three millions sterling, yet on certain pay days in the year, every single note in the possession of the bankers, amounting in the whole to about £7,000,000, is called into activity. (See John Fullarton, “Regulation of Currencies.” Lond. 1845, p. 86, note.) By way of explanation, it should be said that in Scotland, at the time of Fullarton’s work, deposits were withdrawn with notes, not with checks.

As money develops into a medium of payment, it becomes necessary to accumulate money against the dates fixed for paying the sums owed. Hoarding, as a distinct way of getting rich, vanishes as civil society progresses, while the formation of reserves of the means of payment grows with that progress.
