## Chapter 14: Division of Labor and Manufacture

### Section 1: Two-Fold Origin of Manufacture

<!-- ch14-p001 -->
The kind of cooperation that is based on division of labor takes its typical form in manufacture. (Cooperation means many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist's command. Division of labor, here, means the splitting of the making of one product into many separate tasks, each done by a specialized worker. Manufacture means the stage and form of capitalist production based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) Throughout the manufacturing period properly so called, this cooperation is the prevailing, characteristic form of the capitalist process of production. That period, roughly speaking, runs from the middle of the 16th to the last third of the 18th century.

<!-- ch14-p002 -->
Manufacture arises in two ways:

<!-- ch14-p003 -->
(1.) By bringing together, in one workshop under the control of a single capitalist, workers who belong to various independent handicrafts, but through whose hands a given article must pass on its way to completion. A carriage, for example, used to be the product of the labor of a great number of independent craftsmen, such as wheelwrights, harness-makers, tailors, locksmiths, upholsterers, turners, fringe-makers, glaziers, painters, polishers, gilders, etc. In the manufacture of carriages, however, all these different craftsmen are brought together in one building, where they work into one another's hands. It is true that a carriage cannot be gilded before it has been made. But if a number of carriages are being made at the same time, some may be in the hands of the gilders while others are going through an earlier process. So far, we are still in the domain of simple cooperation, which finds its materials ready to hand in the shape of men and things. But very soon an important change takes place. The tailor, the locksmith, and the other craftsmen are now occupied only in carriage-making, and each of them gradually loses, through lack of practice, the ability to carry on his old handicraft to its full extent. But, on the other hand, his activity, now confined to one groove, takes the form best suited to its narrowed sphere of action. At first, carriage manufacture is a combination of various independent handicrafts. Little by little, it becomes the splitting up of carriage-making into its various detail processes. Each of these processes crystallizes into the exclusive function of a particular worker, while the manufacture as a whole is carried on by these men together. In the same way, cloth manufacture, and a whole series of other branches of manufacture too, arose by combining different handicrafts under the control of a single capitalist.[^1]

[^1]: To give a more modern example: The silk spinning and weaving of Lyon and Nîmes “est toute patriarcale; elle emploie beaucoup de femmes et d’enfants, mais sans les épuiser ni les corrompre; elle les laisse dans leur belles valises de la Drôme, du Var, de l’Isère, de Vaucluse, pour y élever des vers et dévider leurs cocons; jamais elle n’entre dans une véritable fabrique. <!-- ch14-p004 -->
(2.) Manufacture also arises in a way that is exactly the reverse of this ‒ namely, when one capitalist employs at the same time, in one workshop, a number of craftsmen who all do the same work, or the same kind of work, such as making paper, type, or needles. This is cooperation in its most elementary form. Each of these craftsmen (with the help, perhaps, of one or two apprentices) makes the entire commodity, and so he performs, one after another, all the operations needed to produce it. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use.) He still works in his old handicraft way. But very soon external circumstances cause a different use to be made of the concentration of the workers on one spot, and of the fact that they work at the same time. Perhaps an increased quantity of the article has to be delivered within a given time. The work is therefore redistributed. Instead of each man being allowed to perform all the various operations one after another, these operations are changed into disconnected, isolated ones, carried on side by side. Each is assigned to a different craftsman, and all of them together are performed at the same time by the cooperating workers. This chance division of the work gets repeated, develops advantages of its own, and gradually hardens into a systematic division of labor. The commodity, from being the individual product of an independent craftsman, becomes the social product of a union of craftsmen, each of whom performs one, and only one, of the partial operations that make it up. In the case of a papermaker belonging to a German Guild, certain operations merged one into the other as the successive acts of one craftsman. In the Dutch paper manufacture, the same operations became so many partial operations carried on side by side by numerous cooperating workers. The needlemaker of the Nuremberg Guild was the cornerstone on which the English needle manufacture was built. But in Nuremberg that single craftsman performed a series of perhaps 20 operations one after another, while in England it was not long before there were 20 needlemakers side by side, each performing just one of those 20 operations. And as a result of further experience, each of those 20 operations was split up again, isolated, and made the exclusive function of a separate worker.

<!-- ch14-p005 -->
So the way in which manufacture arises, its growth out of handicrafts, is two-fold. On the one hand, it arises from the union of various independent handicrafts. These are stripped of their independence and specialized to such an extent that they are reduced to mere supplementary partial processes in the production of one particular commodity. On the other hand, it arises from the cooperation of craftsmen of one handicraft. It splits that particular handicraft up into its various detail operations, isolating them and making them independent of one another, up to the point where each becomes the exclusive function of a particular worker. On the one hand, therefore, manufacture either introduces division of labor into a process of production, or develops that division further; on the other hand, it unites handicrafts that were formerly separate. But whatever its particular starting-point may have been, its final form is always the same ‒ a productive mechanism whose parts are human beings.

<!-- ch14-p006 -->
To understand the division of labor in manufacture properly, it is essential to grasp the following points firmly. First, here the breaking down of a process of production into its various successive steps coincides strictly with the breaking down of a handicraft into its successive manual operations. Whether complex or simple, each operation has to be done by hand. It keeps the character of a handicraft, and so it depends on the strength, skill, quickness, and sureness of the individual worker in handling his tools. The handicraft remains the basis. This narrow technical basis rules out a really scientific analysis of any definite process of industrial production, since it is still a condition that each detail process the product goes through must be capable of being done by hand and of forming, in its way, a separate handicraft. It is just because handicraft skill remains, in this way, the foundation of the process of production, that each worker is assigned exclusively to a partial function, and that for the rest of his life his labor-power is turned into the organ of this detail function. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.)

<!-- ch14-p007 -->
Secondly, this division of labor is a particular sort of cooperation, and many of its disadvantages spring from the general character of cooperation, and not from this particular form of it.
