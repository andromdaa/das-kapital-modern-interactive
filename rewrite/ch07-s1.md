# Part 3: The Production of Absolute Surplus-Value

## Chapter 7: The Labor-Process and the Process of Producing Surplus-Value

### Section 1: The Labor-Process or the Production of Use-Values

<!-- ch07-p001 -->
The capitalist buys labor-power in order to use it, and labor-power in use is labor itself. (Labor-power is a person’s capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) The buyer of labor-power consumes it by setting its seller to work. By working, the seller actually becomes what before he was only potentially: labor-power in action, a worker. For his labor to reappear in a commodity (a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use), he must, before all else, spend it on something useful, on something able to satisfy a want of some sort. So what the capitalist sets the worker to produce is a particular use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties), a specific article. The production of use-values, or goods, is carried on under the control of a capitalist and on his behalf; but this fact does not change the general character of that production. So we must first consider the labor process apart from the particular form it takes under given social conditions.

<!-- ch07-p002 -->
Labor is, first of all, a process in which both man and Nature take part, and in which man, of his own accord, starts, regulates, and controls the material reactions between himself and Nature. He confronts Nature as one of her own forces. He sets in motion arms and legs, head and hands, the natural forces of his body, in order to make Nature’s products his own in a form suited to his wants. By acting on the external world in this way and changing it, he at the same time changes his own nature. He develops his sleeping powers and forces them to act under his command. We are not now dealing with those primitive, instinctive forms of labor that remind us of mere animals. An immeasurable stretch of time separates the state of things in which a man brings his labor-power to market for sale as a commodity from the state in which human labor was still in its first instinctive stage. We assume labor in a form that marks it as exclusively human. A spider carries out operations that resemble those of a weaver, and a bee puts many an architect to shame in building her cells. But what distinguishes the worst architect from the best of bees is this: the architect raises his structure in imagination before he builds it in reality. At the end of every labor process we get a result that already existed in the worker’s imagination when it began. He not only changes the form of the material he works on; he also realizes a purpose of his own. That purpose lays down the law for his way of working, and he must subordinate his will to it. And this subordination is no mere passing act. Besides the effort of the bodily organs, the process demands that the worker’s will stay steadily in tune with his purpose during the whole operation. This means close attention. The less he is attracted by the nature of the work and the way it is carried on, and so the less he enjoys it as something that gives play to his bodily and mental powers, the closer his attention is forced to be.

<!-- ch07-p003 -->
The elementary factors of the labor process are 1, the personal activity of man, that is, work itself, 2, the object of that work, and 3, its instruments.

<!-- ch07-p004 -->
The soil (and this, economically speaking, includes water), in the virgin state in which it supplies[^1] man with the necessaries of life, or the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), ready to hand, exists independently of him and is the universal object of human labor. All those things that labor merely separates from their immediate connection with their surroundings are objects of labor provided by Nature of her own accord. Such are fish that we catch and take from their element, water; timber that we fell in the virgin forest; and ores that we extract from their veins. If, on the other hand, the object of labor has, so to speak, been filtered through earlier labor, we call it raw material. Ore already extracted and ready for washing is an example. All raw material is an object of labor, but not every object of labor is raw material: it can become raw material only after labor has changed it in some way.

[^1]: “The earth’s spontaneous productions being in small quantity, and quite independent of man, appear, as it were, to be furnished by Nature, in the same way as a small sum is given to a young man, in order to put him in a way of industry, and of making his fortune.” (James Steuart: “Principles of Polit. Econ.” edit. Dublin, 1770, v. I, p.116.)

<!-- ch07-p005 -->
An instrument of labor is a thing, or a complex of things, that the worker places between himself and the object of his labor, and that serves as the conductor of his activity. He makes use of the mechanical, physical, and chemical properties of some substances in order to make other substances serve his aims.[^2] Leave aside such ready-made means of subsistence as fruits, which a man gathers with his own limbs as the instruments of his labor. Then the first thing the worker takes possession of is not the object of labor but its instrument. In this way Nature becomes one of the organs of his activity, one that he adds to his own bodily organs, adding to his stature in spite of the Bible. Just as the earth is his original pantry, so it is also his original toolshed. It supplies him, for instance, with stones for throwing, grinding, pressing, cutting, etc. The earth itself is an instrument of labor; but when it is used as one in agriculture, that implies a whole series of other instruments and a comparatively high development of labor.[^3] As soon as labor develops in the least, it needs specially prepared instruments. So in the oldest caves we find stone tools and weapons. In the earliest period of human history, domesticated animals, that is, animals that have been bred for the purpose and have been changed by means of labor, play the chief part as instruments of labor, along with specially prepared stones, wood, bones, and shells.[^4] The use and making of instruments of labor, although they exist in germ among certain species of animals, are specifically characteristic of the human labor process, and so Franklin defines man as a tool-making animal. Remains of past instruments of labor are as important for investigating extinct economic forms of society as fossil bones are for identifying extinct species of animals. It is not the articles made, but how they are made and with what instruments, that lets us distinguish different economic epochs.[^5] Instruments of labor not only give a standard for the degree of development that human labor has reached; they also indicate the social conditions under which that labor is carried on. Among the instruments of labor, those of a mechanical kind, which taken as a whole we may call the bones and muscles of production, show the characteristics of a given epoch of production much more clearly than those that serve only to hold the materials of labor, like pipes, tubs, baskets, jars, etc. In a general way we may call this second class the vascular system of production. It first begins to play an important part in the chemical industries.

[^2]: “Reason is just as cunning as she is powerful. Her cunning consists principally in her mediating activity, which, by causing objects to act and re-act on each other in accordance with their own nature, in this way, without any direct interference in the process, carries out reason’s intentions.” (Hegel: “Enzyklopädie, Erster Theil, Die Logik,” Berlin, 1840, p. 382.)

[^3]: In his otherwise miserable work (“Théorie de l’Econ. Polit.” Paris, 1815), Ganilh, arguing against the “Physiocrats,” lists in a striking way the long series of earlier processes that are needed before agriculture proper can begin.

[^4]: Turgot, in his “Réflexions sur la Formation et la Distribution des Richesses” (1766), brings out well the importance of domesticated animals to early civilization.

[^5]: The least important commodities of all for comparing the technology of different epochs of production are articles of luxury, in the strict sense of the term. Our written histories up to now take little notice of the development of material production, which is the basis of all social life, and therefore of all real history. Yet however little they notice it, prehistoric times have been classified according to the results not of so-called historical investigations but of materialistic ones. These periods have been divided according to the materials their tools and weapons were made from, namely into the stone, the bronze, and the iron ages.

<!-- ch07-p006 -->
In a wider sense we may count among the instruments of labor, besides the things used to transfer labor directly to its object, which therefore serve in one way or another as conductors of activity, all the objects needed for carrying on the labor process. These do not enter directly into the process, but without them it either cannot take place at all or can take place only in part. Here again we find the earth to be a universal instrument of this sort, for it gives the worker a place to stand and a field in which to employ his activity. Among the instruments that are the result of earlier labor and also belong to this class, we find workshops, canals, roads, and so on.

<!-- ch07-p007 -->
So in the labor process man’s activity, with the help of the instruments of labor, brings about a change in the material worked on, a change intended from the start. The process disappears in the product. The product is a use-value: Nature’s material adapted to man’s wants by a change of form. Labor has combined itself with its object: the labor is materialized, the object is transformed. What appeared in the worker as movement now appears in the product as a fixed quality without motion. The blacksmith forges, and the product is a forging.

<!-- ch07-p008 -->
If we look at the whole process from the point of view of its result, the product, clearly both the instruments and the object of labor are means of production,[^6] and the labor itself is productive labor.[^7]

[^6]: It seems paradoxical to say that uncaught fish, for instance, are a means of production in the fishing industry. But so far no one has discovered the art of catching fish in waters that contain none.

[^7]: This way of determining what productive labor is, from the standpoint of the labor process alone, is by no means directly applicable to the capitalist process of production.

<!-- ch07-p009 -->
Although a use-value, in the form of a product, comes out of the labor process, other use-values, products of earlier labor, go into it as means of production. The same use-value is both the product of an earlier process and a means of production in a later one. So products are not only results of labor but also its essential conditions.

<!-- ch07-p010 -->
Apart from the extractive industries, in which Nature directly provides the material for labor, such as mining, hunting, fishing, and agriculture (so far as agriculture is limited to breaking up virgin soil), all branches of industry work on raw material: objects already filtered through labor, already products of labor. Seed in agriculture is an example. Animals and plants, which we are used to thinking of as products of Nature, are in their present form not only products of, say, last year’s labor. They are the result of a gradual transformation, continued through many generations under man’s supervision and by means of his labor. But in the great majority of cases, instruments of labor show traces of the labor of past ages even to the most superficial observer.

<!-- ch07-p011 -->
Raw material may either form the principal substance of a product, or enter into its formation only as an accessory. An accessory may be consumed by the instruments of labor, as coal is under a boiler, oil by a wheel, hay by draft-horses. Or it may be mixed with the raw material in order to modify it in some way, as chlorine is put into unbleached linen, coal with iron, dye with wool. Or, again, it may help to carry on the work itself, as with the materials used for heating and lighting workshops. The distinction between principal substance and accessory vanishes in the true chemical industries, because there none of the raw material reappears, in its original composition, in the substance of the product.[^8]

[^8]: Storch calls true raw materials “matières” [substances], and accessory material “matériaux” [materials]. Cherbuliez describes accessories as “matières instrumentales” [instrumental substances].

<!-- ch07-p012 -->
Every object has various properties, and so it can be put to different uses. One and the same product may therefore serve as raw material in very different processes. Grain, for example, is a raw material for millers, starch-manufacturers, distillers, and cattle breeders. It also enters as raw material into its own production, in the form of seed. Coal, too, is at the same time the product of coal-mining and a means of production in it.

<!-- ch07-p013 -->
Again, a particular product may be used in one and the same process both as an instrument of labor and as raw material. Take, for instance, the fattening of cattle: the animal is the raw material, and at the same time an instrument for producing manure.

<!-- ch07-p014 -->
A product, though ready for immediate consumption, may still serve as raw material for a further product, as grapes do when they become the raw material for wine. On the other hand, labor may give us its product in such a form that we can use it only as raw material, as is the case with cotton, thread, and yarn. Such a raw material, though itself a product, may have to go through a whole series of different processes. In each of these in turn it serves as raw material, its form constantly changing, until the last process of the series leaves it a finished product, ready for individual consumption or for use as an instrument of labor.

<!-- ch07-p015 -->
So we see that whether a use-value is to be regarded as raw material, as instrument of labor, or as product is determined entirely by its function in the labor process, by the position it holds there. As that position varies, so does its character.

<!-- ch07-p016 -->
So whenever a product enters a new labor process as a means of production, it loses its character as a product and becomes a mere factor in the process. A spinner treats spindles only as tools for spinning, and flax only as the material that he spins. Of course it is impossible to spin without material and spindles, and so we must assume that these things exist as products when the spinning begins. But in the process itself, the fact that they are products of earlier labor does not matter in the least. In the same way, in digestion it makes no difference whatever that bread is the product of the earlier labor of the farmer, the miller, and the baker. On the contrary, it is generally through their imperfections as products that the means of production in any process make their character as products felt. A blunt knife or weak thread forcibly reminds us of Mr. A., the cutler, or Mr. B., the spinner. In the finished product, the labor through which it acquired its useful qualities cannot be detected; it has apparently vanished.

<!-- ch07-p017 -->
A machine that does not serve the purposes of labor is useless. On top of that, it falls prey to the destructive action of natural forces. Iron rusts and wood rots. Yarn that we neither weave nor knit is wasted cotton. Living labor (the labor of workers now at work) must seize on these things and wake them from their death-sleep, changing them from mere possible use-values into real and effective ones. Bathed in the fire of labor, taken over as part and parcel of labor’s organism, and, as it were, brought to life to perform their functions in the process, they are indeed consumed. But they are consumed with a purpose, as the elementary parts of new use-values, of new products, always ready to serve as means of subsistence for individual consumption or as means of production for some new labor process.

<!-- ch07-p018 -->
So if, on the one hand, finished products are not only results but also necessary conditions of the labor process, then, on the other hand, being taken into that process, coming into contact with living labor, is the only way they can be made to keep their character as use-values and be put to use.

<!-- ch07-p019 -->
Labor uses up its material factors, its object and its instruments. It consumes them, and so it is a process of consumption. Such productive consumption differs from individual consumption in this: individual consumption uses up products as means of subsistence for the living individual; productive consumption uses them up as the means through which alone labor, the labor-power of the living individual, is able to act. So the product of individual consumption is the consumer himself; the result of productive consumption is a product distinct from the consumer.

<!-- ch07-p020 -->
So to the extent that its instruments and objects are themselves products, labor consumes products in order to create products; in other words, it consumes one set of products by turning them into means of production for another set. But just as in the beginning the only participants in the labor process were man and the earth, which exists independently of man, so even now we still use in the process many means of production that Nature provides directly and that do not represent any combination of natural substances with human labor.

<!-- ch07-p021 -->
The labor process, broken down as above into its simple elementary factors, is human action aimed at producing use-values, the appropriation of natural substances for human needs. It is the necessary condition for the exchange of matter between man and Nature. It is the everlasting condition of human existence, imposed by Nature, and so it is independent of every social phase of that existence, or rather, it is common to every such phase. So it was not necessary to show our worker in connection with other workers; man and his labor on one side, Nature and its materials on the other, were enough. The taste of the porridge does not tell you who grew the oats, and no more does this simple process tell you of itself under what social conditions it is taking place: whether under the slave-owner’s brutal lash or the anxious eye of the capitalist, whether Cincinnatus carries it on in tilling his modest farm or a savage in killing wild animals with stones.[^9]

[^9]: By a wonderful feat of logical sharpness, Colonel Torrens has discovered the origin of capital in this stone of the savage. “In the first stone which he [the savage] flings at the wild animal he pursues, in the first stick that he seizes to strike down the fruit which hangs above his reach, we see the appropriation of one article for the purpose of aiding in the acquisition of another, and thus discover the origin of capital.” (R. Torrens: “An Essay on the Production of Wealth,” etc., pp. 70-71.)

<!-- ch07-p022 -->
Let us now return to our would-be capitalist. We left him just after he had bought, in the open market, all the necessary factors of the labor process: its objective factors, the means of production, as well as its subjective factor, labor-power. With an expert’s keen eye he has selected the means of production and the kind of labor-power best suited to his particular trade, be it spinning, bootmaking, or any other kind. He then proceeds to consume the commodity he has just bought, the labor-power, by making the worker, the personification of that labor-power, consume the means of production by his labor. The general character of the labor process is clearly not changed by the fact that the worker works for the capitalist instead of for himself. Moreover, the particular methods and operations used in bootmaking or spinning are not immediately changed by the capitalist’s stepping in. He must begin by taking labor-power as he finds it in the market, and so he must be satisfied with labor of the kind found in the period just before capitalists arose. Changes in the methods of production through the subordination of labor to capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger) can take place only at a later period, and so they will have to be dealt with in a later chapter.

<!-- ch07-p023 -->
The labor process, turned into the process by which the capitalist consumes labor-power, shows two characteristic features. First, the worker works under the control of the capitalist, to whom his labor belongs. The capitalist takes good care that the work is done properly and that the means of production are used intelligently, so that there is no unnecessary waste of raw material and no wear and tear on the tools beyond what the work necessarily causes.

<!-- ch07-p024 -->
Secondly, the product is the property of the capitalist and not of the worker, its immediate producer. Suppose that a capitalist pays for a day’s labor-power at its value (value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time). Then the right to use that power for a day belongs to him, just as much as the right to use any other commodity, such as a horse he has hired for the day. The use of a commodity belongs to its buyer, and the seller of labor-power, by giving his labor, in reality does no more than part with the use-value he has sold. From the moment he steps into the workshop, the use-value of his labor-power, and therefore also its use, which is labor, belongs to the capitalist. By buying labor-power, the capitalist incorporates labor, as a living ferment, into the lifeless elements of the product. From his point of view, the labor process is nothing more than the consumption of the commodity he has bought, that is, of labor-power; but he cannot carry out this consumption except by supplying the labor-power with means of production. The labor process is a process between things that the capitalist has bought, things that have become his property. So the product of this process belongs to him, just as much as the wine that is the product of a process of fermentation completed in his cellar.[^10]

[^10]: “Products are appropriated before they are converted into capital; this conversion does not secure them from such appropriation.” (Cherbuliez: “Richesse ou Pauvreté,” edit. Paris, 1841, p. 54.) “The Proletarian [a member of the class of wage workers], by selling his labour for a definite quantity of the necessaries of life, renounces all claim to a share in the product. The mode of appropriation of the products remains the same as before; it is in no way altered by the bargain we have mentioned. The product belongs exclusively to the capitalist, who supplied the raw material and the necessaries of life; and this is a rigorous consequence of the law of appropriation, a law whose fundamental principle was the very opposite, namely, that every labourer has an exclusive right to the ownership of what he produces.” (cited above, p. 58.) “When the labourers receive wages for their labour ... the capitalist is then the owner not of the capital only” (he means the means of production) “but of the labour also. If what is paid as wages is included, as it commonly is, in the term capital, it is absurd to talk of labour separately from capital. The word capital as thus employed includes labour and capital both.” (James Mill: “Elements of Pol. Econ.,” etc., Ed. 1821, pp. 70, 71.)
