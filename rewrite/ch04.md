# Part 2: Transformation of Money into Capital

## Chapter 4: The General Formula for Capital

<!-- ch04-p001 -->
The circulation of commodities is the starting point of capital. (Commodities are things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use. Their circulation is their exchange through money: selling and buying.) The production of commodities, their circulation, and that more developed form of their circulation called trade, together form the historical groundwork from which capital rises. The modern history of capital dates from the creation, in the 16th century, of a trade and a market that embraced the whole world.

<!-- ch04-p002 -->
If we set aside the material substance of the circulation of commodities, that is, the exchange of the various use-values (the usefulness of things: what they can be used for, which comes from their physical properties), and look only at the economic forms that this process of circulation produces, we find that its final result is money. This final product of the circulation of commodities is the first form in which capital appears.

<!-- ch04-p003 -->
Historically, capital, as opposed to landed property, invariably takes the form of money at first. It appears as moneyed wealth, as the capital of the merchant and of the usurer.[^1] But we do not need to look back to the origin of capital to discover that capital’s first form of appearance is money. We can see it daily, before our very eyes. Even in our own day, all new capital first comes on the stage, that is, on the market, whether the market of commodities, of labor, or of money, in the shape of money: money that has to be turned into capital by a definite process.

[^1]: The contrast between the power conferred by landed property, which rests on personal relations of domination and servitude, and the impersonal power given by money, is well expressed by two French proverbs: “Nulle terre sans seigneur,” and “L’argent n’a pas de maître” [“No land without its lord,” and “Money has no master”].

<!-- ch04-p004 -->
The first difference we notice between money that is only money and money that is capital is nothing more than a difference in their form of circulation.

<!-- ch04-p005 -->
The simplest form of the circulation of commodities is C—M—C: commodities are turned into money, and the money is changed back into commodities. In other words, selling in order to buy. But alongside this form we find another, specifically different form: M—C—M. Here money is turned into commodities, and the commodities are changed back into money. In other words, buying in order to sell. Money that circulates in this second way is thereby transformed into capital, becomes capital, and is already potentially capital.

<!-- ch04-p006 -->
Now let us look a little more closely at the circuit M—C—M. Like the other circuit, it consists of two opposed phases. In the first phase, M—C, the purchase, the money is changed into a commodity. In the second phase, C—M, the sale, the commodity is changed back into money. These two phases combined make up a single movement. In it, money is exchanged for a commodity, and the same commodity is exchanged again for money. A commodity is bought in order to be sold. Or, if we ignore the difference in form between buying and selling, a commodity is bought with money, and then money is bought with a commodity.[^2] The result, in which the phases of the process vanish, is the exchange of money for money, M—M. If I buy 2,000 lbs. of cotton for £100, and resell the 2,000 lbs. of cotton for £110, I have in fact exchanged £100 for £110, money for money.

[^2]: “Avec de l’argent on achète des marchandises et avec des marchandises on achète de l’argent.” [“With money one buys commodities, and with commodities one buys money.”] (Mercier de la Rivière: “L’ordre naturel et essentiel des sociétés politiques,” p. 543.)

<!-- ch04-p007 -->
Now clearly the circuit M—C—M would be absurd and meaningless if the intention were to exchange two equal sums of money by this means, £100 for £100. The miser’s plan would be far simpler and surer: he holds on to his £100 instead of exposing it to the dangers of circulation. And yet, whether the merchant who paid £100 for his cotton sells it for £110, or lets it go for £100, or even for £50, his money has in any case gone through a characteristic and original movement. This movement is quite different in kind from the one money goes through in the hands of the peasant who sells grain and, with the money thus set free, buys clothes. We must therefore first examine the features that distinguish the forms of the circuits M—C—M and C—M—C. In doing this, the real difference that lies beneath the mere difference of form will reveal itself.

<!-- ch04-p008 -->
Let us see, first, what the two forms have in common.

<!-- ch04-p009 -->
Both circuits can be broken down into the same two opposed phases: C—M, a sale, and M—C, a purchase. In each of these phases the same material elements confront one another, a commodity and money, and so do the same economic characters in the drama, a buyer and a seller. Each circuit is the unity of the same two opposed phases. In each case this unity is brought about through the intervention of three contracting parties: one only sells, another only buys, and the third both buys and sells.

<!-- ch04-p010 -->
But what first and foremost distinguishes the circuit C—M—C from the circuit M—C—M is that the two phases come in reverse order. The simple circulation of commodities begins with a sale and ends with a purchase, while the circulation of money as capital begins with a purchase and ends with a sale. In the one case both the starting point and the goal are commodities; in the other, they are money. In the first form, the intervention of money brings about the movement; in the second, the intervention of a commodity does.

<!-- ch04-p011 -->
In the circulation C—M—C, the money is in the end converted into a commodity that serves as a use-value; it is spent once and for all. In the reversed form, M—C—M, on the contrary, the buyer lays out money so that, as a seller, he may get money back. By buying his commodity he throws money into circulation, in order to withdraw it again by selling the same commodity. He lets the money go, but only with the sly intention of getting it back again. So the money is not spent; it is merely advanced.[^3]

[^3]: “When a thing is bought in order to be sold again, the sum employed is called money advanced; when it is bought not to be sold, it may be said to be expended.” — (James Steuart: “Works,” etc. Edited by Gen. Sir James Steuart, his son. London, 1805, V. I., p. 274.)

<!-- ch04-p012 -->
In the circuit C—M—C, the same piece of money changes places twice. The seller gets it from the buyer and pays it out to another seller. The complete circulation begins when money is received for commodities and ends when money is paid for commodities. It is just the opposite in the circuit M—C—M. Here it is not the piece of money that changes places twice, but the commodity. The buyer takes it from the seller’s hands and passes it into the hands of another buyer. In the simple circulation of commodities, the double change of place of the same piece of money moves it from one hand into another. In just the same way, here the double change of place of the same commodity brings about the flowing back of the money to its point of departure.

<!-- ch04-p013 -->
This flowing back does not depend on the commodity being sold for more than was paid for it. That circumstance affects only the amount of money that comes back. The flowing back itself takes place as soon as the purchased commodity is resold, in other words, as soon as the circuit M—C—M is completed. So here we have a tangible difference between the circulation of money as capital and its circulation as mere money.

<!-- ch04-p014 -->
The circuit C—M—C comes completely to an end as soon as the money brought in by selling one commodity is withdrawn again by buying another.

<!-- ch04-p015 -->
If money nevertheless flows back to its starting point afterward, this can only happen through a renewal or repetition of the operation. If I sell a quarter of grain for £3, and with this £3 buy clothes, the money, as far as I am concerned, is spent and done with. It belongs to the clothes merchant. If I now sell a second quarter of grain, money does flow back to me, but not as a sequel to the first transaction; it flows back because that transaction is repeated. The money leaves me again as soon as I complete this second transaction with a new purchase. Therefore, in the circuit C—M—C, the spending of money has nothing to do with its flowing back. In M—C—M, on the other hand, the flowing back of the money is conditioned by the very way it is spent. Without this flowing back, the operation fails, or the process is interrupted and incomplete, because its complementary and final phase, the sale, is missing.

<!-- ch04-p016 -->
The circuit C—M—C starts with one commodity and finishes with another, which falls out of circulation and into consumption. Consumption, the satisfaction of wants, in one word, use-value, is its end and aim. The circuit M—C—M, on the contrary, begins with money and ends with money. Its leading motive, and the goal that draws it on, is therefore mere exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for).

<!-- ch04-p017 -->
In the simple circulation of commodities, the two extremes of the circuit have the same economic form. They are both commodities, and commodities of equal value (value being what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time). But they are also use-values of different qualities, for example grain and clothes. Here the basis of the movement is the exchange of products, of the different materials in which the labor of society is embodied. It is otherwise in the circulation M—C—M, which at first sight seems purposeless, because it merely repeats the same thing. Both extremes have the same economic form. They are both money, and so they are not use-values of different qualities; for money is only the converted form of commodities, in which their particular use-values vanish. To exchange £100 for cotton, and then this same cotton again for £100, is merely a roundabout way of exchanging money for money, the same for the same. It seems to be an operation just as purposeless as it is absurd.[^4] One sum of money can be told apart from another only by its amount. So the character and tendency of the process M—C—M come not from any difference in quality between its extremes, since both are money, but solely from their difference in quantity. More money is withdrawn from circulation at the finish than was thrown into it at the start. The cotton that was bought for £100 is perhaps resold for £100 + £10, or £110. The exact form of this process is therefore M—C—M', where M' = M + ∆ M = the original sum advanced, plus an increment. I call this increment, or excess over the original value, “surplus-value.” So the value originally advanced not only stays intact while in circulation, but adds a surplus-value to itself, or expands itself. It is this movement that turns it into capital.

[^4]: “On n’échange pas de l’argent contre de l’argent” [“One does not exchange money for money”], says Mercier de la Rivière to the Mercantilists (cited above, p. 486). A work that deals expressly with “trade” and “speculation” contains the following: “All trade consists in the exchange of things of different kinds; and the advantage” (to the merchant?) “arises out of this difference. To exchange a pound of bread against a pound of bread ... would be attended with no advantage; ... Hence trade is advantageously contrasted with gambling, which consists in a mere exchange of money for money.” (Th. Corbet, “An Inquiry into the Causes and Modes of the Wealth of Individuals; or the Principles of Trade and Speculation Explained.” London, 1841, p. 5.) Corbet does not see that M—M, the exchange of money for money, is the characteristic form of circulation not only of merchants’ capital but of all capital. Yet at least he admits that this form is common to gambling and to one kind of trade, namely speculation. But then along comes MacCulloch and makes out that to buy in order to sell is to speculate, and so the difference between Speculation and Trade vanishes. “Every transaction in which an individual buys produce in order to sell it again, is, in fact, a speculation.” (MacCulloch: “A Dictionary Practical, &c., of Commerce.” London, 1847, p. 1009.) With much more naïveté, Pinto, the Pindar of the Amsterdam Stock Exchange, remarks: “Le commerce est un jeu: (taken from Locke) et ce n’est pas avec des gueux qu’on peut gagner. Si l’on gagnait longtemps en tout avec tous, il faudrait rendre de bon accord les plus grandes parties du profit pour recommencer le jeu.” [“Trade is a game, and it is not from beggars that one can win. If one kept on winning everything from everyone for a long time, the greater part of the profit would have to be given back by common consent, in order to start the game again.”] (Pinto: “Traité de la Circulation et du Crédit.” Amsterdam, 1771. p. 231,)

<!-- ch04-p018 -->
Of course, it is also possible that in C—M—C the two extremes C-C, say grain and clothes, may represent different quantities of value. The farmer may sell his grain above its value, or may buy the clothes below their value. On the other hand, he may “be done” (cheated) by the clothes merchant. Yet in the form of circulation we are now considering, such differences in value are purely accidental. The fact that the grain and the clothes are equivalents does not rob the process of all meaning, as it does in M—C—M. Rather, the equality of their values is a necessary condition for its normal course.

<!-- ch04-p019 -->
The repetition or renewal of the act of selling in order to buy is kept within bounds by the very aim it pursues, namely consumption, or the satisfaction of definite wants, an aim that lies entirely outside the sphere of circulation. But when we buy in order to sell, we, on the contrary, begin and end with the same thing, money, exchange-value; and so the movement becomes endless. No doubt M becomes M + ∆M, £100 become £110. But looked at only in terms of quality, £110 are the same as £100, namely money. And looked at in terms of quantity, £110 is, like £100, a sum of definite and limited value. If the £110 are now spent as money, they stop playing their part. They are no longer capital. Withdrawn from circulation, they become petrified into a hoard, and even if they stayed in that state until doomsday, not a single farthing would be added to them. So, if the expansion of value is once aimed at, there is just the same incentive to increase the value of the £110 as of the £100. For both are only limited expressions of exchange-value, and so both have the same calling: to come as near as possible to absolute wealth by growing in quantity. For a moment, indeed, the value originally advanced, the £100, can be told apart from the surplus-value of £10 that is added to it during circulation; but the distinction vanishes at once. At the end of the process, we do not receive the original £100 in one hand and the surplus-value of £10 in the other. We simply get a value of £110, which is in exactly the same condition, and just as fit to begin the expanding process, as the original £100 was. Money ends the movement only to begin it again.[^5] Therefore the final result of every separate circuit, in which a purchase and the sale that follows it are completed, forms of itself the starting point of a new circuit. The simple circulation of commodities, selling in order to buy, is a means of carrying out a purpose unconnected with circulation, namely, the acquiring of use-values, the satisfaction of wants. The circulation of money as capital, on the contrary, is an end in itself, for the expansion of value takes place only within this constantly renewed movement. The circulation of capital therefore has no limits.[^6]

[^5]: “Capital is divisible ... into the original capital and the profit, the increment to the capital ... although in practice this profit is immediately turned into capital, and set in motion with the original.” (F. Engels, “Umrisse zu einer Kritik der Nationalökonomie, in: Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher, herausgegeben von Arnold Ruge und Karl Marx.” Paris, 1844, p. 99.)

[^6]: Aristotle contrasts Œconomic with Chrematistic. He starts from Œconomic. Insofar as it is the art of making a living, it is limited to obtaining the articles that are necessary for existence and useful either to a household or to the state. “True wealth (ὁ ἀληθινὸς πλοῦτος) consists of such values in use; for the quantity of possessions of this kind, capable of making life pleasant, is not unlimited. There is, however, a second mode of acquiring things, to which we may by preference and with correctness give the name of Chrematistic, and in this case there appear to be no limits to riches and possessions. Trade (ἡ καπηλικὴ literally means retail trade, and Aristotle takes this kind because use-values predominate in it) does not in its nature belong to Chrematistic, for here the exchange has reference only to what is necessary to themselves (the buyer or seller).” Therefore, as he goes on to show, the original form of trade was barter, but as barter spread, the need for money arose. Once money was discovered, barter necessarily developed into καπηλικὴ, into trading in commodities, and this in turn, against its original tendency, grew into Chrematistic, into the art of making money. Now Chrematistic differs from Œconomic in this way: “in the case of Chrematistic circulation is the source of riches (ποιητικὴ χρημάτων ... διὰ χρημάτων μεταβολῆς). And it appears to revolve about money, for money is the beginning and end of this kind of exchange (τὸ γὰρ νόμισμα στοιχεῖον καὶ πέρας τῆς ἀλλαγῆς ἐστίν). Therefore also riches, such as Chrematistic strives for, are unlimited. Just as every art that is not a means to an end, but an end in itself, has no limit to its aims, because it seeks constantly to approach nearer and nearer to that end, while those arts that pursue means to an end, are not boundless, since the goal itself imposes a limit upon them, so with Chrematistic, there are no bounds to its aims, these aims being absolute wealth. Œconomic not Chrematistic has a limit ... the object of the former is something different from money, of the latter the augmentation of money.... By confounding these two forms, which overlap each other, some people have been led to look upon the preservation and increase of money ad infinitum [without end] as the end and aim of Œconomic.” (Aristoteles, De Rep. edit. Bekker, lib. I., c. 8, 9. passim.)

<!-- ch04-p020 -->
As the conscious representative of this movement, the owner of money becomes a capitalist. His person, or rather his pocket, is the point from which the money starts and to which it returns. The expansion of value is the objective basis or mainspring of the circulation M—C—M, and it becomes his subjective aim. Only insofar as the appropriation of more and more wealth in the abstract becomes the sole motive of his operations does he function as a capitalist, that is, as capital personified and endowed with consciousness and a will. Use-values must therefore never be looked upon as the real aim of the capitalist;[^7] nor must the profit on any single transaction. The restless, never-ending process of profit-making is all he aims at.[^8] This boundless greed for riches, this passionate chase after exchange-value,[^9] is common to the capitalist and the miser. But while the miser is merely a capitalist gone mad, the capitalist is a rational miser. The miser strives after the never-ending increase of exchange-value by seeking to save[^10] his money from circulation. The shrewder capitalist achieves it by constantly throwing his money afresh into circulation.[^11]

[^7]: “Commodities (here used in the sense of use-values) are not the terminating object of the trading capitalist, money is his terminating object.” (Th. Chalmers, On Pol. Econ. &c.*,* 2nd Ed., Glasgow, 1832, pp. 165, 166.)

[^8]: “Il mercante non conta quasi per niente il lucro fatto, ma mira sempre al futuro.” [“The merchant counts the gain he has made as almost nothing, but always looks to the future.”] (A. Genovesi, Lezioni di Economia Civile (1765), Custodi’s edit. of Italian Economists. Parte Moderna t. viii, p. 139.)

[^9]: “The inextinguishable passion for gain, the auri sacra fames [accursed hunger for gold], will always lead capitalists.” (MacCulloch: “The Principles of Polit. Econ.” London, 1830, p. 179.) This view, of course, does not stop the same MacCulloch and others of his kind, when they get into theoretical difficulties, for example over the question of over-production, from turning the same capitalist into a moral citizen whose sole concern is for use-values, and who even develops an insatiable hunger for boots, hats, eggs, calico, and other extremely familiar sorts of use-values.

[^10]: Σὠξειν [to save, to keep safe] is a characteristic Greek expression for hoarding. In the same way, in English “to save” has the same two meanings: sauver [to rescue] and épargner [to put by, to spare].

[^11]: “Questo infinito che le cose non hanno in progresso, hanno in giro.” [“That infinity which things do not have in progress, they have in circulation.”] (Galiani.)

In simple circulation, the value of commodities takes on an independent form, that is, the money form (the form of value in which one commodity, gold, has become the universal equivalent). This form serves only one purpose, namely, their exchange, and it vanishes in the final result of the movement. In the circulation M—C—M, on the other hand, both the money and the commodity represent only different modes of existence of value itself: the money its general mode, and the commodity its particular, or, so to speak, disguised mode.[^12] Value is constantly changing from one form to the other without being lost in the process, and so it takes on an automatically active character. If we now take in turn each of the two different forms that self-expanding value assumes, one after the other, in the course of its life, we arrive at these two propositions: Capital is money: Capital is commodities.[^13] In truth, however, value is here the active factor in a process. In this process, while it constantly takes the form of money and of commodities in turn, it at the same time changes in magnitude: it differentiates itself by throwing off surplus-value from itself. In other words, the original value expands spontaneously. For the movement in the course of which it adds surplus-value is its own movement, and so its expansion is automatic expansion. Because it is value, it has acquired the occult quality of being able to add value to itself. It brings forth living offspring, or, at the least, lays golden eggs.

[^12]: “Ce n’est pas la matière qui fait le capital, mais la valeur de ces matières.” [“It is not matter that makes capital, but the value of those materials.”] (J. B. Say: “Traité d’Econ. Polit.” 3ème éd. Paris, 1817, t. II., p. 429.)

[^13]: “Currency (!) employed in producing articles... is capital.” (Macleod: “The Theory and Practice of Banking.” London, 1855, v. 1, ch. i, p. 55.) “Capital is commodities.” (James Mill: “Elements of Pol. Econ.” London, 1821, p. 74.)

<!-- ch04-p022 -->
Value, then, is the active factor in such a process. At one time it takes the form of money, at another that of commodities, but through all these changes it preserves itself and expands. For this reason it needs some independent form by which its identity can be established at any time. And it has this form only in the shape of money. It is in the form of money that value begins and ends, and begins again, every act of its own spontaneous generation. It began as £100, it is now £110, and so on. But the money itself is only one of the two forms of value. Unless it takes the form of some commodity, it does not become capital. Here there is no antagonism between the money and commodities, as there is in the case of hoarding. The capitalist knows that all commodities, however shabby they may look, or however badly they may smell, are in faith and in truth money, inwardly circumcised Jews, and, what is more, a wonderful means of making more money out of money.

<!-- ch04-p023 -->
In simple circulation, C—M—C, the value of commodities attained at most a form independent of their use-values, that is, the form of money. But now, in the circulation M—C—M, or the circulation of capital, that same value suddenly presents itself as an independent substance, endowed with a motion of its own, passing through a life-process of its own, in which money and commodities are mere forms that it takes on and casts off in turn. Indeed, more than that: instead of simply representing the relations of commodities, it now enters, so to speak, into private relations with itself. It differentiates itself as original value from itself as surplus-value, as the father differentiates himself from himself as the son, yet both are one and of one age. For only by the surplus-value of £10 does the £100 originally advanced become capital. And as soon as this happens, as soon as the son is begotten, and through the son the father, their difference vanishes, and they become one again, £110.

<!-- ch04-p024 -->
So value now becomes value in process, money in process, and, as such, capital. It comes out of circulation, enters it again, preserves and multiplies itself within its circuit, comes back out of it increased in size, and begins the same round over and over again.[^14] M—M', money that begets money: such is the description of Capital from the mouths of its first interpreters, the Mercantilists.

[^14]: Capital: “portion fructifiante de la richesse accumulée... valeur permanente, multipliante.” [fruit-bearing portion of accumulated wealth ... permanent, multiplying value.] (Sismondi: “Nouveaux Principes d’Econ. Polit.,” t. i., p. 88, 89.)

<!-- ch04-p025 -->
Buying in order to sell, or, more exactly, buying in order to sell dearer, M—C—M', certainly appears to be a form peculiar to only one kind of capital, namely, merchants’ capital. But industrial capital too is money that is changed into commodities and, by the sale of these commodities, turned back into more money. The events that take place outside the sphere of circulation, in the interval between the buying and the selling, do not affect the form of this movement. Lastly, in the case of interest-bearing capital, the circulation M—C—M' appears shortened. We have its result without the stage in between, in the form M—M', “en style lapidaire” [in the style of an inscription], so to speak: money that is worth more money, value that is greater than itself.

<!-- ch04-p026 -->
M—C—M' is therefore in reality the general formula of capital as it appears at first sight within the sphere of circulation.
