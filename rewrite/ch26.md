# Part 8: Primitive Accumulation

## Chapter 26: The Secret of Primitive Accumulation

<!-- ch26-p001 -->
We have seen how money is changed into capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger). We have seen how surplus-value is made through capital, and how more capital is made from surplus-value. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) But the accumulation of capital presupposes surplus-value. Surplus-value presupposes capitalist production. And capitalist production presupposes that considerable masses of capital and of labor-power already exist in the hands of producers of commodities. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work, which the worker sells to the capitalist; a commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, not for the producer's own use.) So the whole movement seems to turn in a vicious circle. We can only get out of it by supposing a primitive accumulation (what Adam Smith calls previous accumulation) that comes before capitalist accumulation: an accumulation that is not the result of the capitalist mode of production, but its starting point.

<!-- ch26-p002 -->
This primitive accumulation plays about the same part in political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) as original sin plays in theology. Adam bit the apple, and from then on sin fell on the human race. Its origin is supposed to be explained by telling it as a story from the past. Long, long ago there were two sorts of people. One was the diligent, intelligent, and, above all, thrifty elite. The other was lazy good-for-nothings, who spent all they had, and more, in wild living. The legend of theological original sin certainly tells us how man came to be condemned to eat his bread in the sweat of his brow. But the history of economic original sin shows us that there are people for whom this is by no means necessary. Never mind! And so it came about that the thrifty sort piled up wealth, and the lazy sort in the end had nothing to sell except their own skins. And from this original sin dates the poverty of the great majority, who, despite all their labor, have up to now nothing to sell but themselves; and the wealth of the few, which keeps growing although they stopped working long ago. Such insipid childishness is preached to us every day in defense of property. M. Thiers, for example, had the nerve to repeat it, with all the solemnity of a statesman, to the French people, who were once so witty. But as soon as the question of property comes up, it becomes a sacred duty to proclaim the mental food of the infant as the only thing fit for all ages and all stages of development. In actual history it is well known that conquest, enslavement, robbery, murder, in short force, play the great part. In the gentle chronicles of political economy, the idyllic has reigned since time immemorial. Right and “labour” were from the beginning the only means of getting rich, with the present year, of course, always excepted. In fact, the methods of primitive accumulation are anything but idyllic.

<!-- ch26-p003 -->
In themselves, money and commodities are no more capital than the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things) and the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) are. They need to be transformed into capital. But this transformation itself can only take place under certain conditions, which come down to this: two very different kinds of owners of commodities must come face to face and into contact. On the one side are the owners of money, means of production, and means of subsistence, who are eager to increase the sum of values they own by buying other people's labor-power. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) On the other side are free workers, the sellers of their own labor-power, and therefore the sellers of labor. They are free workers in a double sense. They are not themselves part and parcel of the means of production, as slaves, bondsmen, etc. are. Nor do the means of production belong to them, as they do to peasant-proprietors. So they are free from, unencumbered by, any means of production of their own. With this split of the market for commodities into two poles, the basic conditions of capitalist production are given. The capitalist system presupposes the complete separation of the workers from all property in the means by which they can put their labor to work. Once capitalist production stands on its own legs, it not only maintains this separation but reproduces it on a constantly growing scale. So the process that clears the way for the capitalist system can be none other than the process that takes away from the worker the possession of his means of production. On the one side, this process turns the social means of subsistence and of production into capital. On the other, it turns the immediate producers into wage workers. The so-called primitive accumulation, therefore, is nothing else than the historical process of divorcing the producer from the means of production. It appears as primitive because it forms the prehistoric stage of capital and of the mode of production that corresponds to capital.

<!-- ch26-p004 -->
The economic structure of capitalist society has grown out of the economic structure of feudal society. The breaking up of feudal society set free the elements of capitalist society.

<!-- ch26-p005 -->
The immediate producer, the worker, could only dispose of his own person once he was no longer tied to the soil and no longer the slave, serf, or bondsman of another person. To become a free seller of labor-power, who takes his commodity wherever he finds a market, he also had to escape from the rule of the guilds, their rules for apprentices and journeymen, and the obstacles of their labor regulations. So the historical movement that changes the producers into wage workers appears, on the one hand, as their liberation from serfdom and from the chains of the guilds. This side alone exists for our bourgeois historians (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it). But, on the other hand, these newly freed men became sellers of themselves only after they had been robbed of all their own means of production, and of all the guarantees of existence that the old feudal arrangements offered. And the history of this, their expropriation (the taking away of their property), is written in the annals of mankind in letters of blood and fire.

<!-- ch26-p006 -->
The industrial capitalists, these new potentates, had for their part to push aside not only the guild masters of the handicrafts, but also the feudal lords, who owned the sources of wealth. In this respect, their conquest of social power appears as the fruit of a victorious struggle on two fronts. It was a struggle against feudal lordship and its revolting privileges. And it was a struggle against the guilds and the chains they laid on the free development of production and the free exploitation of man by man. The chevaliers d’industrie [knights of industry, that is, swindlers], however, only managed to replace the chevaliers of the sword [knights] by making use of events in which they themselves had played no part at all. They rose by means as vile as those by which the Roman freedman once made himself the master of his *patronus* [patron, his former master].

<!-- ch26-p007 -->
The starting point of the development that produced the wage worker as well as the capitalist was the servitude of the worker. The advance consisted in a change in the form of this servitude, in the transformation of feudal exploitation into capitalist exploitation. To understand how this development proceeded, we need not go back very far. We do come across the first beginnings of capitalist production as early as the 14th or 15th century, here and there, in certain towns of the Mediterranean. But the capitalist era dates from the 16th century. Wherever it appears, serfdom has long since been abolished, and the highest achievement of the middle ages, the existence of sovereign towns, has long been in decline.

<!-- ch26-p008 -->
In the history of primitive accumulation, every revolution is epoch-making that acts as a lever for the capitalist class as it is being formed. But above all, the epoch-making moments are those when great masses of men are suddenly and forcibly torn from their means of subsistence and hurled onto the labor market as free and “unattached” proletarians (members of the proletariat, the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power). The expropriation of the agricultural producer, of the peasant, from the soil is the basis of the whole process. The history of this expropriation takes on different aspects in different countries. It runs through its various phases in a different order of succession, and at different periods. Only in England, which we take as our example, does it have the classic form.

[^1]: In Italy, where capitalist production developed earliest, the breaking up of serfdom also took place earlier than elsewhere. There the serf was freed before he had gained any prescriptive right to the soil (a right based on long use). His freedom at once transformed him into a free proletarian, who, moreover, found his master ready and waiting for him in the towns, for the most part handed down as legacies from Roman times. About the end of the 15th century, the revolution of the world market destroyed Northern Italy's commercial supremacy. Then a movement in the reverse direction set in. The workers of the towns were driven all together into the countryside, and gave a push, never seen before, to small-scale farming, carried on in the form of gardening.
