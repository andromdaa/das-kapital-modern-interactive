### Section 7: Repulsion and Attraction of Workpeople by the Factory System. Crises in the Cotton Trade

<!-- ch15-p165 -->
All political economists of any standing admit that the introduction of new machinery has a disastrous effect on the workers in the old handicrafts and branches of manufacture with which this machinery at first competes. Almost all of them lament the slavery of the factory worker. And what is the great trump card they play? That machinery, once the horrors of the period of its introduction and development have died down, in the long run increases the number of the slaves of labor instead of reducing it! Yes, political economy revels in the hideous theory, hideous to every “philanthropist” who believes in the eternal, Nature-ordained necessity of capitalist production, that after a period of growth and transition, even its crowning success, the factory system based on machinery, grinds down more workers than it throws on the streets when it is first introduced.[^146]

[^146]: Ganilh, on the contrary, considers that the final result of the factory system is an absolutely smaller number of factory workers, at whose expense a larger number of “gens honnêtes” [respectable people] live and develop their well-known “perfectibilité perfectible” [perfectible perfectibility]. Little as he understands the movement of production, he at least feels that machinery must be a very fatal institution if its introduction turns busy workers into paupers, and its development calls more slaves of labor into existence than it has done away with. The idiocy of his standpoint can only be brought out in his own words: “Les classes condamnées à produire et à consommer diminuent, et les classes qui dirigent le travail, qui soulagent, consolent, et éclairent toute la population, se multiplient ... et s’approprient tous les bienfaits qui résultent de la diminution des frais du travail, de l’abondance des productions, et du bon marché des consommations. Dans cette direction, l’espéce humaine s’élève aux plus hautes conceptions du génie, pénètre dans les profoundeurs mystérieuses de la religion, établit les principes salutaires de la morale (which consists in ‘s’approprier tous les beinfaits,’ etc.), les lois tutélaires de la liberté (liberty of ‘les classes condamnées à produire?’) et du pouvoir, de l’obéissance et de la justice, du devoir et de la l’humanité.” [The classes condemned to produce and to consume grow fewer, and the classes that direct labor, that relieve, console and enlighten the whole population, multiply ... and take for themselves all the benefits that come from the lower costs of labor, from the abundance of products and from the cheapness of consumer goods. Ch. Ganilh,” 2ème ed., Paris, 1821, t. I, p. 224, and see p. 212.

It is true that in some cases, as we saw from the examples of English worsted and silk factories, a huge extension of the factory system may, at a certain stage of its development, go along with a decrease in the number of factory workers employed that is not only relative but absolute. In the year 1860 Parliament ordered a special census of all the factories in the United Kingdom. In the parts of Lancashire, Cheshire, and Yorkshire that made up the district of Mr. Baker, the factory inspector, there were 652 factories. 570 of these contained 85,622 power-looms and 6,819,146 spindles (not counting doubling spindles), used 27,439 horse-power (steam) and 1,390 (water), and employed 94,119 persons. In the year 1865 the same factories contained 95,163 looms and 7,025,031 spindles, had a steam-power of 28,925 horses and a water-power of 1,445 horses, and employed 88,913 persons. So between 1860 and 1865 looms increased by 11%, spindles by 3%, and engine-power by 3%, while the number of persons employed decreased by 5½%.[^147] Between 1852 and 1862 the English woolen industry expanded considerably, while the number of workers employed in it stayed almost the same,

In certain cases the increase in the number of workers employed is only apparent. That is, it does not come from the extension of factories already established, but from the gradual takeover of connected trades. For instance, between 1838 and 1856 power-looms increased, and so did the workers who ran them. In the cotton trade this was simply due to the growth of that branch of industry. But in the other trades it was due to steam-power being applied to the carpet-loom, the ribbon-loom, and the linen-loom, which had previously been worked by human muscle.[^149] So the increase of the workers in these latter trades was merely a symptom of a reduction in the total number employed. Finally, we have looked at this question entirely apart from the fact that everywhere except in the metal industries, young persons (under 18), women and children make up the predominant part of the class of factory workers.

[^149]: See “Reports, &c., 31 Oct., 1856,” p. 16.

Nevertheless, despite the mass of workers actually displaced and virtually replaced by machinery, we can understand how factory workers may become more numerous than the manufacturing workers and craftsmen who have been displaced, through the building of more mills and the extension of old ones in a given industry. Suppose, for example, that in the old mode of production a capital of £500 is employed weekly, two-fifths of it constant and three-fifths variable capital. That is, £200 is laid out in means of production, and £300, say £1 per man, in labor-power. When machinery is introduced, the composition of this capital changes. We will suppose it now consists of four-fifths constant and one-fifth variable capital, which means that only £100 is now laid out in labor-power. As a result, two-thirds of the workers are dismissed. If the business now expands, and the total capital employed grows to £1,500 under unchanged conditions, the number of workers employed will increase to 300, just as many as before the machinery was introduced. If the capital grows further to £2,000, 400 men will be employed, or one-third more than under the old system. Their numbers have in fact increased by 100. But relatively, that is, in proportion to the total capital advanced, they have decreased by 800, for under the old conditions the £2,000 capital would have employed 1,200 men instead of 400. So a relative decrease in the number of workers is consistent with an actual increase. We assumed above that while the total capital increases, its composition stays the same, because the conditions of production stay constant. But we have already seen that with every advance in the use of machinery, the constant part of capital increases (the part that consists of machinery, raw material, etc.), while the variable part, the part laid out in labor-power, decreases. We also know that in no other system of production is improvement so continuous, and the composition of the capital employed so constantly changing, as in the factory system. However, these changes are continually interrupted by periods of rest, during which the factories merely expand in quantity on the existing technical basis. During such periods the number of factory workers increases. Thus in 1835 the total number of workers in the cotton, woolen, worsted, flax, and silk factories of the United Kingdom was only 354,684, while in 1861 the power-loom weavers alone (of both sexes and all ages, from eight years up) numbered 230,654. Certainly, this growth looks less important when we consider that in 1838 the hand-loom weavers with their families still numbered 800,000,[^150] not to mention those thrown out of work in Asia and on the Continent of Europe.

As long as the factory system spreads in a given branch of industry at the expense of the old handicrafts or of manufacture, the result is as certain as the result of a battle between an army equipped with breech-loading rifles and one armed with bows and arrows. This first period, in which machinery conquers its field of action, is of decisive importance because of the extraordinary profits it helps to produce. These profits are not only a source of faster accumulation (accumulation of capital: turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows). They also attract into the favored sphere of production a large part of the additional social capital that is constantly being created and is always on the lookout for new investments. The special advantages of this first period of fast and furious activity are felt in every branch of production that machinery invades. But as soon as the factory system has gained a certain breadth of footing and a definite degree of maturity, and especially as soon as its technical basis, machinery, is itself produced by machinery; as soon as coal mining and iron mining, the metal industries, and the means of transport have been revolutionized; as soon, in short, as the general conditions needed for production by the modern industrial system have been established, this mode of production acquires an elasticity. It gains a capacity for sudden expansion by leaps and bounds, which meets no limit except the supply of raw material and the sale of the produce. On the one hand, the immediate effect of machinery is to increase the supply of raw material, in the way, for example, that the cotton gin increased the production of cotton.[^151] On the other hand, the cheapness of the articles produced by machinery, and the improved means of transport and communication, provide the weapons for conquering foreign markets. By ruining handicraft production in other countries, machinery forcibly turns them into fields for supplying its raw material. In this way East India was compelled to produce cotton, wool, hemp, jute, and indigo for Great Britain.[^152] In all countries where it has taken root, modern industry constantly makes a part of the workers “supernumerary” (surplus to need). In this way it spurs emigration and the colonization of foreign lands, which are thereby turned into settlements for growing the raw material of the mother country, just as Australia, for example, was turned into a colony for growing wool.[^153] A new and international division of labor springs up, a division suited to the requirements of the chief centers of modern industry. It turns one part of the globe into a chiefly agricultural field of production, for supplying the other part, which remains a chiefly industrial field. This revolution is bound up with radical changes in agriculture, which we need not look into further here.[^154]

The enormous power of expanding by jumps that is built into the factory system, and that system's dependence on the markets of the world, necessarily give rise to feverish production. This is followed by overfilling of the markets, and then the shrinking of the markets cripples production. The life of modern industry becomes a series of periods of moderate activity, prosperity, overproduction, crisis and stagnation. Machinery makes the employment of the factory workers, and so their conditions of life, uncertain and unstable. Because of these periodic changes of the industrial cycle, this uncertainty and instability become normal. Except in the periods of prosperity, the most furious struggle rages among the capitalists over each one's share of the markets. This share is directly proportional to the cheapness of the product. This struggle breeds rivalry in the use of improved machinery to replace labor-power, and of new methods of production. Besides this, there also comes a time in every industrial cycle when an attempt is made to force wages down below the value of labor-power, in order to cheapen commodities.[^155]

[^155]: In an appeal to the Trade Societies of England made in July, 1866, by the shoemakers of Leicester, who had been thrown on the streets by a lock-out, it is stated: “Twenty years ago the Leicester shoe trade was revolutionised by the introduction of riveting in the place of stitching. At that time good wages could be earned. Great competition was shown between the different firms as to which could turn out the neatest article. Shortly afterwards, however a worse kind of competition sprang up, namely, that of underselling one another in the market. The injurious consequences soon manifested themselves in reductions of wages, and so sweepingly quick was the fall in the price of labour, that many firms now pay only one half of the original wages. And yet, though wages sink lower and lower, profits appear, with each alteration in the scale of wages, to increase.” The manufacturers use even bad times to make exceptional profits by cutting wages excessively, that is, by directly robbing the worker of means of subsistence. One example (it refers to the crisis in the Coventry silk weaving): “From information I have received from manufacturers as well as workmen, there seems to be no doubt that wages have been reduced to a greater extent than either the competition of the foreign producers, or other circumstances have rendered necessary ... the majority of weavers are working at a reduction of 30 to 40 per cent. in their wages. A piece of ribbon for making which the weaver got 6s. or 7s. five years back, now only brings them 3s. 3d. or 3s. 6d.; other work is now priced at 2s. and 2s. 3d. which was formerly priced at 4s. and 4s. 3d. The reduction in wage seems to have been carried to a greater extent than is necessary for increasing demand. Indeed, the reduction in the cost of weaving, in the case of many descriptions of ribbons, has not been accompanied by any corresponding reduction in the selling price of the manufactured article.” (Mr. F. D. Longe’s Report. “Ch. Emp. Com., V. Rep., 1866,” p. 114, 1.)

So a necessary condition for the growth of the number of factory workers is a proportionally much faster growth of the amount of capital invested in mills. This growth, however, depends on the ebb and flow of the industrial cycle. Besides, it is constantly interrupted by technical progress, which at one time virtually takes the place of new workers, and at another actually displaces old ones. This qualitative change in mechanical industry continually discharges workers from the factory, or shuts its doors against the fresh stream of recruits. Meanwhile the purely quantitative extension of the factories absorbs not only the men thrown out of work, but also fresh contingents. So the workers are continually both repelled and attracted, hustled from pillar to post, while at the same time the sex, age, and skill of the recruits constantly change.

The lot of the factory workers will be best shown by a rapid survey of the course of the English cotton industry.

From 1770 to 1815 this trade was depressed or stagnant for 5 years only. During this period of 45 years the English manufacturers had a monopoly of machinery and of the markets of the world. From 1815 to 1821 depression; 1822 and 1823 prosperity; 1824 repeal of the laws against Trades’ Unions, great extension of factories everywhere; 1825 crisis; 1826 great misery and riots among the factory workers; 1827 slight improvement; 1828 great increase in power-looms and in exports; 1829 exports, especially to India, surpass all former years; 1830 glutted markets, great distress; 1831 to 1833 continued depression, the East India Company loses its monopoly of the trade with India and China; 1834 great increase of factories and machinery, shortage of workers. The new poor law encourages the migration of agricultural laborers into the factory districts. The country districts are swept clean of children. White slave trade; 1835 great prosperity, while at the same time the hand-loom weavers starve; 1836 great prosperity; 1837 and 1838 depression and crisis; 1839 revival; 1840 great depression, riots, the military called out; 1841 and 1842 frightful suffering among the factory workers; 1842 the manufacturers lock the workers out of the factories in order to force the repeal of the Corn Laws (the tariffs on imported grain). The factory workers stream in thousands into the towns of Lancashire and Yorkshire, are driven back by the military, and their leaders are brought to trial at Lancaster; 1843 great misery; 1844 revival; 1845 great prosperity; 1846 continued improvement at first, then reaction. Repeal of the Corn Laws; 1847 crisis, general reduction of wages by 10 and more percent in honor of the “big loaf”; 1848 continued depression; Manchester under military protection; 1849 revival; 1850 prosperity; 1851 falling prices, low wages, frequent strikes; 1852 improvement begins, strikes continue, the manufacturers threaten to import foreign workers; 1853 increasing exports. Strike for 8 months, and great misery at Preston; 1854 prosperity, glutted markets; 1855 news of failures streams in from the United States, Canada, and the Eastern markets; 1856 great prosperity; 1857 crisis; 1858 improvement; 1859 great prosperity, increase in factories; 1860 peak of the English cotton trade, the Indian, Australian, and other markets so glutted with goods that even in 1863 they had not absorbed the whole lot; the French Treaty of Commerce, enormous growth of factories and machinery; 1861 prosperity continues for a time, reaction, the American Civil War, cotton famine: 1862 to 1863 complete collapse.

The history of the cotton famine is too characteristic for us not to dwell on it for a moment. From the signs of the state of the world markets in 1860 and 1861, we see that the cotton famine came in the nick of time for the manufacturers, and was to some extent to their advantage. This fact was acknowledged in the reports of the Manchester Chamber of Commerce, proclaimed in Parliament by Palmerston and Derby, and confirmed by events.[^156] No doubt, among the 2,887 cotton mills in the United Kingdom in 1861, there were many small ones. According to the report of Mr. A. Redgrave, out of the 2,109 mills included in his district, 392, or 19%, used less than ten horse-power each; 345, or 16%, used 10 H. P. and less than 20 H. P.; while 1,372 used more than 20 H. P.[^157] Most of the small mills were weaving sheds built during the period of prosperity after 1858, mostly by speculators, one of whom supplied the yarn, another the machinery, a third the buildings. They were run by men who had been overseers, or by other persons of small means. Most of these small manufacturers went under. The same fate would have overtaken them in the commercial crisis that was put off only by the cotton famine. Although they made up one-third of the total number of manufacturers, their mills absorbed a much smaller part of the capital invested in the cotton trade. As to the extent of the stoppage, reliable estimates show that in October 1862, 60.3% of the spindles and 58% of the looms were standing idle. This refers to the cotton trade as a whole, and of course needs considerable adjustment for individual districts. Only very few mills worked full time (60 hours a week); the rest worked at intervals. Even in the few cases where full time was worked, at the usual rate of piece wages (wages paid per piece of work done), the weekly wages of the factory workers necessarily shrank. This was because good cotton was replaced by bad: Sea Island by Egyptian (in fine spinning mills), American and Egyptian by Surat, and pure cotton by mixtures of waste and Surat. The shorter fiber of the Surat cotton and its dirty condition, the greater fragility of the thread, and the use of all sorts of heavy ingredients instead of flour in sizing the warps all had the same effects. They lowered the speed of the machinery, or the number of looms one weaver could tend, increased the labor caused by defects in the machinery, and reduced piece wages by reducing the amount of product turned out. Where Surat cotton was used, the loss to the factory workers on full time amounted to 20, 30, and more percent. But besides this, most of the manufacturers cut the rate of piece wages by 5, 7½, and 10 percent. We can therefore imagine the situation of those workers who were employed for only 3, 3½ or 4 days a week, or for only 6 hours a day. Even in 1863, after a comparative improvement had set in, the weekly wages of spinners and of weavers were 3s. 4d., 3s. 10d., 4s. 6d. and 5s. 1d.[^158] Yet even in this miserable state of things, the inventive spirit of the employer never stood still, but busied itself with making deductions from wages. To some extent these were imposed as a penalty for defects in the finished article that were really due to his bad cotton and his unsuitable machinery. Moreover, where the manufacturer owned the workers' cottages, he paid himself his rents by deducting them from these miserable wages. So in the first 45 years of the English cotton trade, from 1770 to 1815, we find only 5 years of crisis and stagnation; but this was the period of monopoly. The second period, from 1815 to 1863, counts in its 48 years only 20 years of revival and prosperity against 28 of depression and stagnation. In 1858 the official value of cotton goods exported, was £182,221,681; but the real or marketable value was only £43,001,322, being a ten-fold quantity sold for little more than double the former price. To produce results so disadvantageous to the country generally, and to the factory workers in particular, several causes have co-operated, which, had circumstances permitted, we should have brought more prominently under your notice; suffice it for the present to say that the most obvious one is the constant redundancy of labour, without which a trade so ruinous in its effects never could have been carried on, and which requires a constantly extending market to save it from annihilation. Our cotton mills may be brought to a stand by the periodical stagnations of trade, which, under present arrangements, are as inevitable as death itself; but the human mind is constantly at work, and although we believe we are under the mark in stating that six millions of persons have left these shores during the last 25 years, yet, from the natural increase of population, and the displacement of labour to cheapen production, a large percentage of the male adults in the most prosperous times find it impossible to obtain work in factories on any conditions whatever.” (“Reports of Insp. of Fact., 30th April 1863,” pp. 51-52.) In a later chapter we shall see how our friends the manufacturers tried, during the catastrophe in the cotton trade, to prevent the emigration of the factory workers by every means, including State interference.
