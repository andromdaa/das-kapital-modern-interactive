## Chapter 21: Piece Wages

<!-- ch21-p001 -->
Wages by the piece are nothing other than a converted form of wages by time, just as wages by time are a converted form of the value or price of labor-power. (Time wages are wages paid by the hour, day, or week; piece wages are wages paid per piece of work done. Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work.)

<!-- ch21-p002 -->
With piece wages it seems at first sight as if the use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties) bought from the worker were not the functioning of his labor-power, living labor (the labor of workers now at work), but labor already realized in the product. It also seems as if the price of this labor were determined not, as with time wages, by the fraction

<!-- ch21-p003 -->
daily value of labor-power\
the working day of a given number of hours

<!-- ch21-p004 -->
but by the producer's capacity for work.[^1]

[^1]: “The system of piece-work illustrates an epoch in the history of the working-man; it is halfway between the position of the mere day-labourer depending upon the will of the capitalist and the co-operative artisan, who in the not distant future promises to combine the artisan and the capitalist in his own person. Piece-workers are in fact their own masters, even whilst working upon the capital of the employer.” (John Watts: “Trade Societies and Strikes, Machinery and Co-operative Societies.” Manchester, 1865, pp. 52, 53.) I quote this little work because it is a real cesspool of every apologetic commonplace that rotted long ago. This same Mr. Watts once dealt in Owenism (the socialism of Robert Owen), and in 1842 he published another pamphlet, “Facts and Fictions of Political Economists,” in which, among other things, he declares that “property is robbery.” That was long ago.

<!-- ch21-p005 -->
Faith in this appearance ought to get its first severe shock from the fact that both forms of wages exist side by side, at the same time, in the same branches of industry; for example,

<!-- ch21-p006 -->
> “the compositors [typesetters] of London, as a general rule, work by the piece, time-work being the exception, while those in the country work by the day, the exception being work by the piece. The shipwrights of the port of London work by the job or piece, while those of all other parts work by the day.”[^2]

[^2]: T. J. Dunning: “Trades’ Unions and Strikes,” London, 1860, p. 22.

<!-- ch21-p007 -->
In the same saddlery shops of London, often for the same work, the French are paid piece wages and the English time wages. In those regular factories in which piece wages predominate throughout, particular kinds of work are unsuited to this form of wage, and so they are paid by time.[^3] But it is, moreover, self-evident that the difference in the form in which wages are paid alters nothing in their essential nature, although one form may be more favorable to the development of capitalist production than the other.

[^3]: How the existence of these two forms of wage side by side, at the same time, favors the employers' cheating: “A factory employs 400 people, the half of which work by the piece, and have a direct interest in working longer hours. The other 200 are paid by the day, work equally long with the others, and get no more money for their over-time.... The work of these 200 people for half an hour a day is equal to one person’s work for 50 hours, or 5/6’s of one person’s labour in a week, and is a positive gain to the employer.” (“Reports of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1860,” p. 9.) “Over-working to a very considerable extent still prevails; and, in most instances, with that security against detection and punishment which the law itself affords. I have in many former reports shown ... the injury to workpeople who are not employed on piece-work, but receive weekly wages.” (Leonard Horner in “Reports of Insp. of Fact.,” 30th April, 1859, pp. 8, 9.)

<!-- ch21-p008 -->
Let the ordinary working day contain 12 hours, of which 6 are paid and 6 unpaid. (The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor.) Let its value-product be 6 shillings, so that the value-product of one hour's labor is 6d. (The value-product is the new value created by labor in a given period, as distinct from the old value transferred from the means of production.) Let us suppose that experience has shown the following. A worker who works with the average intensity and skill, and who therefore in fact spends only the time socially necessary to produce an article, supplies 24 pieces in 12 hours, either separate products or measurable parts of a continuous whole. (Socially necessary labor-time is the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor.) Then the value of these 24 pieces, after subtracting the part of constant capital contained in them, is 6 shillings, and the value of a single piece is 3d. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production. Its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) The worker receives 1 ½d. per piece, and so earns 3 shillings in 12 hours. With time wages, it makes no difference whether we assume that the worker works 6 hours for himself and 6 hours for the capitalist, or half of every hour for himself and the other half for the capitalist. Just so, here it makes no difference whether we say that each single piece is half paid for and half unpaid, or that the price of 12 pieces is the equivalent only of the value of the labor-power, while surplus-value is embodied in the other 12 pieces. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.)

<!-- ch21-p009 -->
The form of piece wages is just as irrational as that of time wages. In our example, two pieces of a commodity, after subtracting the value of the means of production used up in them, are worth 6d., as the product of one hour. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use. Means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things.) Yet the worker receives a price of 3d. for them. In fact, piece wages do not distinctly express any relation of value. So it is not a question of measuring the value of the piece by the working time embodied in it. On the contrary, it is a question of measuring the working time the worker has spent by the number of pieces he has produced. With time wages, labor is measured by its immediate duration; with piece wages, by the quantity of products in which the labor has embodied itself during a given time.[^4] The price of labor-time itself is finally determined by the equation: value of a day's labor = daily value of labor-power. So piece wages are only a modified form of time wages.

[^4]: “Wages can be measured in two ways: either by the duration of the labour, or by its product.” (“Abrégé élémentaire des principes de l’économie politique.” Paris, 1796, p. 32.) The author of this anonymous work is G. Garnier.

<!-- ch21-p010 -->
Let us now look a little more closely at the characteristic features of piece wages.

<!-- ch21-p011 -->
Here the quality of the labor is controlled by the work itself, which must be of average perfection if the piece-price is to be paid in full. From this point of view, piece wages become the most fruitful source of wage cuts and capitalist cheating.

<!-- ch21-p012 -->
They give the capitalist an exact measure of the intensity of labor. (Intensity of labor is how much labor is packed into a given time, that is, how hard and fast people must work.) Only the working time embodied in an amount of commodities fixed in advance by experiment counts as socially necessary working time, and is paid as such. So in the larger workshops of the London tailors, a certain piece of work, a waistcoat for example, is called an hour, or half an hour, with the hour at 6d. Practice shows how much the average product of one hour is. With new fashions, repairs, etc., a contest arises between employer and worker over whether a particular piece of work is one hour, and so on, until here too experience decides. It is the same in the London furniture workshops, etc. If the worker does not have the average capacity, and so cannot supply a certain minimum of work per day, he is dismissed.[^5]

[^5]: “So much weight of cotton is delivered to him” (the spinner), “and he has to return by a certain time, in lieu of it, a given weight of twist or yarn, of a certain degree of fineness, and he is paid so much per pound for all that he so returns. If his work is defective in quality, the penalty falls on him, if less in quantity than the minimum fixed for a given time, he is dismissed and an abler operative procured.” (Ure, cited above, p. 317.)

<!-- ch21-p013 -->
Since the form of wage itself here controls the quality and intensity of the work, supervision of labor becomes largely superfluous. So piece wages form the basis of the modern “domestic labour” described earlier, and also of a hierarchically organized system of exploitation and oppression. This system has two basic forms. On the one hand, piece wages make it easier for parasites to step in between the capitalist and the wage worker: the “sub-letting of labour.” These middlemen's gain comes entirely from the difference between the labor-price that the capitalist pays and the part of that price that they actually allow to reach the worker.[^6] In England this system is called, characteristically, the “sweating system.” On the other hand, piece wages allow the capitalist to make a contract at so much per piece with the head worker – in manufacturing workshops with the chief of some group, in mines with the man who cuts the coal, in the factory with the actual machine-worker – at a price for which the head worker himself takes on the hiring and paying of his assistants. (Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) Here the exploitation of the worker by capital is carried out through the exploitation of the worker by the worker.[^7]

[^6]: “It is when work passes through several hands, each of which is to take its share of profits, while only the last does the work, that the pay which reaches the workwoman is miserably disproportioned.” (“Child. Emp. Comm. II Report,” p. lxx., n. 424.)

[^7]: Even Watts, the apologist, remarks: “It would be a great improvement to the system of piece-work, if all the men employed on a job were partners in the contract, each according to his abilities, instead of one man being interested in over-working his fellows for his own benefit.” (cited above, p. 53.) On the vileness of this system, compare “Child. Emp. Comm., Rep. III.,” p. 66, n. 22, p. 11, n. 124, p. xi, n. 13, 53, 59, etc.

<!-- ch21-p014 -->
With piece wages, it is naturally in the worker's personal interest to strain his labor-power as intensely as possible. This makes it easier for the capitalist to raise the normal degree of intensity of labor.[^8] It is moreover now in the worker's personal interest to lengthen the working day, since that raises his daily or weekly wages.[^9] This gradually brings on a reaction like the one already described for time wages, not to mention that lengthening the working day, even if the piece wage stays constant, necessarily includes a fall in the price of labor.

[^8]: This spontaneous result is often helped along artificially. For example, in the Engineering Trade of London a customary trick is “the selecting of a man who possesses superior physical strength and quickness, as the principal of several workmen, and paying him an additional rate, by the quarter or otherwise, with the understanding that he is to exert himself to the utmost to induce the others, who are only paid the ordinary wages, to keep up to him ... without any comment this will go far to explain many of the complaints of stinting the action, superior skill, and working-power, made by the employers against the men” (in Trades-Unions. Dunning, cited above, pp. 22, 23). Since the author is himself a worker and secretary of a Trades’ Union, this might be taken for exaggeration. But the reader may compare the “highly respectable” “Cyclopedia of Agriculture” of J. C. Morton, Art., the article “Labourer,” where this method is recommended to the farmers as an approved one.

[^9]: “All those who are paid by piece-work ... profit by the transgression of the legal limits of work. This observation as to the willingness to work over-time is especially applicable to the women employed as weavers and reelers.” (“Rept. of Insp. of Fact., 30th April, 1858,” p. 9.) “This system” (piece-work), “so advantageous to the employer ... tends directly to encourage the young potter greatly to over-work himself during the four or five years during which he is employed in the piece-work system, but at low wages.... This is ... another great cause to which the bad constitutions of the potters are to be attributed.” (“Child. Empl. Comm. 1. Rept.,” p. xiii.)

<!-- ch21-p015 -->
With time wages, with few exceptions, the same wage holds for the same kind of work. With piece wages, on the other hand, although the price of the working time is measured by a certain quantity of product, the day's or week's wage will vary with the individual differences between workers: one supplies only the minimum product in a given time, another the average, a third more than the average. So as regards actual earnings there is great variety, according to the different skill, strength, energy, staying-power, etc., of the individual workers.[^10] Of course this does not alter the general relations between capital and wage labor. First, the individual differences balance one another out in the workshop as a whole, which thus supplies the average product in a given working time, and the total wages paid will be the average wages of that particular branch of industry. Second, the proportion between wages and surplus-value remains unchanged, since the mass of surplus labor supplied by each particular worker corresponds to the wage he receives. (Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.) But the wider scope that piece wages give to individuality tends to develop, on the one hand, that individuality, and with it the workers' sense of liberty, independence, and self-control, and on the other hand, their competition with one another. So piece-work tends, while raising individual wages above the average, to lower this average itself. But where a particular rate of piece wage had long been fixed by tradition, so that lowering it presented special difficulties, the employers in such exceptional cases sometimes resorted to forcibly turning it into time wages. Hence, for example, a great strike among the ribbon-weavers of Coventry in 1860.[^11] Finally, piece wages are one of the main supports of the hour system described in the preceding chapter.[^12]

[^10]: “Where the work in any trade is paid for by the piece at so much per job ... wages may very materially differ in amount.... But in work by the day there is generally an uniform rate ... recognized by both employer and employed as the standard of wages for the general run of workmen in the trade.” (Dunning, cited above, p. 17.)

[^11]: “The work of the journeyman-artisans will be ruled by the day or by the piece. These master-artisans know about how much work a journeyman-artisan can do per day in each craft, and often pay them in proportion to the work which they do; the journey men, therefore, work as much as they can, in their own interest, without any further inspection.” (Cantillon, “Essai sur la Nature du Commerce en général,” Amst. Ed., 1756, pp. 185 and 202. The first edition appeared in 1755.) Cantillon, from whom Quesnay, Sir James Steuart & A. Smith have drawn heavily, already presents piece wages here as simply a modified form of time wages. The French edition of Cantillon claims in its title to be a translation from the English. But the English edition, “The Analysis of Trade, Commerce, &c.,” by Philip Cantillon, late of the city of London, Merchant, is not only of later date (1759), but shows by its contents that it is a later and revised edition. For example, in the French edition Hume is not yet mentioned, while in the English edition, on the other hand, Petty hardly appears any longer. The English edition is less important theoretically, but it contains many details specifically about English trade, the bullion trade, etc., that are missing from the French text. So the words on the title-page of the English edition, according to which the work is “taken chiefly from the manuscript of a very ingenious gentleman, deceased, and adapted, &c.,” seem to be a pure fiction, of a kind very common at that time.

[^12]: “How often have we seen, in some workshops, many more workers recruited than the work actually called for? On many occasions, workers are recruited in anticipation of future work, which may never materialize. Because they are paid by piece wages, it is said that no risk is incurred, since any loss of time will be charged against the unemployed.” (H. Gregoir: “Les Typographes devant le Tribunal correctionnel de Bruxelles,” Brusseles, 1865, p. 9.)

From what has been shown so far, it follows that piece wages are the form of wages most in harmony with the capitalist mode of production. Although they are by no means new – they appear officially, side by side with time wages, in the French and English labor statutes of the 14th century – they only win a larger field of action during the period of manufacture properly so called. In the stormy youth of modern industry, especially from 1797 to 1815, they served as a lever for lengthening the working day and lowering wages. (Modern industry is large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system.) Very important materials on the fluctuation of wages during that period can be found in the Blue books: “Report and Evidence from the Select Committee on Petitions respecting the Corn Laws” (Parliamentary Session of 1813-14), and “Report from the Lords’ Committee, on the State of the Growth, Commerce, and Consumption of Grain, and all Laws relating thereto” (Session of 1814-15). Here we find documentary evidence of the constant lowering of the price of labor from the beginning of the anti-Jacobin War (the war against revolutionary France). In the weaving industry, for example, piece wages had fallen so low that, despite the very great lengthening of the working day, the daily wages were then lower than before.

<!-- ch21-p017 -->
> “The real earnings of the cotton weaver are now far less than they were; his superiority over the common labourer, which at first was very great, has now almost entirely ceased. Indeed... the difference in the wages of skillful and common labour is far less now than at any former period.”[^13]

[^13]: “Remarks on the Commercial Policy of Great Britain,” London, 1815.

<!-- ch21-p018 -->
The following passage, taken from a work that sides with the landlords and farmers, shows how little the greater intensity and extension of labor through piece wages benefited the agricultural proletariat (the proletariat is the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power):

<!-- ch21-p019 -->
> “By far the greater part of agricultural operations is done by people who are hired for the day or on piece-work. Their weekly wages are about 12s., and although it may be assumed that a man earns on piece-work under the greater stimulus to labour, 1s. or perhaps 2s. more than on weekly wages, yet it is found, on calculating his total income, that his loss of employment, during the year, outweighs this gain...Further, it will generally be found that the wages of these men bear a certain proportion to the price of the necessary means of subsistence [the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live], so that a man with two children is able to bring up his family without recourse to parish relief.”[^14]

[^14]: “A Defense of the Landowners and Farmers of Great Britain,” 1814, pp. 4, 5

<!-- ch21-p020 -->
Malthus remarked at that time, referring to the facts published by Parliament:

<!-- ch21-p021 -->
> “I confess that I see, with misgiving, the great extension of the practice of piece-wage. Really hard work during 12 or 14 hours of the day, or for any longer time, is too much for any human being.”[^15]

[^15]: Malthus, “Inquiry into the Nature and Progress of Rent,” London, 1815.

<!-- ch21-p022 -->
In the workshops under the Factory Acts, piece wages become the general rule, because there capital can increase the effectiveness of the working day only by intensifying labor.[^16]

[^16]: “Those who are paid by piece-work ... constitute probably four-fifths of the workers in the factories.” “Report of Insp. of Fact.,” 30th April, 1858.

<!-- ch21-p023 -->
As the productivity of labor changes, the same amount of product represents a varying working time. (Productivity of labor is how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time.) So the piece wage varies too, for it is the money expression of a definite working time. In our example above, 24 pieces were produced in 12 hours, while the value of the product of the 12 hours was 6s., the daily value of the labor-power 3s., the price of the labor-hour 3d., and the wage for one piece ½d. One piece absorbed half an hour's labor. Now suppose that the same working day supplies 48 pieces instead of 24, because the productivity of labor has doubled, and that all other circumstances remain unchanged. Then the piece wage falls from 1 ½d. to 3/4d., as every piece now represents only 1/4 of a working-hour, instead of ½. 24 by 1½d. = 3s., and in the same way 48 by 3/4d. = 3s. In other words, the piece wage is lowered in the same proportion as the number of pieces produced in the same time rises,[^17] and so in the same proportion as the working time spent on the same piece falls. This change in the piece wage, so far purely nominal, leads to constant battles between capitalist and labor. The battles arise either because the capitalist uses it as a pretext for actually lowering the price of labor, or because the increased productive power of labor is accompanied by an increased intensity of labor. Or they arise because the worker takes the appearance of piece wages seriously (namely, that his product is paid for, and not his labor-power), and so revolts against a lowering of wages that is not accompanied by a lowering of the selling price of the commodity.

[^17]: “The productive power of his spinning-machine is accurately measured, and the rate of pay for work done with it decreases with, though not as, the increase of its productive power.” (Ure, cited above, p. 317.) Ure himself cancels this last apologetic phrase again. The lengthening of the mule causes some increase of labor, he admits. So the labor does not diminish in the same ratio as its productivity increases. Further: “By this increase the productive power of the machine will be augmented one-fifth. When this event happens the spinner will not be paid at the same rate for work done as he was before, but as that rate will not be diminished in the ratio of one-fifth, the improvement will augment his money earnings for any given number of hours’ work,” but “the foregoing statement requires a certain modification.... The spinner has to pay something additional for juvenile aid out of his additional sixpence, accompanied by displacing a portion of adults” (cited above, p. 321), which in no way tends to raise wages.

<!-- ch21-p024 -->
> “The operatives...carefully watch the price of the raw material and the price of manufactured goods, and are thus enabled to form an accurate estimate of their master’s profits.”[^18]

[^18]: H. Fawcett: “The Economic Position of the British labourer.” Cambridge and London, 1865, p. 178.

The capitalist rightly knocks such pretensions on the head as gross errors about the nature of wage labor.[^19] He cries out against this usurping attempt to lay taxes on the advance of industry, and declares flatly that the productivity of labor does not concern the worker at all.[^20]

[^19]: In the “London Standard” of October 26, 1861, there is a report of proceedings by the firm of John Bright & Co. before the Rochdale magistrates “to prosecute for intimidation the agents of the Carpet Weavers Trades’ Union. Bright’s partners had introduced new machinery which would turn out 240 yards of carpet in the time and with the labour (!) previously required to produce 160 yards. The workmen had no claim whatever to share in the profits made by the investment of their employer’s capital in mechanical improvements. Accordingly, Messrs. Bright proposed to lower the rate of pay from 1½d. per yard to 1d., leaving the earnings of the men exactly the same as before for the same labour. But there was a nominal reduction, of which the operatives, it is asserted, had not fair warning beforehand.”

[^20]: “Trades’ Unions, in their desire to maintain wages, endeavor to share in the benefits of improved machinery.” (Quelle horreur! [How horrible!]) “... the demanding higher wages, because labour is abbreviated, is in other words the endeavor to establish a duty on mechanical improvements.” (“On Combination of Trades,” new ed., London, 1834, p. 42.)
