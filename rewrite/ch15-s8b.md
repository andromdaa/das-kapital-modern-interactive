#### E. Passage of Modern Manufacture, and Domestic Industry into Modern Mechanical Industry. The Hastening of this Revolution by the Application of the Factory Acts to those Industries

<!-- ch15-p223 -->
The cheapening of labor-power, by sheer abuse of the labor of women and children, by sheer robbery of every normal condition needed for working and living, and by the sheer brutality of overwork and night-work, at last runs into natural obstacles that cannot be crossed. So do the cheapening of commodities and capitalist exploitation in general, when they are based on these methods. As soon as this point is at last reached – and it takes many years – the hour has struck for the introduction of machinery, and for the rapid conversion, from then on, of the scattered domestic industries and also of branches of manufacture into factory industries.

<!-- ch15-p224 -->
The production of clothing offers an example of this movement on the most colossal scale. According to the classification of the Children’s Employment Commission, this industry includes straw-hat makers, ladies’-hat makers, cap-makers, tailors, milliners and dressmakers, shirt-makers, corset-makers, glove-makers, shoemakers, and many minor branches as well, such as the making of neck-ties, collars, etc. In 1861, the number of females employed in these industries in England and Wales came to 586,299. Of these, at least 115,242 were under 20, and 16,650 under 15 years of age. The number of these women workers in the United Kingdom in 1861 was 750,334. The number of males employed in England and Wales in hat-making, shoemaking, glove-making and tailoring was 437,969. Of these, 14,964 were under 15 years, 89,285 between 15 and 20, and 333,117 over 20 years. Many of the smaller branches are not included in these figures. But take the figures as they stand. Then, according to the census of 1861, we have for England and Wales alone a total of 1,024,277 persons, about as many as are absorbed by agriculture and cattle breeding. We begin to understand what becomes of the immense quantities of goods conjured up by the magic of machinery, and of the enormous masses of workers that this machinery sets free.

<!-- ch15-p225 -->
The production of clothing is carried on partly in manufacturing workshops whose workrooms merely reproduce that division of labor whose membra disjecta [scattered limbs] were found ready to hand. It is carried on partly by small master craftsmen. These, however, no longer work for individual consumers, as they used to, but for manufacturing workshops and warehouses, and to such an extent that whole towns and stretches of country often carry on certain branches, such as shoemaking, as a specialty. Finally, it is carried on, on a very great scale, by the so-called domestic workers, who form an outside department of the manufacturing workshops, the warehouses, and even the workshops of the smaller employers.[^185]

[^185]: In England, millinery and dressmaking are for the most part carried on at the employer’s premises, partly by women workers who live there, partly by women who live elsewhere.

<!-- ch15-p226 -->
The raw material, etc., is supplied by mechanical industry. The mass of cheap human material (taillable à merci et miséricorde [taxable at will and at mercy]) is made up of the individuals “liberated” by mechanical industry and improved agriculture. The manufacturing workshops of this kind owed their origin chiefly to the capitalist’s need to have at hand an army, fully equipped, ready to meet any increase in demand.[^186] All the same, these manufacturing workshops allowed the scattered handicrafts and domestic industries to go on existing as a broad foundation. The great production of surplus-value in these branches of labor, and the steady cheapening of their articles, were and are due chiefly to the minimum wages paid, no more than is needed to vegetate miserably, and to the stretching of working time to the maximum that the human organism can endure. It was in fact by the cheapness of human sweat and human blood, turned into commodities, that the markets were constantly being extended, and are still extended every day. This was especially the case with England’s colonial markets, where English tastes and habits also prevail. At last the critical point was reached. The basis of the old method – sheer brutality in exploiting the workers, accompanied more or less by a systematic division of labor – was no longer enough for the expanding markets and for the even faster-expanding competition among the capitalists. The hour struck for the coming of machinery. The decisively revolutionary machine is the sewing-machine: the machine that attacks equally the whole of the countless branches of this sphere of production, dressmaking, tailoring, shoemaking, sewing, hat-making, and many others.

[^186]: Mr. White, a commissioner, visited a military clothing manufacturing workshop that employed 1,000 to 1,200 persons, almost all females, and a shoe manufacturing workshop with 1,300 persons. Of these, nearly one half were children and young persons.

<!-- ch15-p227 -->
Its immediate effect on the workers is like that of all machinery that has seized on new branches of trade since the rise of modern industry. Children who are too young are cast adrift. The wages of the machine workers rise compared with those of the home workers, many of whom belong to the poorest of the poor. The wages of the better-placed craftsman, with whom the machine competes, sink. The new machine workers are exclusively girls and young women. With the help of mechanical force, they destroy the monopoly that male labor had over the heavier work, and they drive many old women and very young children out of the lighter work. The overpowering competition crushes the weakest of the manual workers. The fearful increase in deaths from starvation in London during the last 10 years runs parallel with the spread of machine sewing.[^187] The new women workers turn the machines by hand and foot, or by hand alone, sometimes sitting, sometimes standing, according to the weight, size, and special make of the machine, and they spend a great deal of labor-power. Their occupation is unhealthy because of the long hours, although in most cases these are not as long as under the old system. Wherever the sewing-machine settles into narrow, already overcrowded workrooms, it adds to the unhealthy influences.

[^187]: An example. The Registrar-General’s weekly report of deaths dated 26th Feb., 1864, contains 5 cases of death from starvation. On the same day *The Times* reports another case. Six victims of starvation in one week!

“The effect,” says Mr. Lord, “on entering low-ceiled workrooms in which 30 to 40 machine hands are working is unbearable.... The heat, partly due to the gas stoves used for warming the irons, is horrible.... Even when moderate hours of work, i.e., from 8 in the morning till 6 in the evening, prevail in such places, yet 3 or 4 persons fall into a swoon [faint] regularly every day.”[^188]

The revolution in industrial methods, which is the necessary result of the revolution in the instruments of production, is carried out through a medley of transition forms. These forms vary according to how far the sewing-machine has spread in one branch of industry or another, how long it has been in operation, the previous condition of the workers, whether manufacture, handicrafts or domestic industry predominates, the rent of the workrooms,[^189] etc. In dressmaking, for instance, the labor was for the most part already organized, chiefly by simple cooperation, and there the sewing-machine at first was merely a new factor in that manufacturing industry. In tailoring, shirtmaking, shoemaking, etc., all the forms are mixed together. Here we find the factory system proper. There, middlemen receive the raw material from the capitalist in chief, and group from 10 to 50 or more women workers around their sewing-machines, in “chambers” and “garrets.” Finally, as is always the case with machinery when it is not organized into a system and can also be used on a dwarfish scale, craftsmen and domestic workers, along with their families, or with a little extra labor from outside, use their own sewing-machines.[^190] The system actually prevalent in England is this: the capitalist concentrates a large number of machines on his premises, and then distributes the product of those machines among the domestic workers for further working up.[^191] The variety of the transition forms, however, does not hide the tendency toward conversion into the factory system proper. This tendency is fed by the very nature of the sewing-machine, whose many uses push forward the concentration, under one roof and one management, of branches of a trade that used to be separate. It is also favored by the fact that preparatory needlework, and certain other operations, are most conveniently done on the premises where the machine is at work. It is favored, too, by the inevitable expropriation (taking away people’s property, here their means of production) of the hand sewers, and of the domestic workers who work with their own machines. This fate has already in part overtaken them. The constantly increasing amount of capital invested in sewing-machines[^192] spurs on the production of machine-made articles and gluts the markets with them, and so gives the domestic workers the signal to sell their machines. The overproduction of the sewing-machines themselves causes their producers, badly in need of sales, to rent them out at so much a week, and so to crush the small owners of machines by their deadly competition.[^193] Constant changes in the construction of the machines, and their ever-increasing cheapness, make the older makes lose value day by day. This lets them be sold in great numbers, at absurd prices, to large capitalists, who alone can thus use them at a profit. Finally, here as in all similar revolutions, the replacement of man by the steam-engine gives the finishing blow. At first, the use of steam power meets with merely technical difficulties, such as unsteadiness in the machines, difficulty in controlling their speed, rapid wear and tear of the lighter machines, etc., all of which experience soon overcomes.[^194] If, on the one hand, the concentration of many machines in large manufacturing workshops leads to the use of steam power, on the other hand the competition of steam with human muscles hastens the concentration of workers and machines in large factories. Thus England is at present experiencing the conversion of manufacture, of handicrafts, and of domestic work into the factory system. It is experiencing this not only in the colossal industry of making clothing, but in most of the other trades mentioned above. And this comes after each of those forms of production, totally changed and disorganized under the influence of modern industry, has long since reproduced, and even outdone, all the horrors of the factory system, without sharing in any of the elements of social progress that the factory system contains.[^195]

This industrial revolution, which takes place spontaneously, is artificially helped along by the extension of the Factory Acts to all industries in which women, young persons and children are employed. The compulsory regulation of the working day – its length, its pauses, its beginning and end – the system of relays of children, the exclusion of all children under a certain age, etc., make necessary, on the one hand, more machinery[^196] and the replacement of muscles by steam as the motive power.[^197] On the other hand, to make up for the lost time, the means of production used in common expand: the furnaces, buildings, etc. In one word, there is a greater concentration of the means of production and a correspondingly greater gathering of workers. In fact, the chief objection, urged again and again and passionately on behalf of each manufacture threatened with the Factory Act, is this: that in order to continue the business on the old scale, a greater outlay of capital will be necessary. But as for labor in the so-called domestic industries and in the intermediate forms between them and manufacture, as soon as limits are put on the working day and on the employment of children, those industries go to the wall. Unlimited exploitation of cheap labor-power is the sole foundation of their power to compete.

Acts is to force on the further introduction of machinery” (cited above, pp. 13-14).

One of the essential conditions for the existence of the factory system, especially when the length of the working day is fixed, is certainty in the result, that is, the production of a given quantity of commodities, or of a given useful effect, in a given time. The statutory pauses in the working day, moreover, assume that periodic and sudden stopping of the work does no harm to the article in the process of production. This certainty in the result, and this possibility of interrupting the work, are of course easier to achieve in the purely mechanical industries than in those in which chemical and physical processes play a part, as, for instance, in the earthenware trade, in bleaching, dyeing, baking, and in most of the metal industries. Wherever there is a working day with no limit on its length, wherever there is night-work and unrestricted waste of human life, there the slightest obstacle that the nature of the work puts in the way of a change for the better is soon looked on as an everlasting barrier set up by Nature. No poison kills vermin more surely than the Factory Act removes such everlasting barriers. No one made a greater outcry over “impossibilities” than our friends the earthenware manufacturers. In 1864, however, they were brought under the Act, and within sixteen months every “impossibility” had vanished.

In spite of every prophecy, the cost-price of earthenware did not rise, but the quantity produced did, and to such an extent that exports for the twelve months ending December, 1865, exceeded the average of the preceding three years in value by £138,628. In the manufacture of matches it was thought to be an indispensable requirement that boys, even while gulping down their dinner, should go on dipping the matches in melted phosphorus, whose poisonous vapor rose into their faces. The Factory Act (1864) made saving time a necessity, and so forced into existence a dipping machine whose vapor could not come into contact with the workers.[^199] In the same way, at the present time, in those branches of the lace manufacture not yet subject to the Factory Act, it is maintained that meal-times cannot be regular, because the various kinds of lace need different periods to dry, and these periods vary from three minutes up to an hour and more. To this the Children’s Employment Commissioners answer:

Clearly the English legislature – which certainly no one will dare to reproach with an overdose of genius – has been led by experience to the conclusion that a simple compulsory law is enough to enact away all the so-called impediments, opposed by the nature of the process, to the restriction and regulation of the working day. Hence, when the Factory Act is introduced into a given industry, a period varying from six to eighteen months is fixed, within which the manufacturers are obliged to remove all technical impediments to the working of the Act. Mirabeau’s “Impossible! ne me dites jamais ce bête de mot!” [Impossible! Never say that stupid word to me!] applies particularly well to modern technology. But though the Factory Acts thus artificially ripen the material elements needed for the conversion of the manufacturing system into the factory system, at the same time, because they make a greater outlay of capital necessary, they hasten the decline of the small employers and the concentration of capital.[^202]

[^202]: “But it must be borne in mind that those improvements, though carried out fully in some establishments, are by no means general, and are not capable of being brought into use in many of the old manufactories without an expenditure of capital beyond the means of many of the present occupiers.” “I cannot but rejoice,” writes Sub-Inspector May, “that notwithstanding the temporary disorganisation which inevitably follows the introduction of such a measure (as the Factory Act Extension Act), and is, indeed, directly indicative of the evils which it was intended to remedy, &c.” (Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1865.)

Besides the purely technical impediments that can be removed by technical means, the irregular habits of the workers themselves obstruct the regulation of the hours of labor. This is especially the case where piece wages predominate, and where time lost in one part of the day or week can be made good by later overtime or by night-work, a process that brutalizes the adult worker and ruins his wife and children.[^203] Although this lack of regularity in the expenditure of labor-power is a natural and crude reaction against the tedium of monotonous drudgery, it also originates, to a much greater degree, in anarchy in production, an anarchy that in its turn presupposes unbridled exploitation of labor-power by the capitalist. Besides the general periodic changes of the industrial cycle, and the special fluctuations in the markets to which each industry is subject, we may also count what is called “the season.” It depends either on the periodic return of the times of year that favor navigation, or on fashion and the sudden placing of large orders that have to be carried out in the shortest possible time. The habit of giving such orders becomes more frequent with the extension of railways and telegraphs.

In those factories and manufacturing workshops that are not yet subject to the Factory Acts, the most fearful overwork prevails periodically during what is called the season, as a result of sudden orders. In the outside department of the factory, of the manufacturing workshop, and of the warehouse, the so-called domestic workers, whose employment is irregular at the best of times, depend entirely for their raw material and their orders on the whims of the capitalist. In this industry the capitalist is not held back by any concern about the depreciation of his buildings and machinery, and he risks nothing by a stoppage of work except the skin of the worker himself. Here, then, he sets to work systematically to form an industrial reserve force that shall be ready at a moment’s notice. During one part of the year he decimates this force by the most inhuman toil; during the other part, he lets it starve for lack of work.

In the same way as technical impediments, those “usages which have grown with the growth of trade” were, and still are, proclaimed by interested capitalists to be obstacles due to the nature of the work. This was a favorite cry of the cotton lords at the time when they were first threatened with the Factory Acts. Although their industry depends on navigation more than any other, experience has proved them false. Since then, the Factory inspectors have treated every pretended obstruction to business as a mere sham.[^207] The thoroughly conscientious investigations of the Children’s Employment Commission prove the following: that the effect of regulating the hours of work, in some industries, was to spread the mass of labor previously employed more evenly over the whole year;[^208] that this regulation was the first rational bridle on the murderous, meaningless whims of fashion,[^209] whims that fit so badly with the system of modern industry; that the development of ocean navigation and of the means of communication generally has swept away the technical basis on which season-work really rested;[^210] and that all other so-called unconquerable difficulties vanish before larger buildings, additional machinery, an increase in the number of workers employed,[^211] and the changes that all these cause in the way the wholesale trade is conducted.[^212] But for all that, capital never becomes reconciled to such changes – and its own representatives admit this over and over again – except “under the pressure of a General Act of Parliament”[^213] for the compulsory regulation of the hours of labor.
