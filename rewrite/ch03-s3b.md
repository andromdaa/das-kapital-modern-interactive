#### C. Universal Money

<!-- ch03-p094 -->
When money leaves the home sphere of circulation, it strips off the local costumes it wears there: those of a standard of price, of coin, of tokens, and of a symbol of value. It goes back to its original form of bullion. In trade between the markets of the world, the value of commodities is expressed in a way that is recognized everywhere. So in these cases their independent form of value also confronts them in the shape of world money (gold and silver used as money between nations). Only in the markets of the world does money take on, to the full extent, the character of the commodity whose bodily form is also the direct social incarnation of abstract human labor. In this sphere, the real way money exists fully matches its ideal concept.

Within the sphere of home circulation, there can be only one commodity that becomes money by serving as the measure of value. In the markets of the world, a double measure of value rules: gold and silver.[^59]

[^59]: Hence the absurdity of every law that orders the banks of a country to form their reserves only of the precious metal that circulates at home. The “pleasant difficulties” that the Bank of England created for itself in this way are well known. On the great epochs in the history of the changes in the relative value of gold and silver, see Karl Marx, cited above, p. 136 and following. Sir Robert Peel, with his Bank Act of 1844, tried to get around the difficulty by allowing the Bank of England to issue notes against silver bullion, on condition that the reserve of silver should never exceed more than one-fourth of the reserve of gold. For that purpose, the value of silver was to be estimated at its price in the London market.

    *Added in the 4th German edition. —* [We find ourselves once more in a period of serious change in the relative values of gold and silver. About 25 years ago the ratio expressing the relative value of gold and silver was 15-1/2:1; now it is approximately 22:1, and silver is still falling steadily against gold. This is essentially the result of a revolution in the mode of production of both metals. Formerly gold was obtained almost entirely by washing it out of gold-bearing alluvial deposits, which are products of the weathering of gold-bearing rocks. Now this method has become inadequate. It has been pushed into the background by the processing of the quartz lodes themselves, a way of extraction that used to be only of secondary importance, although the ancients knew it well (Diodorus, III, 12-14) (Diodor’s v. Sicilien “Historische Bibliothek,” book III, 12-14. Stuttgart 1828, pp. 258-261). Moreover, not only were huge new silver deposits discovered in North America, in the western part of the Rocky Mountains, but these and the Mexican silver mines were really opened up by the building of railways. The railways made it possible to ship modern machinery and fuel, and so to mine silver on a very large scale at a low cost. However, there is a great difference in the way the two metals occur in the quartz lodes. The gold is mostly native (found as pure metal), but scattered through the quartz in tiny quantities. So the whole mass of the vein must be crushed, and the gold either washed out or extracted with mercury. Often 1,000,000 grams of quartz barely yield 1-3, and very seldom 30-60, grams of gold. Silver is seldom found native. But it occurs in special quartz that can be separated from the lode fairly easily and mostly contains 40-90% silver; or it is contained, in smaller quantities, in copper, lead and other ores that are worth working in themselves. From this alone it is clear that the labor spent on producing gold is rather increasing, while the labor spent on producing silver has decidedly decreased. This quite naturally explains the drop in the value of silver. This fall in value would show itself in a still greater fall in price, if the price of silver were not pegged even today by artificial means. But America’s rich silver deposits have so far barely been tapped, and so the prospects are that the value of this metal will keep on dropping for rather a long time to come. A still greater contributing factor here is the relative decrease in the need for silver for articles of general use and for luxuries, that is, its replacement by plated goods, aluminum, etc. From this one may judge how utopian the bimetallist idea is that compulsory international quotation will raise silver back to the old value ratio of 1:15-1/2. It is more likely that silver will lose its function as money more and more in the markets of the world. — *F E.*]

World money serves as the universal means of payment, as the universal means of purchase, and as the universally recognized embodiment of all wealth. Its chief function is as a means of payment in settling international balances. Hence the watchword of the mercantilists: balance of trade.[^60] Gold and silver serve as international means of purchase chiefly, and necessarily, in those periods when the usual balance in the exchange of products between different nations is suddenly upset. And lastly, world money serves as the universally recognized embodiment of social wealth whenever the question is not one of buying or paying, but of transferring wealth from one country to another. It does so whenever this transfer cannot be made in the form of commodities, either because of special conditions in the markets or because of the very purpose the transfer is meant to serve.[^61]

[^60]: Even the opponents of the mercantile system entirely misunderstood the functions of world money. (The mercantile system regarded the settlement of trade surpluses in gold and silver as the aim of international trade.) I have shown, using the example of Ricardo, how their false idea of the laws that regulate the quantity of the medium of circulation is reflected in their equally false idea of the international movement of the precious metals (cited above, pp. 150 and following). His mistaken dogma: “An unfavourable balance of trade never arises but from a redundant currency.... The exportation of the coin is caused by its cheapness, and is not the effect, but the cause of an unfavourable balance,” already occurs in Barbon: “The Balance of Trade, if there be one, is not the cause of sending away the money out of a nation; but that proceeds from the difference of the value of bullion in every country.” (N. Barbon; cited above, pp. 59, 60.) MacCulloch, in “The Literature of Political Economy, a classified catalogue, Lond. 1845,” praises Barbon for anticipating this, but prudently passes over the naïve forms in which Barbon dresses up the absurd assumption on which the “currency principle” is based. The lack of real criticism, and even of honesty, in that catalogue reaches its peak in the sections on the history of the theory of money. The reason is that in this part of the work MacCulloch is flattering Lord Overstone, whom he calls “facile princeps argentanorum” [easily the first of the money-men].

Just as every country needs a reserve of money for its home circulation, it also needs one for external circulation in the markets of the world. So the functions of hoards arise partly out of money’s function as the medium of home circulation and home payments, and partly out of its function as world money.[^62] For this last function, the genuine money-commodity, actual gold and silver, is necessary. That is why Sir James Steuart, to distinguish gold and silver from their purely local substitutes, calls them “money of the world.”

[^62]: “I would desire, indeed, no more convincing evidence of the competency of the machinery of the hoards in specie-paying [coin-paying] countries to perform every necessary office of international adjustment, without any sensible aid from the general circulation, than the facility with which France, when but just recovering from the shock of a destructive foreign invasion, completed within the space of 27 months the payment of her forced contribution of nearly 20 millions to the allied powers, and a considerable proportion of the sum in specie, without any perceptible contraction or derangement of her domestic currency, or even any alarming fluctuation of her exchanges.” (Fullerton, cited above, p. 141.) [*Added in the 4th German edition. —* We have a still more striking example in the ease with which the same France was able in 1871-73 to pay off, within 30 months, a forced contribution more than ten times as great, a considerable part of it likewise in specie. *— F. E.*]

The stream of gold and silver flows in two directions. On the one hand, it spreads out from its sources over all the markets of the world. There it is absorbed, to various extents, into the different national spheres of circulation: it fills the channels of currency, replaces worn gold and silver coins, supplies the material for luxury articles, and hardens into hoards.[^63] This first current is set going by the countries that exchange their labor, realized in commodities, for the labor embodied in the precious metals by the gold- and silver-producing countries. On the other hand, gold and silver flow continually back and forth between the different national spheres of circulation, a current whose motion depends on the ceaseless ups and downs of the exchange rate.[^64]

Countries in which the bourgeois form of production is developed to a certain extent limit the hoards concentrated in the strong rooms of the banks to the minimum needed to carry out their particular functions properly.[^65] Whenever these hoards are strikingly above their average level, this is, with some exceptions, a sign of stagnation in the circulation of commodities, of a break in the even flow of their metamorphoses.[^66]

[^65]: These various functions are liable to come into dangerous conflict with one another whenever gold and silver also have to serve as a fund for converting bank-notes.

[^66]: “What money is more than of absolute necessity for a Home Trade, is dead stock ... and brings no profit to that country it’s kept in, but as it is transported in trade, as well as imported.” (John Bellers, “Essays,” p. 13.) “What if we have too much coin? We may melt down the heaviest and turn it into the splendour of plate, vessels or utensils of gold or silver, or send it out as a commodity, where the same is wanted or desired; or let it out at interest, where interest is high.” (W. Petty: “Quantulumcunque,” p. 39.) “Money is but the fat of the Body Politick, whereof too much doth as often hinder its agility, as too little makes it sick ... as fat lubricates the motion of the muscles, feeds in want of victuals [food], fills up the uneven cavities, and beautifies the body; so doth money in the state quicken its action, feeds from abroad in time of dearth [scarcity] at home, evens accounts ... and beautifies the whole; altho more especially the particular persons that have it in plenty.” (W. Petty, “Political Anatomy of Ireland,” p. 14.)
