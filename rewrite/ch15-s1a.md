## Chapter 15: Machinery and Modern Industry

### Section 1 : The Development of Machinery

<!-- ch15-p001 -->
John Stuart Mill says in his “Principles of Political Economy” (political economy is the science of economics as it was then called):

<!-- ch15-p002 -->
> “It is questionable if all the mechanical inventions yet made have lightened the day’s toil of any human being.”[^1]

[^1]: Mill should have said “of any human being not fed by other people’s labor,” for machinery has, without doubt, greatly increased the number of well-to-do idlers.

<!-- ch15-p003 -->
But that is by no means the aim of the capitalist use of machinery. Like every other increase in the productivity of labor, machinery is meant to make commodities cheaper and, by shortening the part of the working day in which the worker works for himself, to lengthen the other part, the part that he gives to the capitalist without an equivalent. In short, it is a means of producing surplus-value. (The productivity of labor is how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time. A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use. The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor. Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.)

<!-- ch15-p004 -->
In manufacture, the revolution in the mode of production begins with labor-power; in modern industry, it begins with the instruments of labor. (Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop. Modern industry is large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) So our first question is: how are the instruments of labor turned from tools into machines? Or, what is the difference between a machine and the implements of a handicraft? We are concerned here only with striking and general characteristics. For epochs in the history of society are no more separated from each other by hard and fast dividing lines than geological epochs are.

<!-- ch15-p005 -->
Mathematicians and specialists in mechanics, followed in this by a few English economists, call a tool a simple machine and a machine a complex tool. They see no essential difference between them. They even give the name of machine to the simple mechanical powers: the lever, the inclined plane, the screw, the wedge, etc.[^2] In fact, every machine is a combination of those simple powers, however they may be disguised. From the economic point of view this explanation is worth nothing, because it lacks the historical element. Another explanation of the difference between tool and machine is this: with a tool, man is the motive power, while the motive power of a machine is something other than man, for example an animal, water, wind, and so on.[^3] On this view, a plow drawn by oxen, a device common to the most different epochs, would be a machine. Claussen's circular loom, on the other hand, which is worked by a single worker and weaves 96,000 picks per minute, would be a mere tool. Indeed, this very loom would be a tool when worked by hand, but a machine if worked by steam. And since the use of animal power is one of man's earliest inventions, production by machinery would have come before production by handicrafts. When John Wyatt brought out his spinning machine in 1735, and began the industrial revolution of the 18th century, he did not say a word about a donkey driving it instead of a man. And yet this part fell to the donkey. He described it as a machine “to spin without fingers.”[^4]

[^2]: See, for example, Hutton: “Course of Mathematics.”

[^3]: “From this point of view we may draw a sharp line of distinction between a tool and a machine: spades, hammers, chisels, &c., combinations of levers and of screws, in all of which, no matter how complicated they may be in other respects, man is the motive power, ... all this falls under the idea of a tool; but the plough, which is drawn by animal power, and wind-mills, &c., must be classed among machines.” (Wilhelm Schulz: “Die Bewegung der Produktion.” Zürich, 1843, p. 38.) In many respects a book worth recommending.

[^4]: Spinning machines, though very imperfect ones, had already been used before his time, and Italy was probably the country where they first appeared. A critical history of technology would show how little any of the inventions of the 18th century is the work of a single individual. So far there is no such book. Darwin has interested us in the history of Nature's Technology, that is, in the formation of the organs of plants and animals, organs that serve as instruments of production for sustaining life. Does not the history of the productive organs of man, organs that are the material basis of all social organization, deserve equal attention? And would not such a history be easier to compile, since, as Vico says, human history differs from natural history in this: we have made the former, but not the latter? Technology reveals how man deals with Nature, the process of production by which he sustains his life. In doing so it also lays bare how his social relations are formed, and how the mental conceptions that flow from them are formed. Even every history of religion that fails to take account of this material basis is uncritical. It is, in reality, much easier to discover by analysis the earthly core of the misty creations of religion than to do the reverse: to develop, from the actual relations of life, the heavenly forms that correspond to those relations. This second method is the only materialist, and therefore the only scientific, one. The weak points in the abstract materialism of natural science, a materialism that leaves out history and its process, show at once in the abstract and ideological conceptions of its spokesmen whenever they venture beyond the bounds of their own specialty.

<!-- ch15-p006 -->
All fully developed machinery consists of three essentially different parts: the motor mechanism, the transmitting mechanism, and finally the tool or working machine. The motor mechanism is what sets the whole in motion. Either it generates its own motive power, like the steam-engine, the caloric (hot-air) engine, the electromagnetic machine, etc., or it is driven by some natural force that already exists, as the water-wheel is by a head of water, the wind-mill by wind, etc. The transmitting mechanism is made up of fly-wheels, shafting, toothed wheels, pulleys, straps, ropes, bands, pinions, and gearing of the most varied kinds. It regulates the motion, changes its form where necessary, for example from straight-line to circular, and divides and distributes it among the working machines. These first two parts of the whole mechanism are there only to set the working machines in motion, and by means of this motion the object of labor is seized and changed as desired. The tool or working machine is the part of the machinery with which the industrial revolution of the 18th century started. And to this day it constantly serves as such a starting-point whenever a handicraft, or a manufacture, is turned into an industry carried on by machinery.

<!-- ch15-p007 -->
If we look more closely at the working machine proper, we find in it, as a general rule, though often, no doubt, in very altered forms, the apparatus and tools used by the craftsman or by the worker in manufacture. The difference is that instead of being human implements, they are the implements of a mechanism, or mechanical implements. Either the entire machine is only a more or less altered mechanical edition of the old handicraft tool, as with the power-loom,[^5] or the working parts fitted into the frame of the machine are old acquaintances, as spindles are in a mule, needles in a stocking-loom, saws in a sawing-machine, and knives in a chopping machine. The distinction between these tools and the body proper of the machine exists from their very birth. For the tools mostly continue to be produced by handicraft or by manufacture, and are fitted afterwards into the body of the machine, which is the product of machinery.[^6] The machine proper is therefore a mechanism that, once set in motion, performs with its tools the same operations that the worker formerly performed with similar tools. Whether the motive power comes from man or from some other machine makes no difference in this respect. From the moment the tool proper is taken from man and fitted into a mechanism, a machine takes the place of a mere implement. The difference strikes one at once, even in cases where man himself remains the prime mover. The number of implements that he himself can use at the same time is limited by the number of his own natural instruments of production, by the number of his bodily organs. In Germany they tried at first to make one spinner work two spinning-wheels, that is, to work with both hands and both feet at the same time. This was too difficult. Later a treadle spinning-wheel with two spindles was invented, but skilled spinners who could spin two threads at once were almost as rare as two-headed men. The Jenny, on the other hand, spun with 12-18 spindles even at its very birth, and the stocking-loom knits with many thousand needles at once. From the very first, the number of tools that a machine can bring into play at the same time is freed from the organic limits that hem in the tools of a craftsman.

Examples of machines for making these mechanical tools are the automatic bobbin-making engine, the cardsetting engine, shuttle-making machines, and machines for forging mule and throstle spindles.

In many hand implements the distinction between man as mere motive power and man as the worker or operator properly so called stands out in striking contrast. For example, the foot is merely the prime mover of the spinning-wheel, while the hand, working with the spindle, drawing and twisting, performs the real operation of spinning. It is this last part of the craftsman's implement that the industrial revolution seizes first. It leaves the worker, in addition to his new labor of watching the machine with his eyes and correcting its mistakes with his hands, the merely mechanical part of being the moving power. On the other hand, there are implements for which man has always acted as a simple motive power, for example by turning the crank of a mill,[^7] by pumping, by moving the arm of a bellows up and down, by pounding with a mortar, etc. Such implements soon call for the use of animals, water[^8] and wind as motive powers. Here and there, long before the period of manufacture, and also to some extent during that period, these implements pass over into machines, but without causing any revolution in the mode of production. In the period of modern industry it becomes clear that these implements, even in their form as hand tools, are already machines. For example, the pumps with which the Dutch emptied the Lake of Harlem in 1836-7 were built on the principle of ordinary pumps. The only difference was that their pistons were driven by giant, Cyclops-like steam-engines instead of by men. In England the blacksmith's common and very imperfect bellows is occasionally turned into a blowing-engine by connecting its arm to a steam-engine. The steam-engine itself, as it was when it was invented during the manufacturing period at the close of the 17th century, and as it remained down to 1780,[^9] did not give rise to any industrial revolution. On the contrary, it was the invention of machines that made a revolution in the form of steam-engines necessary. As soon as man, instead of working with an implement on the object of his labor, becomes merely the motive power of an implement-machine, it is a mere accident that the motive power is disguised as human muscle; it may equally well take the form of wind, water or steam. Of course, this does not prevent such a change of form from producing great technical changes in a mechanism that was originally built to be driven by man alone. Nowadays all machines that still have their way to make, such as sewing-machines, bread-making machines, etc., are built to be driven both by human and by purely mechanical motive power, unless their very nature rules out their use on a small scale.

[^7]: Moses says: “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treads the corn.” The Christian philanthropists of Germany, on the contrary, fastened a wooden board around the necks of the serfs whom they used as a motive power for grinding, to keep them from putting flour into their mouths with their hands.

[^8]: It was partly the lack of streams with a good fall, and partly their battles with too much water in other respects, that forced the Dutch to turn to wind as a motive power. They got the wind-mill itself from Germany. There its invention started a pretty squabble among the nobles, the priests, and the emperor over which of the three the wind “belonged” to. The air makes bondage, was the cry in Germany, at the same time as the wind was making Holland free. What it reduced to bondage in this case was not the Dutchman, but the land for the Dutchman. In 1836, 12,000 windmills of 6,000 horse-power were still used in Holland to keep two-thirds of the land from turning back into swamps.

The machine that is the starting-point of the industrial revolution replaces the worker who handles a single tool with a mechanism that operates with a number of similar tools and is set in motion by a single motive power, whatever form that power may take.[^10] Here we have the machine, but only as an elementary factor of production by machinery.

[^10]: “The union of all these simple instruments, set in motion by a single motor, constitutes a machine.” (Babbage, cited above.)

<!-- ch15-p010 -->
An increase in the size of the machine and in the number of its working tools calls for a more massive mechanism to drive it. To overcome its resistance, this mechanism needs a mightier moving power than man's, quite apart from the fact that man is a very imperfect instrument for producing uniform, continuous motion. But assume that he is acting simply as a motor, that a machine has taken the place of his tool: then clearly natural forces can replace him. Of all the great motors handed down from the manufacturing period, horse-power is the worst, partly because a horse has a head of his own, partly because he is costly, and the extent to which he can be used in factories is very limited.[^11] Nevertheless the horse was widely used in the infancy of modern industry. This is proved both by the complaints of agriculturists of the time and by the term “horse-power,” which has survived to this day as an expression for mechanical force.

[^11]: In January, 1861, John C. Morton read a paper before the Society of Arts on “The forces employed in agriculture.” In it he says: “Every improvement that furthers the uniformity of the land makes the steam-engine more and more applicable to the production of pure mechanical force.... Horse-power is requisite wherever crooked fences and other obstructions prevent uniform action. These obstructions are vanishing day by day. For operations that demand more exercise of will than actual force, the only power applicable is that controlled every instant by the human mind – in other words, man-power.” Mr. Morton then reduces steam-power, horse-power, and man-power to the unit generally used for steam-engines, namely the force needed to raise 33,000 lbs. one foot in one minute. He reckons the cost of one horse-power from a steam-engine at 3d. per hour, and from a horse at 5½d. per hour. Further, if a horse is to stay fully healthy, it can work no more than 8 hours a day. By using steam-power, at least three out of every seven horses used on tillage land during the year can be done without, at a cost no greater than what the horses done without would cost during the 3 or 4 months in which alone they can be used effectively. Lastly, in those farming operations in which steam-power can be used, it improves the quality of the work compared with horse-power. To do the work of a steam-engine would take 66 men, at a total cost of 15s. an hour, and to do the work of a horse, 32 men, at a total cost of 8s. an hour.

<!-- ch15-p011 -->
Wind was too unsteady and uncontrollable. Besides, in England, the birthplace of modern industry, the use of water power predominated even during the manufacturing period. In the 17th century attempts had already been made to turn two pairs of millstones with a single water-wheel. But the larger gearing was too much for the water power, which had now become insufficient, and this was one of the circumstances that led to a more exact investigation of the laws of friction. In the same way, in mills set in motion by pushing and pulling a lever, the irregularity caused by the motive power led to the theory and the application of the fly-wheel, which later plays so important a part in modern industry.[^12] In this way the first scientific and technical elements of modern mechanical industry were developed during the manufacturing period. Arkwright's throstle spinning mill was turned by water from the very first. But for all that, the use of water as the predominant motive power was beset with difficulties. It could not be increased at will, it failed at certain seasons of the year, and, above all, it was essentially local.[^13] Not until the invention of Watt's second, so-called double-acting steam-engine was a prime mover found that produced its own force by consuming coal and water, and whose power was entirely under man's control. It was mobile and a means of locomotion. It was urban and not, like the water-wheel, rural. It allowed production to be concentrated in towns, instead of being scattered up and down the country as with the water-wheels.[^14] It could be applied to every kind of technical use, and, relatively speaking, local circumstances had little effect on its choice of location. The greatness of Watt's genius showed itself in the specification of the patent that he took out in April, 1784. In that specification his steam-engine is described, not as an invention for a specific purpose, but as an agent that can be applied universally in mechanical industry. In it he points out uses, many of which, such as the steam-hammer, were not introduced until half a century later. Nevertheless he doubted that steam-engines could be used in navigation. His successors, Boulton and Watt, sent steam-engines of colossal size for ocean steamers to the exhibition of 1851.

[^13]: The modern turbine frees the industrial exploitation of water-power from many of its former fetters.

[^14]: “In the early days of textile manufactures, the locality of the factory depended upon the existence of a stream having a sufficient fall to turn a water-wheel; and, although the establishment of the water-mills was the commencement of the breaking up of the domestic system of manufacture, yet the mills necessarily situated upon streams, and frequently at considerable distances the one from the other, formed part of a rural, rather than an urban system; and it was not until the introduction of the steam-power as a substitute for the stream that factories were congregated in towns, and localities where the coal and water required for the production of steam were found in sufficient quantities. As soon as tools had been turned from hand implements of man into implements of a mechanical apparatus, of a machine, the motive mechanism also took on an independent form, entirely freed from the limits of human strength. With that, the individual machine that we have been considering so far sinks into a mere factor in production by machinery. One motive mechanism could now drive many machines at once. The motive mechanism grows with the number of machines that are turned at the same time, and the transmitting mechanism becomes a wide-spreading apparatus.

<!-- ch15-p013 -->
We now go on to distinguish the cooperation of a number of machines of one kind from a complex system of machinery.
