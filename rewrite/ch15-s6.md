### Section 6: The Theory of Compensation as Regards the Workpeople Displaced by Machinery

<!-- ch15-p145 -->
James Mill, MacCulloch, Torrens, Senior, John Stuart Mill, and a whole series of other bourgeois political economists insist on the following: all machinery that displaces workers at the same time, and necessarily, sets free an amount of capital large enough to employ the very same workers.[^132]

[^132]: Ricardo was at first also of this opinion. But later he expressly withdrew it, with the scientific impartiality and love of truth that were typical of him. See the work cited above, ch. xxxi. “On Machinery.”

<!-- ch15-p146 -->
Suppose a capitalist employs 100 workers in a carpet factory, at £30 a year each. So the variable capital he lays out each year amounts to £3,000. Suppose also that he dismisses 50 of his workers, and employs the remaining 50 with machinery that costs him £1,500. To keep things simple, we leave out buildings, coal, etc. Suppose further that the raw material used up each year costs £3,000, both before and after the change.[^133] Is any capital set free by this metamorphosis, this change of form? Before the change, of the total sum of £6,000, half was constant capital and half variable capital. After the change it consists of £4,500 constant capital (£3,000 raw material and £1,500 machinery) and £1,500 variable capital. Instead of being one half of the total capital, the variable capital is only one quarter of it. Instead of being set free, part of the capital is here locked up in such a way that it is no longer exchanged for labor-power: variable capital has been changed into constant capital. Other things remaining unchanged, the capital of £6,000 can in future employ no more than 50 men. With each improvement in the machinery, it will employ fewer. Now suppose the newly introduced machinery had cost less than the labor-power and tools it displaced. If, for instance, it had cost only £1,000, instead of £1,500, then a variable capital of £1,000 would have been converted into constant capital and locked up, and a capital of £500 would have been set free. Supposing wages unchanged, this £500 would form a fund large enough to employ about 16 of the 50 men dismissed; indeed, fewer than 16, for in order to be employed as capital, part of this £500 must now itself become constant capital. That leaves only the rest to be laid out in labor-power.

[^133]: Note well: my illustration follows exactly the lines of those given by the economists named above.

<!-- ch15-p147 -->
But suppose, besides, that making the new machinery gives work to a greater number of mechanics. Can that be called compensation to the carpet-makers who have been thrown onto the streets? At best, building the machinery employs fewer men than using it displaces. The sum of £1,500 that used to represent the wages of the dismissed carpet-makers now represents, in the shape of machinery: (1) the value of the means of production used in building that machinery, (2) the wages of the mechanics employed in building it, and (3) the surplus-value that falls to the share of their “master.” Further, the machinery need not be renewed until it is worn out. Hence, to keep the increased number of mechanics in constant employment, one carpet manufacturer after another must displace workers with machines.

<!-- ch15-p148 -->
In fact, the apologists do not mean this sort of setting free.

<!-- ch15-p149 -->
What they have in mind is the means of subsistence of the liberated workers. In the example above, it cannot be denied that the machinery not only liberates 50 men, and so places them at others' disposal, but at the same time withdraws from their consumption, and sets free, means of subsistence to the value of £1,500. Machinery cuts the workers off from their means of subsistence: that is a simple fact, and by no means a new one. In the language of economics, therefore, this fact amounts to saying that machinery liberates means of subsistence for the worker, or converts those means into capital for employing him. The way you put it, you see, is everything. Nominibus mollire licet mala [It is allowed to soften evils with names].

<!-- ch15-p150 -->
This theory implies that the £1,500 worth of means of subsistence was capital, which was being expanded by the labor of the 50 men who were dismissed. It implies, consequently, that this capital falls out of employment as soon as the men begin their forced holidays. It implies that the capital never rests until it has found a new investment, where these same 50 men can once again consume it productively. It implies, therefore, that sooner or later the capital and the workers must come together again, and that the compensation is then complete. And it implies that the sufferings of the workers displaced by machinery are therefore as passing as the riches of this world.

<!-- ch15-p151 -->
In relation to the dismissed workers, the £1,500 worth of means of subsistence never was capital. What really faced them as capital was the sum of £1,500, which was later laid out in machinery. Looking closer, we see that this sum represented part of the carpets the 50 dismissed men produced in a year. They received this part as wages from their employer, in money instead of in kind. With the carpets, now in the form of money, they bought means of subsistence to the value of £1,500. So for them these means were not capital but commodities. And with regard to these commodities, they were not wage workers but buyers. The machinery “freed” them from their means of purchase, and this changed them from buyers into non-buyers. Hence a reduced demand for those commodities – voilà tout [that's all]. If this fall in demand is not made up by a rise from some other quarter, the market price of the commodities falls. If this state of things lasts for some time and spreads, workers employed in producing these commodities are dismissed. Part of the capital that used to go into producing necessary means of subsistence has to be reproduced in another form. While prices fall and capital is being displaced, the workers employed in producing necessary means of subsistence are in their turn “freed” from part of their wages. So our apologists, with their cut-and-dried law of supply and demand, do not prove that when machinery frees the worker from his means of subsistence, it at the same time converts those means into capital for employing him further. On the contrary, they prove that machinery throws workers onto the streets, not only in the branch of production where it is introduced, but also in the branches where it is not introduced.

<!-- ch15-p152 -->
The real facts, which the optimism of the economists turns into a travesty, are as follows. When the machinery drives the workers out of the workshop, they are thrown onto the labor market. There they add to the number of workers at the capitalists' disposal. In Part VII of this book we shall see that this effect of machinery, which, as we have seen, is presented as a compensation to the working class, is on the contrary a most frightful scourge. For the present I will say only this. The workers thrown out of work in any branch of industry can no doubt look for employment in some other branch. If they find it, and so renew the bond between themselves and the means of subsistence, this happens only through a new and additional capital that is looking for investment. It does not happen at all through the capital that used to employ them and was later converted into machinery. And even if they do find employment, what poor prospects they have! Crippled as they are by the division of labor, these poor devils are worth so little outside their old trade that they cannot get into any industries except a few of an inferior kind, which are over-supplied with underpaid workers.[^134] Further, every branch of industry attracts a new stream of men each year. These men provide a contingent from which to fill vacancies and to draw a supply for expansion. As soon as machinery sets free part of the workers employed in a given branch of industry, these reserve men are also diverted into new channels of employment and absorbed in other branches. Meanwhile the original victims, during the period of transition, for the most part starve and perish.

[^134]: A follower of Ricardo, answering the insipid remarks of J. B. Say, observes on this point: “Where division of labour is well developed, the skill of the labourer is available only in that particular branch in which it has been acquired; he himself is a sort of machine. It does not therefore help matters one jot, to repeat in parrot fashion, that things have a tendency to find their level. On looking around us we cannot but see, that they are unable to find their level for a long time; and that when they do find it, the level is always lower than at the commencement of the process.” (“An Inquiry into those Principles Respecting the Nature of Demand,” etc., London 1821, p. 72.)

<!-- ch15-p153 -->
It is an undoubted fact that machinery, as such, is not responsible for “setting free” the worker from the means of subsistence. In the branch it seizes on, it makes production cheaper and greater, and at first it makes no change in the mass of means of subsistence produced in other branches. Hence, after machinery is introduced, society has as much of the necessaries of life as before, if not more, for the workers thrown out of work; and that is quite apart from the enormous share of the annual produce wasted by the non-workers. And this is the point our apologists rely on! The contradictions and antagonisms inseparable from the capitalist employment of machinery do not exist, they say, since they do not arise out of machinery as such, but out of its capitalist employment! So, since machinery, considered by itself, shortens the hours of labor, but lengthens them in the service of capital; since in itself it lightens labor, but raises the intensity of labor when capital employs it; since in itself it is a victory of man over the forces of Nature, but in the hands of capital makes man the slave of those forces; since in itself it increases the wealth of the producers, but in the hands of capital makes them paupers ‒ for all these reasons and others besides, says the bourgeois economist without more ado, it is as clear as noonday that all these contradictions are a mere semblance of reality, and that in fact they have neither an actual nor a theoretical existence. In this way he spares himself all further racking of his brain. What is more, he implicitly declares that his opponent is stupid enough to fight, not against the capitalist employment of machinery, but against machinery itself.

<!-- ch15-p154 -->
No doubt he is far from denying that temporary inconvenience may result from the capitalist use of machinery. But where is the medal without its reverse side! To him, any employment of machinery except by capital is an impossibility. So, with him, the exploitation of the worker by the machine is the same thing as the exploitation of the machine by the worker. Therefore, by his reasoning, whoever exposes the real state of things in the capitalist employment of machinery is against employing machinery in any way, and is an enemy of social progress![^135] This is exactly the reasoning of the celebrated Bill Sykes (the murderer in Dickens's *Oliver Twist*). “Gentlemen of the jury, no doubt the throat of this traveling salesman has been cut. But that is not my fault, it is the fault of the knife. Must we, for such a temporary inconvenience, abolish the use of the knife? Only consider! Where would agriculture and trade be without the knife? Is it not as beneficial in surgery as it is expert in anatomy? And a willing helper at the feast table besides? If you abolish the knife – you hurl us back into the depths of barbarism.”[^136]

[^135]: MacCulloch, among others, is a past master of this pretentious idiocy. “If,” he says, with the put-on innocence of a child of 8 years, “if it be advantageous, to develop the skill of the workman more and more, so that he is capable of producing, with the same or with a less quantity of labour, a constantly increasing quantity of commodities, it must also be advantageous, that he should avail himself of the help of such machinery as will assist him most effectively in the attainment of this result.” (MacCulloch: “Princ. of Pol. Econ.,” London 1830, p. 166.)

[^136]: “The inventor of the spinning machine has ruined India, a fact, however, that touches us but little.” A. Thiers: De la propriété. — M. Thiers here confuses the spinning machine with the power-loom, “a fact, however, that touches us but little.”

<!-- ch15-p155 -->
Machinery necessarily throws men out of work in the industries into which it is introduced. Yet, despite this, it may bring about an increase of employment in other industries. This effect, however, has nothing in common with the so-called theory of compensation. Since every article produced by a machine is cheaper than a similar article produced by hand, we deduce the following infallible law. If the total quantity of an article produced by machinery is equal to the total quantity of the same article previously produced by a handicraft or by manufacture, and now made by machinery, then the total labor spent is reduced. The new labor spent on the instruments of labor, on the machinery, on the coal, and so on, must necessarily be less than the labor displaced by using the machinery. Otherwise the product of the machine would be as dear as the product of manual labor, or dearer. But in fact the total quantity of the article produced by machinery with a reduced number of workers does not stay equal to the total quantity of the hand-made article it has displaced: it far exceeds it. Suppose that 400,000 yards of cloth have been produced on power-looms by fewer weavers than could weave 100,000 yards by hand. The quadrupled product contains four times as much raw material. Hence the production of raw material must be quadrupled. But with the instruments of labor, such as buildings, coal, machinery, and so on, it is different. The additional labor needed to produce them can increase only up to a certain limit. That limit varies with the difference between the quantity of the machine-made article and the quantity of the same article that the same number of workers could make by hand.

<!-- ch15-p156 -->
Hence, as the use of machinery spreads in a given industry, the immediate effect is to increase production in the other industries that supply the first with means of production. How far this provides employment for a greater number of men depends, given the length of the working day and the intensity of labor, on the composition of the capital employed, that is, on the ratio of its constant part to its variable part. This ratio, in turn, varies considerably with the extent to which machinery has already seized on those trades, or is seizing on them at the time. The number of men condemned to work in coal and metal mines grew enormously because of the progress of the English factory system. But during the last few decades this increase in numbers has been slower, because of the use of new machinery in mining.[^137] A new type of worker springs into life along with the machine, namely its maker. We have already learned that machinery has taken over even this branch of production, on a scale that grows greater every day.[^138] As to raw material,[^139] there is not the least doubt about what the rapid strides of cotton spinning did. They not only pushed on the growth of cotton in the United States with tropical luxuriance, and with it the African slave trade, but also made the breeding of slaves the chief business of the border slave-states. When the first census of slaves was taken in the United States, in 1790, their number was 697,000; in 1861, it had nearly reached four millions. On the other hand, it is no less certain that the rise of the English woolen factories, together with the gradual conversion of cropland into sheep pasture, brought about the surplus of agricultural workers that led to their being driven in masses into the towns. Ireland has reduced its population by nearly one half during the last twenty years. At this moment it is undergoing the process of reducing the number of its inhabitants still further, so as to suit exactly the requirements of its landlords and of the English woolen manufacturers.

[^137]: According to the census of 1861 (Vol. II., London, 1863), the number of people employed in coal mines in England and Wales amounted to 246,613. Of these, 73,545 were under and 173,067 were over 20 years. Of those under 20, 835 were between 5 and 10 years old, 30,701 between 10 and 15 years, and 42,010 between 15 and 19 years. The number employed in iron, copper, lead, tin, and other mines of every description was 319, 222.

[^138]: In England and Wales, in 1861, 60,807 persons were employed in making machinery. This number includes the employers and their clerks, etc., and also all agents and business people connected with this industry. It excludes the makers of small machines, such as sewing-machines, etc., and also the makers of the working parts of machines, such as spindles. The total number of civil engineers was 3,329.

[^139]: Since iron is one of the most important raw materials, let me state here that in 1861 there were in England and Wales 125,771 working iron founders, of whom 123,430 were males and 2,341 females. Of the males, 30,810 were under and 92,620 over 20 years.

When machinery is applied to any of the preliminary or intermediate stages that the object of labor (the material being worked on) has to pass through on its way to completion, the yield of material in those stages increases. At the same time, the demand for labor increases in the handicrafts or branches of manufacture that the products of the machines supply. Spinning by machinery, for example, supplied yarn so cheaply and so abundantly that the hand-loom weavers were at first able to work full time without any increased outlay. Their earnings accordingly rose.[^140] Hence a flow of people into the cotton-weaving trade. In the end, the 800,000 weavers whom the Jenny, the throstle and the mule had called into existence were overwhelmed by the power-loom. In the same way, because machinery produced clothing materials in abundance, the number of tailors, seamstresses and needlewomen kept increasing until the sewing-machine appeared.

[^140]: “A family of four grown-up persons, with two children as winders, earned at the end of the last, and the beginning of the present century, by ten hours’ daily labour, £4 a week. If the work was very pressing, they could earn more.... Before that, they had always suffered from a deficient supply of yarn.” (Gaskell, cited above, pp. 25-27.)

<!-- ch15-p158 -->
In proportion as machinery, with the help of a relatively small number of workers, increases the mass of raw materials, intermediate products, instruments of labor, etc., the working-up of these raw materials and intermediate products splits into countless branches. Social production becomes more varied. The factory system carries the social division of labor immeasurably further than manufacture does, because it raises the productivity of the industries it seizes on to a far higher degree.

<!-- ch15-p159 -->
The immediate result of machinery is to increase surplus-value and the mass of products in which surplus-value is embodied. And as the things consumed by the capitalists and their dependents become more plentiful, so do these layers of society themselves. Their growing wealth, together with the relatively smaller number of workers needed to produce the necessaries of life, gives rise to the means of satisfying new luxurious wants, at the same time as those wants arise. A larger portion of society's produce is changed into surplus produce, and a larger part of the surplus produce is supplied for consumption in many refined forms. In other words, the production of luxuries increases.[^141] The refined and varied forms of the products are also due to new relations with the markets of the world, relations that modern industry creates. Not only are greater quantities of foreign luxury articles exchanged for home products. A greater mass of foreign raw materials, ingredients, and intermediate products is also used as means of production in the home industries. Because of these relations with the markets of the world, the demand for labor increases in the carrying trades (shipping and transport), which split up into numerous varieties.[^142]

[^141]: F. Engels, in “Lage, &c.,” points out the miserable condition of a large number of those who work on these very luxury articles. See also numerous instances in the “Reports of the Children’s Employment Commission.”

[^142]: In 1861, in England and Wales, there were 94,665 sailors in the merchant service.

<!-- ch15-p160 -->
The means of production and subsistence increase, while the number of workers falls relatively. This causes an increased demand for labor in making canals, docks, tunnels, bridges, and so on: works that can bear fruit only in the far future. Entirely new branches of production are also formed, creating new fields of labor. They arise as the direct result either of machinery or of the general industrial changes that machinery brings about. But even in the most developed countries, the place these branches occupy in total production is far from important. The number of workers they employ is directly proportional to the demand these industries create for the crudest kind of manual labor. At present the chief industries of this kind are gas-works, telegraphs, photography, steam navigation, and railways. According to the census of 1861 for England and Wales, we find 15,211 persons in the gas industry (gas-works, production of mechanical apparatus, employees of the gas companies, etc.); 2,399 in telegraphy; 2,366 in photography; 3,570 in steam navigation; and in railways, 70,599, of whom about 28,000 are made up of the unskilled “navvies” (laborers who dig and build the earthworks), employed more or less permanently, together with the whole administrative and commercial staff. So the total number of persons employed in these five new industries is 94,145.

<!-- ch15-p161 -->
Lastly, the extraordinary productivity of modern industry, accompanied as it is by both a more extensive and a more intense exploitation of labor-power in all other spheres of production, allows a larger and larger part of the working class to be employed unproductively. As a consequence, it allows the ancient domestic slaves to be reproduced, on a constantly extending scale, under the name of a servant class, which includes men-servants, women-servants, lackeys, etc. According to the census of 1861, the population of England and Wales was 20,066,244: 9,776,259 males and 10,289,965 females. From this population let us deduct all who are too old or too young for work; all unproductive women, young persons and children; the “ideological” classes, such as government officials, priests, lawyers, soldiers, etc.; further, all whose only occupation is to consume the labor of others in the form of rent, interest, etc.; and lastly, paupers, vagabonds, and criminals. There remain, in round numbers, eight millions of both sexes and of every age. That number includes every capitalist who is in any way engaged in industry, trade, or finance.
