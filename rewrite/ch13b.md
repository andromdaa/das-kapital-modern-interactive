<!-- ch13-p012 -->
On the one hand, cooperation allows the work to be carried on over a wide area. So it is absolutely required in certain undertakings, such as draining land, building dikes, irrigation works, and making canals, roads, and railways. On the other hand, while it extends the scale of production, it makes possible a relative shrinking of the area in which production takes place. This shrinking of the area happens at the same time as the extension of scale, and arises from it, and it cuts out a number of useless expenses. It is due to the gathering together of workers, to the bringing together of various processes, and to the concentration of the means of production.[^12]

[^12]: As cultivation progresses, “all, and perhaps more than all, the capital and labour which once loosely occupied 500 acres, are now concentrated for the more complete tillage of 100.” Although “relatively to the amount of capital and labour employed, space is concentrated, it is an enlarged sphere of production, as compared to the sphere of production formerly occupied or worked upon by one single independent agent of production.” (R. Jones: “An Essay on the Distribution of Wealth,” part I. On Rent. London, 1831. p. 191.)

<!-- ch13-p013 -->
Compared with an equal sum of isolated working days, the combined working day produces a greater quantity of use-values, and so it reduces the labor-time needed to produce a given useful effect. In a given case, the combined working day may gain this increased productive power for any of several reasons. It may raise the mechanical force of labor. It may extend labor's sphere of action over a greater space. It may shrink the field of production relative to the scale of production. It may set large masses of labor to work at the critical moment. It may stir up rivalry between individuals and raise their animal spirits. It may stamp the similar operations carried on by a number of people with continuity and many-sidedness. It may carry out different operations at the same time. It may save on means of production by using them in common. Or it may give individual labor the character of average social labor. Whichever of these is the cause of the increase, the special productive power of the combined working day is, under all circumstances, the social productive power of labor, or the productive power of social labor. This power comes from cooperation itself. When the worker cooperates with others in a planned way, he strips off the chains of his individuality and develops the capabilities of his species.[^13]

[^13]: “La forza di ciascuno uomo è minima, ma la riunione delle minime forze forma una forza totale maggiore anche della somma delle forze medesime fino a che le forze per essere riunite possono diminuere il tempo ed accrescere lo spazio della loro azione.” [“The strength of each man is very small, but the union of very small strengths forms a total strength greater even than the sum of those same strengths, to the point that the strengths, by being united, can reduce the time and enlarge the space of their action.”] (G. R. Carli, Note to P. Verri, cited above, t. xv., p. 196.)

<!-- ch13-p014 -->
As a general rule, workers cannot cooperate without being brought together: being gathered in one place is a necessary condition of their cooperation. So wage workers cannot cooperate unless the same capital, the same capitalist, employs them at the same time, and therefore unless he buys their labor-powers at the same time. The total value of these labor-powers, or the amount of these workers' wages for a day or a week, as the case may be, must be ready in the capitalist's pocket before the workers are assembled for the process of production. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) Paying 300 workers at once, even for only one day, requires a greater outlay of capital than paying a smaller number of men, week by week, for a whole year. So the number of workers who cooperate, or the scale of cooperation, depends in the first place on the amount of capital that the individual capitalist can spare for buying labor-power. In other words, it depends on how far a single capitalist has command over the means of subsistence of a number of workers, that is, over the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities they need to live.

<!-- ch13-p015 -->
And what holds for the variable capital holds for the constant capital too. For example, the capitalist who employs 300 men spends 30 times as much on raw material as each of 30 capitalists who employ 10 men each. It is true that the value and quantity of the instruments of labor used in common do not increase at the same rate as the number of workers, but they do increase very considerably. So the concentration of large masses of the means of production in the hands of individual capitalists is a material condition for the cooperation of wage workers. And the extent of the cooperation, or the scale of production, depends on the extent of this concentration.

<!-- ch13-p016 -->
We saw in an earlier chapter that a certain minimum amount of capital was necessary so that the number of workers employed at the same time, and so the amount of surplus-value produced, might be large enough to free the employer himself from manual labor, to turn him from a small employer into a capitalist, and in this way formally to establish capitalist production. We now see that a certain minimum amount is a necessary condition for turning numerous isolated and independent processes into one combined social process.

<!-- ch13-p017 -->
We also saw that at first, the subjection of labor to capital was only a formal result of the fact that the worker works for the capitalist, and so under him, instead of working for himself. Through the cooperation of numerous wage workers, the rule of capital develops into a requirement for carrying on the labor process itself, into a real requirement of production. It is now as indispensable for a capitalist to command on the field of production as it is for a general to command on the field of battle.

<!-- ch13-p018 -->
All combined labor on a large scale needs, more or less, a directing authority, to secure the harmonious working of the individual activities and to perform the general functions that arise from the action of the combined organism as a whole, as distinct from the action of its separate organs. A single violin player is his own conductor; an orchestra needs a separate one. The work of directing, supervising, and adjusting becomes one of the functions of capital from the moment that the labor under capital's control becomes cooperative. Once it is a function of capital, it takes on special characteristics.

<!-- ch13-p019 -->
The driving motive, the end and aim of capitalist production, is to extract the greatest possible amount of surplus-value,[^14] and so to exploit labor-power to the greatest possible extent. As the number of cooperating workers grows, so does their resistance to the rule of capital, and with it the need for capital to overcome this resistance by pressing back. The control the capitalist exercises is not only a special function that arises from the nature of the social labor process and is specific to that process. It is at the same time a function of exploiting a social labor process. So it is rooted in the unavoidable antagonism between the exploiter and the living and laboring raw material he exploits.

[^14]: “Profits ... is the sole end of trade.” (J. Vanderlint, cited above, p. 11.)

<!-- ch13-p020 -->
Again, as the mass of the means of production grows, means that now belong no longer to the worker but to the capitalist, so does the need for some effective control over their proper use.[^15] Moreover, the cooperation of wage workers is brought about entirely by the capital that employs them. Their union into one single productive body, and the linking of their individual functions, are things foreign and external to them. They are not the workers' own act, but the act of the capital that brings them together and keeps them together. So the connection between their various kinds of labor appears to them, in idea, as a plan drawn up in advance by the capitalist. In practice it appears to them as the authority of that same capitalist, as the powerful will of another, who subjects their activity to his aims. The process of production itself has a two-fold nature: on the one hand, it is a social process for producing use-values; on the other, it is a process for creating surplus-value. If, then, the capitalist's control is two-fold in substance because of this, in form that control is despotic. As cooperation extends its scale, this despotism takes on forms of its own. At first the capitalist is freed from actual labor as soon as his capital has reached the minimum amount with which capitalist production as such begins. In the same way, he now hands over the work of direct and constant supervision of individual workers and groups of workers to a special kind of wage worker. An industrial army of workers under the command of a capitalist needs, like a real army, officers (managers) and sergeants (foremen, overseers), who command in the capitalist's name while the work is being done. The work of supervision becomes their established and exclusive function. When the political economist compares the mode of production of isolated peasants and craftsmen with production by slave labor, he counts this labor of supervision among the overhead costs of production.[^16] But when he considers the capitalist mode of production, he does the opposite. He treats the work of control that the cooperative character of the labor process makes necessary as identical with the different work of control that the capitalist character of that process, and the antagonism of interests between capitalist and worker, make necessary.[^17] A man is not a capitalist because he is a leader of industry; on the contrary, he is a leader of industry because he is a capitalist. The leadership of industry is an attribute of capital, just as in feudal times the functions of general and judge were attributes of landed property.[^18]

[^15]: That Philistine paper, the *Spectator*, states that after a sort of partnership between capitalist and workers was introduced in the “Wirework Company of Manchester,” “the first result was a sudden decrease in waste, the men not seeing why they should waste their own property any more than any other master’s, and waste is, perhaps, next to bad debts, the greatest source of manufacturing loss.” The same paper finds that the main defect in the Rochdale cooperative experiments is this: “They showed that associations of workmen could manage shops, mills, and almost all forms of industry with success, and they immediately improved the condition of the men; but then they did not leave a clear place for masters.” Quelle horreur! [How horrible!]

[^16]: Professor Cairnes first states that the supervision of labor is a leading feature of production by slaves in the Southern States of North America. He then continues: “The peasant proprietor (of the North), appropriating the whole produce of his toil, needs no other stimulus to exertion. Superintendence is here completely dispensed with.” (Cairnes, cited above, pp. 48, 49.)

[^17]: Sir James Steuart, a writer altogether remarkable for his quick eye for the typical social differences between different modes of production, says: “Why do large undertakings in the manufacturing way ruin private industry, but by coming nearer to the simplicity of slaves?” (“Prin. of Pol. Econ.,” London, 1767, v. I., pp. 167, 168.)

[^18]: So Auguste Comte and his school might have shown that feudal lords are an eternal necessity, in the same way that they have done for the lords of capital.

The worker owns his labor-power until he has finished bargaining over its sale with the capitalist. And he can sell no more than what he has, that is, his individual, isolated labor-power. This state of things is in no way changed by the fact that the capitalist buys the labor-power of 100 men instead of one, and makes separate contracts with 100 unconnected men instead of with one. He is free to set the 100 men to work without letting them cooperate. He pays them the value of 100 independent labor-powers, but he does not pay for the combined labor-power of the hundred. Since they are independent of each other, the workers are isolated persons, who enter into relations with the capitalist, but not with one another. Their cooperation begins only with the labor process, but by then they have ceased to belong to themselves. On entering that process, they become part of capital. As cooperators, as members of a working organism, they are merely particular forms in which capital exists. So the productive power the worker develops when working in cooperation is the productive power of capital. This power is developed free of charge whenever the workers are placed under certain conditions, and it is capital that places them under those conditions. This power costs capital nothing. On the other hand, the worker himself does not develop it before his labor belongs to capital. For both reasons, it appears as a power with which Nature has endowed capital ‒ a productive power built into capital.

<!-- ch13-p022 -->
The colossal effects of simple cooperation can be seen in the gigantic structures of the ancient Asian peoples, the Egyptians, the Etruscans, etc.

<!-- ch13-p023 -->
> “It has happened in times past that these Oriental States, after supplying the expenses of their civil and military establishments, have found themselves in possession of a surplus which they could apply to works of magnificence or utility. And in the construction of these, their command over the hands and arms of almost the entire non-agricultural population has produced stupendous monuments which still indicate their power. The teeming valley of the Nile ... produced food for a swarming non-agricultural population, and this food, belonging to the monarch and the priesthood, afforded the means of erecting the mighty monuments which filled the land.... In moving the colossal statues and vast masses of which the transport creates wonder, human labour almost alone, was prodigally [lavishly] used.... The number of the labourers and the concentration of their efforts sufficed. We see mighty coral reefs rising from the depths of the ocean into islands and firm land, yet each individual depositor is puny, weak, and contemptible. The non-agricultural labourers of an Asiatic monarchy have little but their individual bodily exertions to bring to the task, but their number is their strength, and the power of directing these masses gave rise to the palaces and temples, the pyramids, and the armies of gigantic statues of which the remains astonish and perplex us. It is that confinement of the revenues which feed them, to one or a few hands, which makes such undertakings possible.”[^19]

[^19]: R. Jones. “Textbook of Lectures,” etc., pp. 77, 78. The ancient Assyrian, Egyptian, and other collections in London and in other European capitals make us eye-witnesses of the ways that cooperative labor was carried on.

<!-- ch13-p024 -->
In modern society, this power of the Asiatic and Egyptian kings, the Etruscan priest-rulers, etc., has been transferred to the capitalist, whether he is an isolated capitalist or, as in joint-stock companies, a collective one.

<!-- ch13-p025 -->
Cooperation as we find it at the dawn of human development, among peoples who live by hunting,[^20] or, say, in the agriculture of Indian communities, rests on two things. On the one hand, it rests on common ownership of the means of production. On the other hand, it rests on the fact that in those cases each individual has no more torn himself loose from the umbilical cord of his tribe or community than each bee has freed itself from its connection with the hive. Both of these features distinguish such cooperation from capitalist cooperation. The occasional use of cooperation on a large scale in ancient times, in the middle ages, and in modern colonies rests on relations of domination and servitude, mainly on slavery. The capitalist form, on the contrary, presupposes from first to last the free wage worker, who sells his labor-power to capital. Historically, however, this form develops in opposition to peasant agriculture and to the carrying on of independent handicrafts, whether in guilds or not.[^21] From the standpoint of these, capitalist cooperation does not show itself as one particular historical form of cooperation. Instead, cooperation itself appears to be a historical form peculiar to the capitalist process of production, one that specifically distinguishes it.

[^20]: Linguet is improbably right when, in his “Théorie des Lois Civiles”, he declares hunting to be the first form of cooperation, and man-hunting (war) one of the earliest forms of hunting.

[^21]: Small-scale peasant agriculture and the carrying on of independent handicrafts together form the basis of the feudal mode of production, and after that system dissolves, they continue side by side with the capitalist mode. They also form the economic foundation of the classical communities at their best, after the primitive form of common ownership of land had disappeared, and before slavery had seized on production in earnest.

<!-- ch13-p026 -->
The social productive power of labor that cooperation develops appears to be the productive power of capital. In just the same way, cooperation itself, contrasted with the process of production carried on by isolated independent workers, or even by small employers, appears to be a specific form of the capitalist process of production. It is the first change that the actual labor process undergoes when it is subjected to capital. This change takes place spontaneously. Its necessary condition, the employment of a large number of wage workers at the same time in one and the same process, is also the starting-point of capitalist production. This point coincides with the birth of capital itself. So if, on the one hand, the capitalist mode of production presents itself to us historically as a necessary condition for turning the labor process into a social process, then, on the other hand, this social form of the labor process presents itself as a method capital uses to exploit labor more profitably, by increasing that labor's productivity.

<!-- ch13-p027 -->
In the elementary form in which we have looked at it so far, cooperation necessarily goes hand in hand with all production on a large scale. But in itself it does not represent a fixed form typical of a particular epoch in the development of the capitalist mode of production. At most it appears to do so, and then only roughly, in the handicraft-like beginnings of manufacture,[^22] and in the kind of large-scale agriculture that corresponds to the epoch of manufacture. That agriculture differs from peasant agriculture mainly in the number of workers employed at the same time and in the mass of means of production concentrated for their use. Simple cooperation is always the prevailing form in those branches of production in which capital operates on a large scale and division of labor and machinery play only a minor part.

[^22]: “Whether the united skill, industry, and emulation of many together on the same work be not the way to advance it? And whether it had been otherwise possible for England, to have carried on her Woollen Manufacture to so great a perfection?” (Berkeley. “The Querist.” London, 1751, p. 56, par. 521.)

<!-- ch13-p028 -->
Cooperation always remains the fundamental form of the capitalist mode of production. Nevertheless, the elementary form of cooperation continues to exist as a particular form of capitalist production, side by side with the more developed forms of that mode of production.
