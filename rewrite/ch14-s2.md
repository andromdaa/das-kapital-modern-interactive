### Section 2: The Detail Laborer and his Implements

<!-- ch14-p008 -->
If we now look more closely at the details, it is clear, in the first place, that a worker who performs one and the same simple operation all his life turns his whole body into the automatic, specialized tool of that operation. As a result, he takes less time to do it than the craftsman who performs a whole series of operations one after another. But the collective worker, who makes up the living mechanism of manufacture, consists solely of such specialized detail workers. Hence, compared with independent handicraft, more is produced in a given time, or the productive power of labor is increased.[^2] Moreover, once this fractional work has been established as the exclusive function of one person, the methods it uses become perfected. The worker’s constant repetition of the same simple act, and the concentration of his attention on it, teach him by experience how to get the desired result with the least possible effort. But since there are always several generations of workers living at the same time and working together at making a given article, the technical skill and the tricks of the trade acquired in this way become established, and are accumulated and handed down.[^3]

[^2]: “The more any manufacture of much variety shall be distributed and assigned to different artists [craftsmen], the same must needs be better done and with greater expedition, with less loss of time and labour.” (“The Advantages of the East India Trade,” London, 1720, p. 71.)

[^3]: “Easy labour is transmitted skill.” (Th. Hodgskin, “Popular Political Economy,” p. 48.)

In fact, manufacture produces the skill of the detail worker. It does so by reproducing within the workshop the naturally developed differentiation of trades that it found ready to hand in society at large, and by systematically driving that differentiation to an extreme. On the other hand, turning fractional work into one man’s lifelong calling corresponds to the tendency shown by earlier societies to make trades hereditary: either to petrify them into castes, or, whenever definite historical conditions produced in the individual a tendency to vary in a way that did not fit the nature of castes, to harden them into guilds. Castes and guilds arise from the action of the same natural law that governs the differentiation of plants and animals into species and varieties. The only difference is that, once a certain degree of development has been reached, the heredity of castes and the exclusiveness of guilds are decreed as a law of society.[^4]

[^4]: “The arts also have ... in Egypt reached the requisite degree of perfection. For it is the only country where artificers [craftsmen] may not in any way meddle with the affairs of another class of citizens, but must follow that calling alone which by law is hereditary in their clan.... In other countries it is found that tradesmen divide their attention between too many objects. At one time they try agriculture, at another they take to commerce, at another they busy themselves with two or three occupations at once. In free countries, they mostly frequent the assemblies of the people.... In Egypt, on the contrary, every artificer is severely punished if he meddles with affairs of State, or carries on several trades at once. Thus there is nothing to disturb their application to their calling.... Moreover, since they inherit from their forefathers numerous rules, they are eager to discover fresh advantages” (Diodorus Siculus: Bibl. Hist. I. 1. c., 74.)

> “The muslins of Dakka in fineness, the calicoes and other piece goods of Coromandel in brilliant and durable colours, have never been surpassed. Yet they are produced without capital, machinery, division of labour, or any of those means which give such facilities to the manufacturing interest of Europe. The weaver is merely a detached individual, working a web when ordered of a customer, and with a loom of the rudest construction, consisting sometimes of a few branches or bars of wood, put roughly together. There is even no expedient for rolling up the warp [the lengthwise threads of the cloth]; the loom must therefore be kept stretched to its full length, and becomes so inconveniently large that it cannot be contained within the hut of the manufacturer, who is therefore compelled to ply his trade in the open air, where it is interrupted by every vicissitude [change] of the weather.”[^5]

It is only the special skill accumulated from generation to generation, and passed down from father to son, that gives the Hindu this proficiency, as it gives it to the spider. And yet the work of such a Hindu weaver is very complicated compared with that of a worker in manufacture.

<!-- ch14-p012 -->
A craftsman who performs, one after another, the various fractional operations involved in producing a finished article must sometimes change his place and sometimes his tools. Passing from one operation to another interrupts the flow of his labor and creates, so to speak, gaps in his working day. These gaps close up as soon as he is tied to one and the same operation all day long; they vanish in proportion as the changes in his work decrease. The resulting increase in productive power is due either to more labor-power being spent in a given time, that is, to increased intensity of labor, or to a decrease in the amount of labor-power consumed unproductively. Every transition from rest to motion demands an extra expenditure of power, and this is made up for by keeping up the normal speed for longer once it has been reached. On the other hand, constant labor of one uniform kind disturbs the intensity and flow of a man’s animal spirits, which find refreshment and delight in mere change of activity.

<!-- ch14-p013 -->
The productivity of labor, that is, how much a given amount of labor produces in a given time, depends not only on the skill of the worker but also on how perfect his tools are. Tools of the same kind, such as knives, drills, gimlets, hammers, etc., may be used in different processes, and the same tool may serve various purposes in a single process. But as soon as the different operations of a labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things) are separated from one another, and each fractional operation takes on, in the hands of the detail worker, a suitable form of its own, the tools that previously served more than one purpose have to be altered. The direction this change takes is determined by the difficulties caused by the tool’s unchanged form. Manufacture is marked by the differentiation of the instruments of labor, a differentiation by which tools of a given sort take on fixed shapes, adapted to each particular use. It is also marked by the specialization of those instruments, so that each special tool shows its full effect only in the hands of a specific detail worker. In Birmingham alone 500 varieties of hammers are produced. Not only is each adapted to one particular process, but several varieties often serve exclusively for the different operations in one and the same process. The manufacturing period simplifies, improves, and multiplies the implements of labor by adapting them to the exclusively special functions of each detail worker.[^6] In this way it creates, at the same time, one of the material conditions for the existence of machinery, which consists of a combination of simple instruments.

[^6]: Darwin, in his epoch-making work on the origin of species, remarks with reference to the natural organs of plants and animals: “So long as one and the same organ has different kinds of work to perform, a ground for its changeability may possibly be found in this, that natural selection preserves or suppresses each small variation of form less carefully than if that organ were destined for one special purpose alone. Thus, knives that are adapted to cut all sorts of things, may, on the whole, be of one shape; but an implement destined to be used exclusively in one way must have a different shape for every different use.”
