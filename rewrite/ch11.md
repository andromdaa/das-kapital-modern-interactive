## Chapter 11: Rate and Mass of Surplus-Value

<!-- ch11-p001 -->
In this chapter, as up to now, the value of labor-power, and therefore the part of the working day needed to reproduce or maintain that labor-power, are assumed to be given, constant quantities. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work. Value is what commodities, things produced in order to be exchanged and not for the producer's own use, have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor.)

<!-- ch11-p002 -->
With this as our premise, once the rate of surplus-value is given, the mass of surplus-value that the individual worker supplies to the capitalist in a definite period of time is given at the same time. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital (s/v), that is, of surplus labor to necessary labor; it is also called the degree of exploitation of labor-power.) For example, suppose the necessary labor amounts to 6 hours daily, expressed in an amount of gold = 3 shillings. (Necessary labor is the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages.) Then 3s. is the daily value of one labor-power, or the value of the capital advanced in buying one labor-power. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger.) Suppose, further, that the rate of surplus-value is 100%. Then this variable capital of 3s. (variable capital being the part of capital spent on labor-power, which reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so that its size varies) produces a mass of surplus-value of 3s. In other words, the worker supplies daily a mass of surplus labor equal to 6 hours (surplus labor being the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist).

<!-- ch11-p003 -->
But the variable capital of a capitalist is the money expression of the total value of all the labor-powers that he employs at the same time. Its value is therefore equal to the average value of one labor-power multiplied by the number of labor-powers employed. So, with a given value of labor-power, the size of the variable capital varies directly with the number of workers employed at the same time. If the daily value of one labor-power = 3s., then a capital of 300s. must be advanced in order to exploit 100 labor-powers daily, or n times 3s. in order to exploit n labor-powers daily.

<!-- ch11-p004 -->
In the same way, if a variable capital of 3s., the daily value of one labor-power, produces a daily surplus-value of 3s., then a variable capital of 300s. will produce a daily surplus-value of 300s., and one of n times 3s. a daily surplus-value of n × 3s. The mass of surplus-value produced is therefore equal to the surplus-value that the working day of one worker supplies, multiplied by the number of workers employed. But further, the value of labor-power being given, the mass of surplus-value that a single worker produces is determined by the rate of surplus-value. So this law follows: the mass of surplus-value produced is equal to the amount of variable capital advanced, multiplied by the rate of surplus-value. In other words, it is determined by the compound ratio between the number of labor-powers exploited at the same time by the same capitalist and the degree of exploitation of each individual labor-power.

It is always assumed not only that the value of an average labor-power is constant, but also that the workers employed by a capitalist are reduced to average workers. There are exceptional cases in which the surplus-value produced does not increase in proportion to the number of workers exploited; but then the value of labor-power does not remain constant.

<!-- ch11-p008 -->
In producing a definite mass of surplus-value, therefore, a decrease in one factor may be made up for by an increase in the other. If the variable capital shrinks, and at the same time the rate of surplus-value rises in the same ratio, the mass of surplus-value produced stays unchanged. Suppose, on our earlier assumption, that the capitalist must advance 300s. in order to exploit 100 workers a day, and that the rate of surplus-value amounts to 50%. Then this variable capital of 300s. yields a surplus-value of 150s., or of 100 × 3 working hours. Now suppose the rate of surplus-value doubles, or the working day is extended from 6 to 12 hours instead of from 6 to 9, and at the same time the variable capital is cut by half, to 150s. It too yields a surplus-value of 150s., or 50 × 6 working hours. A reduction of the variable capital may therefore be made up for by a proportionate rise in the degree of exploitation of labor-power; or a decrease in the number of workers employed, by a proportionate extension of the working day. Within certain limits, therefore, the supply of labor that capital can exploit is independent of the supply of workers.[^1] On the other hand, a fall in the rate of surplus-value leaves the mass of surplus-value produced unchanged if the amount of variable capital, or the number of workers employed, increases in the same proportion.

[^1]: This elementary law appears to be unknown to the vulgar economists (economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations). Like upside-down Archimedes, they imagine that in the fixing of the market-price of labor by supply and demand they have found the fulcrum by means of which, not to move the world, but to stop its motion.

<!-- ch11-p009 -->
Nevertheless, making up for a decrease in the number of workers employed, or in the amount of variable capital advanced, by a rise in the rate of surplus-value or by lengthening the working day has limits that cannot be crossed. Whatever the value of labor-power may be, whether the working time necessary to maintain the worker is 2 or 10 hours, the total value that a worker can produce, day in, day out, is always less than the value in which 24 hours of labor are embodied. It is less than 12s., if 12s. is the money expression for 24 hours of realized labor. On our former assumption, 6 working hours are needed daily to reproduce the labor-power itself, or to replace the value of the capital advanced to buy it. On that assumption, a variable capital of 1,500s. that employs 500 workers at a rate of surplus-value of 100%, with a 12 hours' working day, produces a surplus-value of 1,500s. daily, or 6 × 500 working hours. A capital of 300s. that employs 100 workers a day with a rate of surplus-value of 200%, or with a working day of 18 hours, produces a mass of surplus-value of only 600s., or 12 × 100 working hours. And its total value-product, the equivalent of the variable capital advanced plus the surplus-value, can never, day in, day out, reach the sum of 1,200s., or 24 × 100 working hours. The average working day is by nature always less than 24 hours, and this absolute limit sets an absolute limit to making up for a reduction of variable capital by a higher rate of surplus-value, or for a decrease in the number of workers exploited by a higher degree of exploitation of labor-power. This obvious law is important for clearing up many phenomena. They arise from a tendency of capital (to be worked out later on) to reduce as much as possible the number of workers it employs, or its variable part, the part turned into labor-power. This tendency contradicts its other tendency, to produce the greatest possible mass of surplus-value. On the other hand, if the mass of labor-power employed, or the amount of variable capital, increases, but not in proportion to the fall in the rate of surplus-value, the mass of surplus-value produced falls.

<!-- ch11-p010 -->
A third law follows from the fact that the mass of surplus-value produced is determined by two factors: the rate of surplus-value and the amount of variable capital advanced. Given the rate of surplus-value, or the degree of exploitation of labor-power, and given the value of labor-power, or the amount of necessary working time, it is self-evident that the greater the variable capital, the greater would be the mass of value and of surplus-value produced. If the limit of the working day is given, and also the limit of its necessary part, the mass of value and surplus-value that an individual capitalist produces clearly depends exclusively on the mass of labor that he sets in motion. But under the conditions assumed above, this depends on the mass of labor-power, or the number of workers, that he exploits; and this number in turn is determined by the amount of variable capital advanced. So, with a given rate of surplus-value and a given value of labor-power, the masses of surplus-value produced vary directly with the amounts of the variable capitals advanced. Now we know that the capitalist divides his capital into two parts. One part he lays out on means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things). This is the constant part of his capital. The other part he lays out on living labor-power. This part forms his variable capital. On the basis of the same mode of social production, the division of capital into constant and variable differs in different branches of production. Within the same branch of production, too, this relation changes with changes in the technical conditions and in the social combinations of the processes of production. But whatever the proportion in which a given capital breaks up into a constant and a variable part, whether the variable part is to the constant part as 1:2 or 1:10 or 1:x, this does not affect the law just laid down. For, according to our earlier analysis, the value of the constant capital reappears in the value of the product, but does not enter into the newly produced value, the newly created value-product. To employ 1,000 spinners, more raw material, spindles, etc., are of course required than to employ 100. But the value of these additional means of production may rise, fall, stay the same, be large or small; it has no influence on the process by which surplus-value is created by means of the labor-powers that set them in motion. The law shown above therefore now takes this form: the masses of value and of surplus-value produced by different capitals – the value of labor-power being given and its degree of exploitation being equal – vary directly with the amounts of the variable parts of these capitals, that is, of their parts turned into living labor-power.

<!-- ch11-p011 -->
This law clearly contradicts all experience based on appearance. Everyone knows that a cotton spinner who, reckoning the percentage on the whole of the capital he applies, employs much constant and little variable capital, does not on that account pocket less profit or surplus-value than a baker, who sets in motion relatively much variable and little constant capital. To solve this apparent contradiction, many intermediate terms are still needed, just as, from the standpoint of elementary algebra, many intermediate terms are needed to understand that 0/0 may represent an actual magnitude. Classical economics (that is, the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production), although it does not formulate the law, holds to it by instinct, because the law is a necessary consequence of the general law of value. Classical economics tries to rescue the law from collision with contradictory phenomena by a violent abstraction. It will be seen later[^2] how the school of Ricardo has come to grief over this stumbling block. Vulgar economics, which indeed “has really learnt nothing,” here as everywhere sticks to appearances against the law that regulates and explains them. In opposition to Spinoza, it believes that “ignorance is a sufficient reason.”

[^2]: Further details will be given in Book IV.

The labor set in motion by the total capital of a society, day in, day out, may be regarded as a single collective working day. If, for example, the number of workers is a million, and the average working day of a worker is 10 hours, the social working day consists of ten million hours. With a given length of this working day, whether its limits are fixed physically or socially, the mass of surplus-value can only be increased by increasing the number of workers, that is, the working population. Here the growth of population forms the mathematical limit to the production of surplus-value by the total social capital. On the other hand, with a given size of population, this limit is formed by the possible lengthening of the working day.[^3] It will be seen in the following chapter, however, that this law holds only for the form of surplus-value dealt with so far.

[^3]: “The Labour, that is the economic time, of society, is a given portion, say ten hours a day of a million of people, or ten million hours.... Capital has its boundary of increase. This boundary may, at any given period, be attained in the actual extent of economic time employed.” (“An Essay on the Political Economy of Nations.” London, 1821, pp. 47, 49.)

From the treatment of the production of surplus-value so far, it follows that not every sum of money, or of value, can be turned into capital at will. To make this transformation, in fact, a certain minimum of money or of exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others) must be presupposed in the hands of the individual owner of money or commodities. The minimum of variable capital is the cost price of a single labor-power, employed the whole year through, day in, day out, for the production of surplus-value. (Price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) If this worker owned his own means of production, and were satisfied to live as a worker, he would not need to work beyond the time necessary to reproduce his means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), say 8 hours a day. Besides, he would need only enough means of production for 8 working hours. The capitalist, on the other hand, who makes him do, say, 4 hours' surplus labor besides these 8 hours, needs an additional sum of money to supply the additional means of production. On our assumption, however, the capitalist would have to employ two workers in order to live on the surplus-value he appropriates daily as well as a worker, and no better, that is, to be able to satisfy his necessary wants. In this case the mere maintenance of life would be the end of his production, not the increase of wealth; but the increase of wealth is implied in capitalist production. For him to live only twice as well as an ordinary worker, and also turn half of the surplus-value produced into capital, he would have to raise the minimum of capital advanced 8 times, along with the number of workers. Of course he can, like his worker, set to work himself and take a direct part in the process of production. But then he is only a hybrid between capitalist and worker, a “small master.” A certain stage of capitalist production requires that the capitalist be able to devote the whole of the time during which he functions as a capitalist, that is, as personified capital, to appropriating and therefore controlling the labor of others, and to selling the products of this labor.[^4] The guilds of the middle ages therefore tried to prevent by force the master of a trade from turning into a capitalist, by limiting the number of workers one master could employ to a very small maximum. The owner of money or commodities actually turns into a capitalist only in cases where the minimum sum advanced for production greatly exceeds the maximum of the middle ages. Here, as in natural science, we see the correctness of the law discovered by Hegel (in his “Logic”), that merely quantitative differences beyond a certain point pass into qualitative changes.[^5]

The minimum sum of value that the individual owner of money or commodities must command in order to metamorphose himself into a capitalist changes with the different stages of development of capitalist production. At given stages it also differs in different spheres of production, according to their special and technical conditions. Certain spheres of production demand, even at the very start of capitalist production, a minimum of capital that is not yet found in the hands of single individuals. This gives rise partly to state subsidies to private persons, as in France in the time of Colbert, and as in many German states up to our own time; and partly to the formation of societies with a legal monopoly for exploiting certain branches of industry and trade, the forerunners of our modern joint-stock companies.[^6]

[^6]: Martin Luther calls institutions of this kind “The Company Monopolia.”

<!-- ch11-p015 -->
Within the process of production, as we have seen, capital acquired command over labor, that is, over functioning labor-power, or the worker himself. The capitalist, as personified capital, takes care that the worker does his work regularly and with the proper degree of intensity.

<!-- ch11-p016 -->
Capital further developed into a coercive relation, which compels the working class to do more work than the narrow round of its own needs prescribes. As a producer of the activity of others, as a pumper-out of surplus labor and exploiter of labor-power, it surpasses all earlier systems of production based on directly forced labor in energy, disregard of limits, recklessness, and efficiency.

<!-- ch11-p017 -->
At first, capital subordinates labor on the basis of the technical conditions in which it historically finds it. It does not, therefore, change the mode of production immediately. So the production of surplus-value in the form we have considered so far, by simply extending the working day, proved to be independent of any change in the mode of production itself. It was no less active in the old-fashioned bakeries than in the modern cotton factories.

<!-- ch11-p018 -->
If we consider the process of production from the point of view of the simple labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things), the worker relates to the means of production not as capital, but as the mere means and material of his own intelligent productive activity. In tanning, for example, he deals with the skins simply as his object of labor. It is not the capitalist whose skin he tans. But it is different as soon as we look at the process of production from the point of view of the process of creating surplus-value. The means of production are at once changed into means for absorbing the labor of others. It is now no longer the worker who employs the means of production, but the means of production that employ the worker. Instead of being consumed by him as material elements of his productive activity, they consume him as the ferment necessary to their own life-process; and the life-process of capital consists only in its movement as value that constantly expands, constantly multiplies itself. Furnaces and workshops that stand idle at night, and absorb no living labor, are “a mere loss” to the capitalist. Hence furnaces and workshops constitute lawful claims on the night-labor of the workers. The simple transformation of money into the material factors of the process of production, into means of production, turns these means of production into a title and a right to the labor and surplus labor of others. One example will show, in conclusion, how this distortion, peculiar to and characteristic of capitalist production, this complete inversion of the relation between dead and living labor (labor already embodied in means of production, and the labor of workers now at work), between value and the force that creates value, is mirrored in the consciousness of capitalists. During the revolt of the English factory lords between 1848 and 1850, “the head of one of the oldest and most respectable houses in the West of Scotland, Messrs. Carlile Sons & Co., of the linen and cotton thread factory at Paisley, a company which has now existed for about a century, which was in operation in 1752, and four generations of the same family have conducted it” ... this “very intelligent gentleman” then wrote a letter[^7] in the *Glasgow Daily Mail* of April 25th, 1849, with the title “The relay system” (a system of shifts in which workers, especially children, were switched in and out at different times, which made the legal hours hard to check). Among other things, the letter contains the following grotesquely naive passage: “Let us now ... see what evils will attend the limiting to 10 hours the working of the factory.... They amount to the most serious damage to the millowner’s prospects and property. If he (that is, his “hands”) worked 12 hours before, and is limited to 10, then every 12 machines or spindles in his establishment shrink to 10, and should the works be disposed of, they will be valued only as 10, so that a sixth part would thus be deducted from the value of every factory in the country.”[^8]

[^7]: Reports of Insp. of Fact., April 30th, 1849, p. 59.

[^8]: Cited above, p. 60. Factory Inspector Stuart, himself a Scot, and, unlike the English Factory Inspectors, wholly taken captive by the capitalist way of thinking, expressly remarks on this letter, which he includes in his report, that it is “the most useful of the communications which any of the factory-owners working with relays have given to those engaged in the same trade, and which is the most calculated to remove the prejudices of such of them as have scruples respecting any change of the arrangement of the hours of work.”

<!-- ch11-p019 -->
To this West of Scotland bourgeois brain (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it), heir to the accumulated capitalist qualities of “four generations,” the value of the means of production, spindles, etc., is so inseparably mixed up with their property, as capital, of expanding their own value and swallowing up daily a definite quantity of the unpaid labor of others, that the head of the firm of Carlile & Co. actually imagines the following. If he sells his factory, so he imagines, not only will the value of the spindles be paid to him, but in addition their power of annexing surplus-value: not only the labor that is embodied in them and is necessary to produce spindles of this kind, but also the surplus labor that they help to pump out daily from the brave Scots of Paisley. And for that very reason he thinks that with the shortening of the working day by 2 hours, the selling-price of 12 spinning machines dwindles to that of 10!
