#### B. Prolongation of the Working day

<!-- ch15-p061 -->
If machinery is the most powerful means of increasing the productivity of labor – that is, of shortening the working time needed to produce a commodity – then in the hands of capital it becomes the most powerful means, in the industries it first invades, of lengthening the working day beyond all limits set by human nature. On the one hand, it creates new conditions that let capital give free rein to this constant tendency of its own. On the other hand, it creates new motives that sharpen capital's appetite for the labor of others.

<!-- ch15-p062 -->
In the first place, in the form of machinery, the implements of labor become automatic: things that move and work independently of the worker. From then on they are an industrial perpetual-motion machine, which would go on producing forever if it did not meet certain natural obstacles in the weak bodies and the strong wills of its human attendants. The automaton, as capital, and because it is capital, is given intelligence and will in the person of the capitalist. It is therefore filled with the longing to reduce to a minimum the resistance put up by that repellent yet elastic natural barrier, man.[^61] This resistance is also lessened by the apparent lightness of machine work, and by the more pliant and docile character of the women and children employed on it.[^62]

[^61]: “Since the general introduction of machinery, human nature has been forced far beyond its average strength.” (Rob. Owen: “Observations on the Effects of the Manufacturing System,” 2nd Ed., London, 1817.)

[^62]: The English, who tend to take the earliest form of appearance of a thing for the cause of its existence, are in the habit of blaming the long hours of work in factories on the widespread kidnapping of children from workhouses and orphanages, which capitalists practiced when the factory system was in its infancy. By means of this robbery they obtained material for exploitation that could not resist. Thus, for instance, Fielden, himself a manufacturer, says: “It is evident that the long hours of work were brought about by the circumstance of so great a number of destitute children being supplied from different parts of the country, that the masters were independent of the hands, and that having once established the custom by means of the miserable materials they had procured in this way, they could impose it on their neighbours with the greater facility.” (J. Fielden: “The Curse of the Factory System,” London, 1836, p. 11.) On the labor of women, Saunders, the factory inspector, says in his report of 1844: “Amongst the female operatives there are some women who, for many weeks in succession, except for a few days, are employed from 6 a. m. till midnight, with less than 2 hours for meals, so that on 5 days of the week they have only 6 hours left out of the 24, for going to and from their homes and resting in bed.”

As we saw, the productivity of machinery is inversely proportional to the value it transfers to the product. The longer the life of the machine, the greater the mass of products over which the value it passes on is spread, and the smaller the share of that value added to each single commodity. But the active lifetime of a machine clearly depends on the length of the working day, or on the duration of the daily labor process multiplied by the number of days for which the process is carried on.

<!-- ch15-p064 -->
The wear and tear of a machine is not exactly proportional to its working time. And even if it were, a machine working 16 hours a day for 7½ years covers as long a working period as the same machine would if it worked only 8 hours a day for 15 years, and passes no more value on to the total product. But in the first case the value of the machine would be reproduced twice as fast as in the second, and by using the machine in this way the capitalist would absorb in 7½ years as much surplus-value as he would absorb in 15 in the second case.

<!-- ch15-p065 -->
The material wear and tear of a machine is of two kinds. One kind comes from use, as coins wear away by circulating. The other comes from not being used, as a sword rusts when left in its sheath. This second kind is due to the elements. The first kind is more or less directly proportional to the use of the machine, the second to a certain extent inversely proportional to it.[^63]

[^63]: “Occasion... injury to the delicate moving parts of metallic mechanism by inaction.” (Ure, cited above, p. 281.)

<!-- ch15-p066 -->
But besides the material wear and tear, a machine also undergoes what we may call a moral depreciation. It loses exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for), either because machines of the same sort are produced more cheaply than it, or because better machines come into competition with it.[^64] In both cases, however young and full of life the machine may be, its value is no longer fixed by the labor actually materialized in it, but by the labor-time needed to reproduce either it or the better machine. So it has lost value, more or less. The shorter the period needed to reproduce its total value, the smaller the danger of moral depreciation; and the longer the working day, the shorter that period. When machinery is first introduced into an industry, new methods of reproducing it more cheaply follow blow upon blow,[^65] and so do improvements that affect not only individual parts and details of the machine, but its entire construction. It is therefore in the early days of the life of machinery that this special incentive to lengthen the working day is felt most sharply.[^66]

[^64]: The Manchester Spinner (*Times*, 26th Nov., 1862), mentioned before, says on this subject: “It (namely, the “allowance for deterioration of machinery”) is also intended to cover the loss which is constantly arising from the superseding of machines before they are worn out, by others of a new and better construction.”

[^65]: “It has been estimated, roughly, that the first individual of a newly-invented machine will cost about five times as much as the construction of the second.” (Babbage, cited above, p. 349.)

[^66]: “The improvements which took place not long ago in frames for making patent net were so great that a machine in good repair which had cost £1,200, sold a few years after for £60 ... improvements succeeded each other so rapidly, that machines which had never been finished were abandoned in the hands of their makers, because new improvements had superseded their utility.” (Babbage, cited above, p. 233.) So in these stormy, go-ahead times the tulle manufacturers soon extended the working day, by using double sets of workers, from the original 8 hours to 24.

<!-- ch15-p067 -->
Given the length of the working day, and all other circumstances remaining the same, exploiting twice the number of workers requires doubling not only the part of constant capital invested in machinery and buildings, but also the part laid out on raw material and auxiliary materials. Lengthening the working day, on the other hand, allows production on an extended scale without any change in the amount of capital laid out on machinery and buildings.[^67] So not only does surplus-value increase, but the outlay needed to obtain it decreases. It is true that this happens, more or less, with every lengthening of the working day. But in the case we are considering, the change is more marked, because the capital turned into instruments of labor predominates to a greater degree.[^68] The development of the factory system fixes an ever-growing part of the capital in a form in which, on the one hand, its value is capable of continual self-expansion, and in which, on the other hand, it loses both use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties) and exchange-value whenever it loses contact with living labor. “When a labourer,” said Mr. Ashworth, a cotton magnate, to Professor Nassau W. Senior, “lays down his spade, he renders useless, for that period, a capital worth eighteen-pence. When one of our people leaves the mill, he renders useless a capital that has cost £100,000.”[^69] Just imagine! Making “useless,” for a single moment, a capital that has cost £100,000! It is truly monstrous that a single one of our people should ever leave the factory! The increased use of machinery, as Senior clearly sees after the instruction he received from Ashworth, makes an ever-greater lengthening of the working day “desirable.”[^70]

[^67]: “It is self-evident, that, amid the ebbings and flowings of the markets and the alternate expansions and contractions of demand, occasions will constantly recur, in which the manufacturer may employ additional floating capital without employing additional fixed capital... if additional quantities of raw material can be worked up without incurring an additional expense for buildings and machinery.” (R. Torrens: “On Wages and Combination.” London, 1834, p. 64.)

[^68]: I mention this circumstance only for the sake of completeness, because I shall not consider the rate of profit, that is, the ratio of the surplus-value to the total capital advanced, until I come to the third book.

[^69]: Senior, “Letters on the Factory Act.” London, 1837, pp. 13, 14.

[^70]: “The great proportion of fixed to circulating capital ... makes long hours of work desirable.” With the increased use of machinery, etc., “the motives to long hours of work will become greater, as the only means by which a large proportion of fixed capital can be made profitable.” (cited above, pp. 11-13.) “There are certain expenses upon a mill which go on in the same proportion whether the mill be running short or full time, as, for instance, rent rates, and taxes, insurance against fire, wages of several permanent servants, deterioration of machinery, with various other charges upon a manufacturing establishment, the proportion of which to profits increases as the production decreases.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1862,” p. 19.)

Machinery produces relative surplus-value – surplus-value obtained by shortening necessary labor-time (by making workers' necessities cheaper through higher productivity), so that more of a working day of given length is surplus labor. It does this not only by directly lowering the value of labor-power, and by cheapening labor-power indirectly through cheapening the commodities that go into its reproduction; when machinery is first introduced sporadically into an industry, it also does it by converting the labor employed by the owner of that machinery into labor of a higher degree and greater effectiveness, by raising the social value of the article produced above its individual value, and so enabling the capitalist to replace the value of a day's labor-power with a smaller part of the value of a day's product. During this transition period, when the use of machinery is a sort of monopoly, the profits are therefore exceptional, and the capitalist tries to exploit to the full “the sunny time of this his first love,” by lengthening the working day as much as possible. The size of the profit sharpens his appetite for more profit.

<!-- ch15-p069 -->
As the use of machinery becomes more general in a particular industry, the social value of the product sinks to its individual value, and the law asserts itself that surplus-value does not arise from the labor-power that the machinery has replaced, but from the labor-power actually employed in working with the machinery. Surplus-value arises from variable capital alone – the part of capital spent on labor-power, which reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so that its size varies. And we saw that the amount of surplus-value depends on two factors, namely the rate of surplus-value (the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital, s/v) and the number of workers employed at the same time. Given the length of the working day, the rate of surplus-value is determined by the relative duration of the necessary labor and of the surplus labor in a day. The number of workers employed at the same time depends, for its part, on the ratio of the variable to the constant capital. Now, however much the use of machinery may increase surplus labor at the expense of necessary labor by raising the productivity of labor, it clearly achieves this result only by reducing the number of workers employed by a given amount of capital. It converts what was formerly variable capital, invested in labor-power, into machinery, which, being constant capital, does not produce surplus-value. It is impossible, for instance, to squeeze as much surplus-value out of 2 workers as out of 24. If each of these 24 men gives only one hour of surplus labor in 12, the 24 men together give 24 hours of surplus labor, while 24 hours is the total labor of the two men. Hence the application of machinery to the production of surplus-value contains a contradiction that is built into it. For of the two factors of the surplus-value created by a given amount of capital, one, the rate of surplus-value, cannot be increased except by reducing the other, the number of workers. This contradiction comes to light as soon as the general use of machinery in a given industry makes the value of the machine-made commodity regulate the value of all commodities of the same sort. And it is this contradiction that in its turn drives the capitalist, without his being conscious of the fact,[^71] to lengthen the working day excessively, so that he may make up for the decrease in the relative number of workers exploited by an increase not only of the relative but of the absolute surplus labor.

[^71]: Why the capitalist, and also the political economists who are steeped in his views, are unconscious of this built-in contradiction will become clear from the first part of the third book.

So if, on the one hand, the capitalist use of machinery supplies new and powerful motives for lengthening the working day excessively, and radically changes both the methods of labor and the character of the social working organism in such a way as to break down all opposition to this tendency, then on the other hand it produces a surplus working population,[^72] which is forced to submit to the dictates of capital. It produces this population partly by opening up to the capitalist new layers of the working class that were previously out of his reach, and partly by setting free the workers it replaces. Hence that remarkable phenomenon in the history of modern industry: machinery sweeps away every moral and natural limit on the length of the working day. Hence, too, the economic paradox that the most powerful instrument for shortening labor-time becomes the most unfailing means of placing every moment of the worker's time, and of his family's, at the disposal of the capitalist, for the purpose of expanding the value of his capital. “If,” dreamed Aristotle, the greatest thinker of antiquity, “if every tool, when summoned, or even of its own accord, could do the work that befits it, just as the creations of Daedalus moved of themselves, or the tripods of Hephaestos went of their own accord to their sacred work, if the weavers’ shuttles were to weave of themselves, then there would be no need either of apprentices for the master workers, or of slaves for the lords.”[^73] And Antipatros, a Greek poet of the time of Cicero, hailed the invention of the water-wheel for grinding grain, an invention that is the elementary form of all machinery, as the giver of freedom to female slaves and the bringer back of the golden age.[^74] Oh! those heathens! As the learned Bastiat, and before him the still wiser MacCulloch, have discovered, they understood nothing of political economy and Christianity. They did not, for example, grasp that machinery is the surest means of lengthening the working day. They perhaps excused the slavery of one on the ground that it was a means to the full development of another. But to preach the slavery of the masses so that a few crude and half-educated upstarts might become “eminent spinners,” “extensive sausage-makers,” and “influential shoe-black dealers” – to do this, they lacked the phrenological bump of Christianity.
