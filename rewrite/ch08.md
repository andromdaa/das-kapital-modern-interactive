## Chapter 8: Constant Capital and Variable Capital

<!-- ch08-p001 -->
The various factors of the labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things) play different parts in forming the value of the product. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.)

<!-- ch08-p002 -->
The worker adds fresh value to the object of his labor by spending a given amount of additional labor on it, whatever the specific character and usefulness of that labor may be. On the other hand, the values of the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things) that are used up in the process are preserved. They show up again as parts of the value of the product. The values of the cotton and the spindle, for instance, reappear in the value of the yarn. So the value of the means of production is preserved by being transferred to the product. This transfer takes place while those means are being turned into a product, in other words, during the labor process. It is brought about by labor; but how?

<!-- ch08-p003 -->
The worker does not perform two operations at once, one to add value to the cotton, and the other to preserve the value of the means of production, or, what amounts to the same thing, to transfer to the yarn, to the product, the value of the cotton he works on and part of the value of the spindle he works with. But by the very act of adding new value, he preserves their former values. Adding new value to the object of his labor and preserving its former value are, however, two entirely distinct results. The worker produces them at the same time, during one operation. So clearly this two-sided result can be explained only by the two-sided nature of his labor. At one and the same time, his labor must create value in one capacity, and preserve or transfer value in another.

<!-- ch08-p004 -->
Now, how does every worker add new labor, and so new value? Clearly, only by working productively in a particular way: the spinner by spinning, the weaver by weaving, the smith by forging. But while he thus embodies labor in general, that is, value, it is only through the particular form of the labor, through the spinning, the weaving, and the forging respectively, that the means of production (the cotton and spindle, the yarn and loom, and the iron and anvil) become parts of the product, of a new use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties).[^1] Each use-value disappears, but only to reappear in a new form in a new use-value. Now, when we were looking at the process of creating value, we saw the following. If a use-value is effectively consumed in producing a new use-value, the quantity of labor spent in producing the consumed article forms part of the quantity of labor needed to produce the new use-value. So this part is labor transferred from the means of production to the new product. Hence the worker preserves the values of the consumed means of production, or transfers them to the product as parts of its value, not because of his additional labor considered abstractly, but because of the particular useful character of that labor, because of its special productive form. So insofar as labor is this kind of specific productive activity, insofar as it is spinning, weaving, or forging, it raises the means of production from the dead by mere contact. It makes them living factors of the labor process, and combines with them to form the new products.

[^1]: “Labour gives a new creation for one extinguished.” (“An Essay on the Polit. Econ. of Nations,” London, 1821, p. 13.)

<!-- ch08-p005 -->
If the special productive labor of the worker were not spinning, he could not turn the cotton into yarn, and so he could not transfer the values of the cotton and spindle to the yarn. Suppose the same worker changed his occupation and became a joiner. He would still add value to the material he works on by a day’s labor. So we see, first, that new value is added not because his labor is spinning in particular, or joinery in particular, but because it is abstract human labor (labor counted simply as the spending of human effort, of brain, muscle, and nerve, whatever particular kind it is), a portion of the total labor of society. And we see next that the value added is of a given, definite amount, not because his labor has a special usefulness, but because it is carried on for a definite time. On the one hand, then, spinning adds new value to the values of the cotton and the spindle because of its general character, as the spending of human labor-power in the abstract. (Labor-power is a person’s capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) On the other hand, the same labor of spinning both transfers the values of the means of production to the product and preserves them in the product, because of its special character, as a concrete, useful process. Hence a two-sided result is produced at one and the same time.

<!-- ch08-p006 -->
By simply adding a certain quantity of labor, new value is added; and by the quality of this added labor, the original values of the means of production are preserved in the product. This two-sided effect, which comes from the two-sided character of labor, can be traced in various phenomena.

<!-- ch08-p007 -->
Let us assume that some invention lets the spinner spin as much cotton in 6 hours as he could spin before in 36 hours. For the purposes of useful production, his labor is now six times as effective as it was. The product of 6 hours’ work has grown six-fold, from 6 lbs. to 36 lbs. But now the 36 lbs. of cotton absorb only the same amount of labor as the 6 lbs. did before. Each pound of cotton absorbs one-sixth as much new labor, and so the value added by the labor to each pound is only one-sixth of what it was before. On the other hand, in the product, in the 36 lbs. of yarn, the value transferred from the cotton is six times as great as before. Through the 6 hours’ spinning, the value of the raw material that is preserved and transferred to the product is six times as great as before, although the new value that the spinner’s labor adds to each pound of the very same raw material is one-sixth of what it was before. This shows that the two properties of labor that enable it in one case to preserve value, and in the other to create value, are essentially different. On the one hand, the longer the time needed to spin a given weight of cotton into yarn, the greater the new value added to the material. On the other hand, the greater the weight of cotton spun in a given time, the greater the value preserved by being transferred from it to the product.

<!-- ch08-p008 -->
Now let us assume that the productivity of the spinner’s labor, instead of varying, stays constant. So he needs the same time as before to turn one pound of cotton into yarn. But let us assume that the exchange-value of the cotton varies (exchange-value is the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for): either it rises to six times its former value, or it falls to one-sixth of that value. In both these cases the spinner puts the same quantity of labor into a pound of cotton, and so adds as much value as he did before the change in value. He also produces a given weight of yarn in the same time as he did before. Nevertheless, the value he transfers from the cotton to the yarn is either one-sixth of what it was before the change, or, as the case may be, six times as much as before. The same result occurs when the value of the instruments of labor rises or falls while their usefulness in the process stays the same.

<!-- ch08-p009 -->
Again, if the technical conditions of the spinning process stay the same, and the means of production undergo no change of value, the spinner goes on consuming, in equal working times, equal quantities of raw material and equal quantities of machinery of unchanging value. The value he preserves in the product is directly proportional to the new value he adds to the product. In two weeks he embodies twice as much labor, and therefore twice as much value, as in one week. During the same time he consumes twice as much material and wears out twice as much machinery, of double the value in each case. So in the product of two weeks he preserves twice as much value as in the product of one week. As long as the conditions of production stay the same, the more value the worker adds by fresh labor, the more value he transfers and preserves. But he does so merely because this addition of new value takes place under conditions that have not changed and that are independent of his own labor. Of course, it may be said in one sense that the worker always preserves old value in proportion to the quantity of new value he adds. Whether the value of cotton rises from one shilling to two shillings, or falls to sixpence, the worker always preserves in the product of one hour only half as much value as he preserves in two hours. In the same way, if the productivity of his own labor varies, rising or falling, he will spin more or less cotton in one hour than he did before, as the case may be. So he will preserve more or less value of cotton in the product of one hour. But all the same, he will preserve twice as much value by two hours’ labor as by one.

<!-- ch08-p010 -->
Value exists only in useful articles, in objects; we leave aside its purely symbolic representation by tokens. (Man himself, seen as the embodiment of labor-power, is a natural object, a thing, although a living, conscious thing; and labor is the outward expression of this power residing in him.) So if an article loses its usefulness, it also loses its value. The reason why means of production do not lose their value at the same time as they lose their use-value is this: in the labor process they lose the original form of their use-value only to take on the form of a new use-value in the product. But however important it may be for value to have some useful object in which to be embodied, it makes no difference at all which particular object serves this purpose. We saw this when we dealt with the metamorphosis of commodities. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use. A metamorphosis is a change of form: a commodity changing into money, a sale, C–M, or money changing into a commodity, a purchase, M–C.) Hence it follows that in the labor process the means of production transfer their value to the product only insofar as they lose their exchange-value along with their use-value. They give up to the product only the value that they themselves lose as means of production. But in this respect the material factors of the labor process do not all behave alike.

<!-- ch08-p011 -->
The coal burned under the boiler vanishes without leaving a trace; so, too, does the tallow used to grease the axles of wheels. Dyes and other auxiliary substances also vanish, but reappear as properties of the product. Raw material forms the substance of the product, but only after it has changed its form. So raw material and auxiliary substances lose the characteristic form they had when they entered the labor process. It is different with the instruments of labor. Tools, machines, workshops, and vessels are of use in the labor process only as long as they keep their original shape, ready each morning to start the process again with their shape unchanged. And just as they keep their shape independent of the product during their lifetime, that is, during the continued labor process in which they serve, so they do after their death too. The corpses of machines, tools, workshops, etc., always remain separate and distinct from the product they helped to turn out. Now take any instrument of labor over the whole period of its service, from the day it enters the workshop until the day it is banished to the junk room. We find that during this period its use-value has been completely consumed, and therefore its exchange-value has been completely transferred to the product. For instance, if a spinning machine lasts for 10 years, clearly its total value is gradually transferred during that working period to the product of the 10 years. So the lifetime of an instrument of labor is spent in repeating a greater or smaller number of similar operations. Its life may be compared with that of a human being. Every day brings a man 24 hours nearer to his grave; but no one can tell exactly, just by looking at him, how many days he still has to travel on that road. This difficulty, however, does not stop life insurance offices from drawing very accurate, and at the same time very profitable, conclusions by means of the theory of averages. It is the same with the instruments of labor. Experience tells us how long, on average, a machine of a particular kind will last. Suppose its use-value in the labor process lasts only six days. Then on average it loses one-sixth of its use-value each day, and so gives up one-sixth of its value to the daily product. The wear and tear of all instruments, their daily loss of use-value, and the corresponding quantity of value they give up to the product, are calculated accordingly on this basis.

<!-- ch08-p012 -->
So it is strikingly clear that means of production never transfer more value to the product than they themselves lose during the labor process through the destruction of their own use-value. If such an instrument has no value to lose, in other words, if it is not the product of human labor, it transfers no value to the product. It helps to create use-value without contributing to the formation of exchange-value. This class includes all means of production supplied by Nature without human help, such as land, wind, water, metals still in the ground, and timber in virgin forests.

<!-- ch08-p013 -->
Yet another interesting phenomenon shows itself here. Suppose a machine is worth £1,000 and wears out in 1,000 days. Then one-thousandth of the value of the machine is transferred each day to the day’s product. At the same time, though with dwindling vitality, the machine as a whole goes on taking part in the labor process. Thus it appears that one factor of the labor process, a means of production, continually enters that process as a whole, while it enters the process of forming value only by fractions. The difference between the two processes is reflected here in their material factors: the same instrument of production takes part as a whole in the labor process, while at the same time, as an element in the formation of value, it enters only by fractions.[^2]

[^2]: The subject of repairs to the implements of labor does not concern us here. A machine that is being repaired no longer plays the part of an instrument, but that of an object of labor. Work is no longer done with it, but on it. For our purpose it is quite permissible to assume that the labor spent on repairing instruments is included in the labor needed to produce them in the first place. But in the text we deal with the wear and tear that no doctor can cure, and that little by little brings about death: with “that kind of wear which cannot be repaired from time to time, and which, in the case of a knife, would ultimately reduce it to a state in which the cutler would say of it, it is not worth a new blade.” We have shown in the text that a machine takes part in every labor process as a whole machine, but that it enters the simultaneous process of creating value only bit by bit. How great, then, is the confusion of ideas shown in the following extract! “Mr. Ricardo says a portion of the labour of the engineer in making [stocking] machines” is contained, for example, in the value of a pair of stockings. “Yet the total labour, that produced each single pair of stockings ... includes the whole labour of the engineer, not a portion; for one machine makes many pairs, and none of those pairs could have been done without any part of the machine.” “Obs. on Certain Verbal Disputes in Pol. Econ., Particularly Relating to Value,” p. 54. The author, an uncommonly self-satisfied know-it-all, is right in his confusion, and therefore in his claim, to this extent only: neither Ricardo nor any other economist, before or since him, has accurately distinguished the two aspects of labor, and still less, therefore, the part labor plays under each of these aspects in the formation of value.

<!-- ch08-p014 -->
On the other hand, a means of production may take part as a whole in the formation of value, while it enters the labor process only bit by bit. Suppose that in spinning cotton, the waste for every 115 lbs. used amounts to 15 lbs., which is turned not into yarn but into “devil’s dust.” This 15 lbs. of cotton never becomes part of the yarn. Yet if we assume that this amount of waste is normal and unavoidable under average conditions of spinning, its value is transferred to the value of the yarn just as surely as the value of the 100 lbs. that form the substance of the yarn. The use-value of 15 lbs. of cotton must vanish into dust before 100 lbs. of yarn can be made. So the destruction of this cotton is a necessary condition for producing the yarn. And because it is a necessary condition, and for no other reason, the value of that cotton is transferred to the product. The same holds for every kind of waste that results from a labor process, at least insofar as such waste cannot be used further as a means of producing new and independent use-values. Such a use of waste can be seen in the large machine works at Manchester, where mountains of iron turnings are carted off to the foundry in the evening, to reappear in the workshops the next morning as solid masses of iron.

<!-- ch08-p015 -->
We have seen that the means of production transfer value to the new product only insofar as they lose value, in the shape of their old use-value, during the labor process. The most value they can lose in the process is clearly limited by the amount of original value they had when they came into the process, in other words, by the labor-time needed to produce them. So the means of production can never add more value to the product than they themselves have, independently of the process they assist in. However useful a given kind of raw material, or a machine, or other means of production may be, even if it costs £150, or, say, 500 days’ labor, it still cannot, under any circumstances, add more than £150 to the value of the product. Its value is determined not by the labor process it enters as a means of production, but by the process it came out of as a product. In the labor process it serves only as a mere use-value, a thing with useful properties. So it could not transfer any value to the product unless it already had such value.[^3]

He claims to explain surplus-value (Interest, Profit, Rent) by the “services productifs” [“productive services”] that the means of production, soil, instruments, and raw material, perform in the labor process through their use-values. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) Mr. Wm. Roscher, who seldom misses a chance to record, in black and white, ingenious fancies that defend the system, records the following specimen: – “J. B. Very true, Mr. Professor! The oil produced by the oil mill is indeed something very different from the labor spent in building the mill! By value, Mr. Roscher understands such stuff as “oil,” because oil has value, even though “Nature” produces petroleum, though relatively “in small quantities.” He seems to be referring to this fact in his further remark: “It (Nature) produces scarcely any exchange-value.” Mr. Roscher’s “Nature” and the exchange-value it produces are rather like the foolish virgin who admitted, indeed, that she had had a child, but “it was such a little one.” This “savant sérieux” [“serious scholar”] goes on to remark: “Ricardo’s school is in the habit of including capital as accumulated labour under the head of labour. While productive labor is changing the means of production into parts of a new product, their value undergoes a metempsychosis. It leaves the consumed body to occupy the newly created one. But this transmigration takes place, as it were, behind the worker’s back. He cannot add new labor, cannot create new value, without at the same time preserving old values. This is because the labor he adds must be of a specific useful kind, and he cannot do useful work without using products as the means of production of a new product, and so transferring their value to the new product. So the property that labor-power in action, living labor, has of preserving value at the same time as it adds value is a gift of Nature that costs the worker nothing. But it is very advantageous to the capitalist, since it preserves the existing value of his capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger).[^4] As long as trade is good, the capitalist is too absorbed in money-grubbing to notice this free gift of labor. A violent interruption of the labor process by a crisis makes him keenly aware of it.[^5]

[^4]: “Of all the instruments of the farmers’ trade, the labour of man ... is that on which he is most to rely for the repayment of his capital. The other two ... the working stock of the cattle and the ... carts, ploughs, spades, and so forth, without a given portion of the first, are nothing at all.” (Edmund Burke: “Thoughts and Details on Scarcity, originally presented to the Right Hon. W. Pitt, in the month of November 1795,” Edit. London, 1800, p. 10.)

[^5]: In *The Times* of 26th November, 1862, a manufacturer whose mill employed 800 workers and consumed on average 150 bales of East Indian, or 130 bales of American, cotton complains dolefully about the standing expenses of his factory when it is not working. He estimates them at £6,000 a year. Among them are a number of items that do not concern us here, such as rent, rates, and taxes, insurance, and the salaries of the manager, book-keeper, engineer, and others. Then he reckons £150 for coal used to heat the mill occasionally and to run the engine now and then. Besides this, he includes the wages of the people employed at odd times to keep the machinery in working order. Lastly, he puts down £1,200 for depreciation of machinery, because “the weather and the natural principle of decay do not suspend their operations because the steam-engine ceases to revolve.” He says emphatically that he does not estimate his depreciation at more than the small sum of £1,200, because his machinery is already nearly worn out.

<!-- ch08-p017 -->
As regards the means of production, what is really consumed is their use-value, and the consumption of this use-value by labor results in the product. There is no consumption of their value,[^6] and so it would be inaccurate to say that it is reproduced. Rather, it is preserved. This is not because of any operation it undergoes itself in the process, but because the article in which it originally exists vanishes, it is true, but vanishes into some other article. Hence in the value of the product the value of the means of production reappears, but strictly speaking there is no reproduction of that value. What is produced is a new use-value in which the old exchange-value reappears.[^7]

[^6]: “Productive consumption ... where the consumption of a commodity is a part of the process of production. ... In these instances there is no consumption of value.” (S. P. Newman, cited above, p. 296.)

[^7]: In an American handbook that has gone through perhaps 20 editions, this passage occurs: “It matters not in what form capital re-appears;” then, after a lengthy list of all the possible ingredients of production whose value reappears in the product, the passage ends like this: “The various kinds of food, clothing, and shelter, necessary for the existence and comfort of the human being, are also changed. They are consumed from time to time, and their value re-appears in that new vigour imparted to his body and mind, forming fresh capital, to be employed again in the work of production.” (F. Wayland, cited above, pp. 31, 32.) Leaving aside any other oddities, it is enough to note that what reappears in the fresh vigor is not the bread’s price (the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity), but its blood-forming substances. What reappears in the value of that vigor, on the other hand, is not the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), but their value. The same necessaries of life, at half the price, would form just as much muscle and bone, just as much vigor, but not vigor of the same value. This confusion of “value” and “vigour,” together with our author’s sanctimonious vagueness, marks an attempt, futile all the same, to work out an explanation of surplus-value from a mere reappearance of values that already existed.

<!-- ch08-p018 -->
It is different with the subjective factor of the labor process, with labor-power in action. Because his labor is of a specialized kind with a special object, the worker preserves the value of the means of production and transfers it to the product. At the same time, by the mere act of working, he creates an additional, new value at every instant. Suppose the process of production stops just when the worker has produced an equivalent for the value of his own labor-power: when, for example, by six hours’ labor, he has added a value of three shillings. This value is the surplus of the total value of the product over the part of its value that is due to the means of production. It is the only original bit of value formed during this process, the only part of the value of the product created by this process. Of course, we do not forget that this new value only replaces the money the capitalist advanced to buy the labor-power, and that the worker spent on the necessities of life. With regard to the money spent, the new value is merely a reproduction. But nevertheless it is an actual reproduction, and not, as in the case of the value of the means of production, only an apparent one. Here one value is replaced by another through the creation of new value.

<!-- ch08-p019 -->
We know, however, from what has gone before, that the labor process may continue beyond the time needed to reproduce, and embody in the product, a mere equivalent for the value of the labor-power. Instead of the six hours that are enough for that purpose, the process may continue for twelve hours. So the action of labor-power not only reproduces its own value, but produces value over and above it. This surplus-value is the difference between the value of the product and the value of the elements consumed in forming that product, in other words, of the means of production and the labor-power.

<!-- ch08-p020 -->
By explaining the different parts that the various factors of the labor process play in forming the value of the product, we have in fact revealed the character of the different functions assigned to the different elements of capital in the process of expanding its own value. The surplus of the total value of the product over the sum of the values of its constituent factors is the surplus of the expanded capital over the capital originally advanced. The means of production on the one hand, and labor-power on the other, are merely the different forms of existence that the value of the original capital took on when it was changed from money into the various factors of the labor process. So the part of capital that is represented by the means of production, by the raw material, auxiliary material, and the instruments of labor, does not undergo any quantitative change of value in the process of production. I therefore call it the constant part of capital, or, more briefly, *constant capital.*

<!-- ch08-p021 -->
On the other hand, the part of capital represented by labor-power does undergo a change of value in the process of production. It both reproduces the equivalent of its own value and also produces an excess, a surplus-value, which may itself vary, and may be greater or smaller according to circumstances. This part of capital is continually being changed from a constant into a variable quantity. I therefore call it the variable part of capital, or, briefly, *variable capital.* The same elements of capital which, from the point of view of the labor process, appear as the objective and subjective factors respectively, as means of production and labor-power, appear, from the point of view of the process of creating surplus-value, as constant and variable capital.

<!-- ch08-p022 -->
The definition of constant capital given above by no means rules out the possibility of a change of value in its elements. Suppose the price of cotton is sixpence a pound one day, and the next day, because the cotton crop has failed, a shilling a pound. Each pound of the cotton bought at sixpence, and worked up after the rise in value, transfers a value of one shilling to the product. And the cotton already spun before the rise, perhaps circulating in the market as yarn, likewise transfers twice its original value to the product. Clearly, however, these changes of value are independent of the increment, the surplus-value, that the spinning itself adds to the value of the cotton. If the old cotton had never been spun, it could, after the rise, be resold at a shilling a pound instead of at sixpence. Further, the fewer the processes the cotton has gone through, the more certain this result is. So we find that when such sudden changes in value occur, speculators make it a rule to speculate in the material on which the least possible quantity of labor has been spent: to speculate, therefore, in yarn rather than in cloth, and in cotton itself rather than in yarn. In the case we have been looking at, the change of value originates, not in the process in which the cotton plays the part of a means of production, and in which it therefore functions as constant capital, but in the process in which the cotton itself is produced. The value of a commodity, it is true, is determined by the quantity of labor contained in it, but this quantity is itself limited by social conditions. Suppose the time socially necessary for producing any commodity changes – and a given weight of cotton represents more labor after a bad harvest than after a good one. Then all previously existing commodities of the same class are affected, because they are, as it were, only individuals of the species,[^8] and their value at any given time is measured by the labor socially necessary, that is, by the labor needed to produce them under the social conditions existing at that time.

[^8]: “Toutes les productions d’un même genre ne forment proprement qu’une masse, dont le prix se détermine en général et sans égard aux circonstances particulières.” (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 893.) [“Properly speaking, all products of the same kind form a single mass, whose price is fixed in general and without regard to particular circumstances.”]

<!-- ch08-p023 -->
Just as the value of the raw material may change, so may the value of the instruments of labor, the machinery, etc., used in the process; and so the part of the value of the product that is transferred to it from them may also change. If a new invention means that machinery of a particular kind can be produced with less labor, the old machinery loses more or less of its value, and so transfers that much less value to the product. But here again the change in value originates outside the process in which the machine acts as a means of production. Once engaged in this process, the machine cannot transfer more value than it has apart from the process.

<!-- ch08-p024 -->
Just as a change in the value of the means of production, even after they have begun to take part in the labor process, does not alter their character as constant capital, so, too, a change in the proportion of constant to variable capital does not affect the respective functions of these two kinds of capital. The technical conditions of the labor process may be revolutionized to such an extent that where formerly ten men, using ten implements of small value, worked up a relatively small quantity of raw material, one man may now, with the help of one expensive machine, work up one hundred times as much raw material. In the latter case we have an enormous increase in the constant capital, represented by the total value of the means of production used, and at the same time a great reduction in the variable capital, invested in labor-power. Such a revolution, however, alters only the quantitative relation between the constant and the variable capital, that is, the proportions in which the total capital is split into its constant and variable parts. It has not in the least affected the essential difference between the two.
