## Chapter 2: Exchange

<!-- ch02-p001 -->
Clearly, commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use) cannot go to market and make exchanges on their own account. We must therefore turn to their guardians, who are also their owners. Commodities are things, and so they have no power to resist man. If they are not docile, he can use force; in other words, he can take possession of them.[^1] For these objects to enter into relation with each other as commodities, their guardians must place themselves in relation to one another as persons whose will resides in those objects. They must behave in such a way that neither takes possession of the other’s commodity, or parts with his own, except by an act done by mutual consent. So they must recognize each other as having the rights of private owners. This legal relation expresses itself in a contract, whether or not that contract is part of a developed legal system. It is a relation between two wills, and it is only the reflection of the real economic relation between the two. It is this economic relation that decides the content of each such legal act.[^2]

[^1]: In the 12th century, so renowned for its piety, they counted some very delicate things among commodities. Thus a French poet of the period lists, among the goods to be found in the market of Landit, not only clothing, shoes, leather, farm tools, etc., but also “femmes folles de leur corps” [“women wanton with their bodies”].

[^2]: Proudhon begins by taking his ideal of Justice, of “justice éternelle” [“eternal justice”], from the legal relations that correspond to the production of commodities. In doing so, it may be noted, he proves, to the comfort of all good citizens, that the production of commodities is a form of production as everlasting as justice. Then he turns round and tries to reform the actual production of commodities, and the actual legal system that corresponds to it, in line with this ideal. What would we think of a chemist who, instead of studying the actual laws of the molecular changes in the composition and decomposition of matter, and on that basis solving definite problems, claimed to regulate the composition and decomposition of matter by means of the “eternal ideas” of “naturalité” [“naturalness”] and “affinité” [“affinity”]? Do we really know any more about “usury” when we say it contradicts “justice éternelle” [“eternal justice”], “équité éternelle” [“eternal equity”], “mutualité éternelle” [“eternal mutuality”], and other “vérités éternelles” [“eternal truths”], than the fathers of the church did when they said it was incompatible with “grâce éternelle” [“eternal grace”], “foi éternelle” [“eternal faith”], and “la volonté éternelle de Dieu” [“the eternal will of God”]?

<!-- ch02-p002 -->
The persons exist for one another merely as representatives of commodities, and therefore as owners of commodities. As our investigation goes on, we shall find, in general, that the characters who appear on the economic stage are only the personifications of the economic relations that exist between them.

<!-- ch02-p003 -->
What chiefly distinguishes a commodity from its owner is this: the commodity looks on every other commodity as merely the form of appearance of its own value. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) A born leveler and a cynic, the commodity is always ready to exchange not only soul but body with any and every other commodity, even one more repulsive than Maritornes herself, the inn servant in *Don Quixote*. The owner makes up for this lack of a sense of the concrete in the commodity with his own five and more senses. His commodity has no immediate use-value for him (use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties). Otherwise, he would not bring it to market. It has use-value for others. But for himself its only direct use-value is that it is a container of exchange-value (the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for), and so a means of exchange.[^3] So he makes up his mind to part with it for commodities whose use-value is of service to him. All commodities are non-use-values for their owners, and use-values for their non-owners. Consequently, they must all change hands. But this change of hands is what makes up their exchange, and exchange puts them in relation with each other as values, and realizes them as values. Hence commodities must be realized as values before they can be realized as use-values.

[^3]: For two-fold is the use of every object.... The one is peculiar to the object as such, the other is not, as a sandal which may be worn, and is also exchangeable. Both are uses of the sandal, for even he who exchanges the sandal for the money or food he is in want of, makes use of the sandal as a sandal. But not in its natural way. For it has not been made for the sake of being exchanged.” (Aristoteles, “De Rep.” l. i. c. 9.)

<!-- ch02-p004 -->
On the other hand, they must show that they are use-values before they can be realized as values. For the labor spent on them counts effectively only to the extent that it is spent in a form that is useful for others. Whether that labor is useful for others, and so whether its product can satisfy the wants of others, can be proved only by the act of exchange.

<!-- ch02-p005 -->
Every owner of a commodity wants to part with it in exchange only for those commodities whose use-value satisfies some want of his. Seen in this way, exchange is for him simply a private transaction. On the other hand, he wants to realize the value of his commodity, to turn it into any other suitable commodity of equal value, whether or not his own commodity has any use-value for the owner of the other. From this point of view, exchange is for him a social transaction of a general character. But one and the same set of transactions cannot be, at the same time and for all owners of commodities, both exclusively private and exclusively social and general.

<!-- ch02-p006 -->
Let us look at the matter a little more closely. To the owner of a commodity, every other commodity is, in relation to his own, a particular equivalent, and so his own commodity is the universal equivalent (a single commodity in which all other commodities express their values) for all the others. But since this applies to every owner, there is in fact no commodity acting as universal equivalent. The relative value of commodities then has no general form under which they can be equated as values and have the magnitude of their values compared. So far, therefore, they do not face each other as commodities, but only as products or use-values. In their difficulties our commodity owners think like Faust: “Im Anfang war die Tat.” [“In the beginning was the deed.” – Goethe, *Faust*.] They therefore acted and transacted before they thought. By instinct they conform to the laws imposed by the nature of commodities. They cannot bring their commodities into relation as values, and therefore as commodities, except by comparing them with some one other commodity as the universal equivalent. That we saw from the analysis of a commodity. But a particular commodity cannot become the universal equivalent except by a social act. So the social action of all other commodities sets apart the particular commodity in which they all represent their values. In this way the bodily form of this commodity becomes the form of the socially recognized universal equivalent. Through this social process, being the universal equivalent becomes the specific function of the commodity that the rest have excluded in this way. Thus it becomes – money. “Illi unum consilium habent et virtutem et potestatem suam bestiae tradunt. Et ne quis possit emere aut vendere, nisi qui habet characterem aut nomen bestiae aut numerum nominis ejus.” [“These have one mind, and shall give their power and strength unto the beast.” Revelations, 17:13; “And that no man might buy or sell, save he that had the mark, or the name of the beast, or the number of his name.” Revelations, 13:17.] (*Apocalypse*.)

<!-- ch02-p007 -->
Money is a crystal formed of necessity in the course of exchanges, in which different products of labor are in practice equated to one another and so, by practice, turned into commodities. As exchange develops and spreads through history, it develops the contrast, hidden in commodities, between use-value and value. Trade needs this contrast to be given an outward expression. That need pushes toward the setting up of an independent form of value (the way the value of one commodity is expressed in another commodity), and it does not rest until it is satisfied once and for all by the splitting of commodities into commodities and money. So at the same rate as products are turned into commodities, one special commodity is turned into money.[^4]

[^4]: From this we can judge the shrewdness of the petit-bourgeois (lower-middle-class) socialism which, while keeping the production of commodities going forever, aims to abolish the “antagonism” between money and commodities, and so, since money exists only by virtue of this antagonism, to abolish money itself. We might just as well try to keep Catholicism without the Pope. For more on this point see my work, “Zur Kritik der Pol. Oekon.,” p. 61, and following.

The direct barter of products reaches the elementary form of the relative expression of value in one respect, but not in another. That form is x Commodity A = y Commodity B. The form of direct barter is x use-value A = y use-value B.[^5] The articles A and B in this case are not yet commodities; they become commodities only by the act of barter. The first step an object of utility takes toward acquiring exchange-value is when it is a non-use-value for its owner. That happens when it is a surplus portion of some article needed for his immediate wants. Objects in themselves are external to man, and so he can alienate them, that is, part with them. For this alienation to be mutual, men only need, by a silent understanding, to treat each other as private owners of those alienable objects, and by implication as independent individuals. But such a state of mutual independence does not exist in a primitive society based on common property, whether that society takes the form of a patriarchal family, an ancient Indian community, or a Peruvian Inca State. The exchange of commodities therefore first begins on the boundaries of such communities, at their points of contact with other similar communities, or with members of those communities. But as soon as products become commodities in the external relations of a community, they also, by reaction, become commodities in its internal dealings. The proportions in which they are exchangeable are at first quite a matter of chance. What makes them exchangeable is their owners’ mutual desire to alienate them. Meanwhile the need for foreign objects of utility gradually takes hold. The constant repetition of exchange makes it a normal social act. In the course of time, therefore, some portion at least of the products of labor must be produced with exchange specially in view. From that moment the distinction becomes firmly fixed between the utility of an object for consumption and its utility for exchange. Its use-value becomes distinguished from its exchange-value. On the other hand, the quantitative proportion in which the articles are exchangeable comes to depend on their production itself. Custom stamps them as values with definite magnitudes.

[^5]: As long as, instead of two distinct use-values being exchanged, a chaotic mass of articles is offered as the equivalent of a single article, as often happens among savages, even the direct barter of products is only in its first infancy.

<!-- ch02-p009 -->
In the direct barter of products, each commodity is directly a means of exchange to its owner, and to all other persons an equivalent, but only to the extent that it has use-value for them. At this stage, therefore, the articles exchanged do not acquire a form of value independent of their own use-value, or of the individual needs of the people exchanging them. The need for a form of value grows as the number and variety of the commodities exchanged increase. The problem and the means of solving it arise at the same time. Commodity-owners never equate their own commodities to those of others, and exchange them on a large scale, without different kinds of commodities belonging to different owners being exchangeable for, and equated as values to, one and the same special article. This last article, by becoming the equivalent of various other commodities, at once acquires, though within narrow limits, the character of a general social equivalent. This character comes and goes with the passing social acts that called it into life. By turns and for a time it attaches itself first to this commodity and then to that. But as exchange develops, it fixes itself firmly and exclusively to particular sorts of commodities, and becomes crystallized by taking on the money form. The particular kind of commodity it sticks to is at first a matter of accident. Nevertheless there are two circumstances whose influence is decisive. The money form attaches itself either to the most important articles of exchange from outside, and these are in fact primitive and natural forms in which the exchange-value of home products finds expression; or else it attaches itself to the object of utility that forms, like cattle, the chief portion of the native wealth that can be alienated. Nomadic peoples are the first to develop the money form. This is because all their worldly goods consist of movable objects and so can be directly alienated, and because their way of life, by constantly bringing them into contact with foreign communities, invites the exchange of products. Man has often made man himself, in the form of slaves, serve as the primitive material of money, but has never used land for that purpose. Such an idea could only arise in a bourgeois society (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it) that is already well developed. It dates from the last third of the 17th century, and the first attempt to put it into practice on a national scale was made a century later, during the French bourgeois revolution.

<!-- ch02-p010 -->
In proportion as exchange bursts its local bonds, and the value of commodities expands more and more into an embodiment of abstract human labor (labor counted simply as the spending of human effort, of brain, muscle, and nerve, whatever particular kind it is), in the same proportion the character of money attaches itself to commodities that are by Nature fitted to perform the social function of a universal equivalent. Those commodities are the precious metals.

<!-- ch02-p011 -->
The truth of the statement that “although gold and silver are not by Nature money, money is by Nature gold and silver”[^6] is shown by how well the physical properties of these metals suit the functions of money.[^7] So far, however, we know only one function of money: to serve as the form of appearance of the value of commodities, that is, as the material in which the magnitudes of their values are socially expressed. Only a material whose every sample shows the same uniform qualities can be an adequate form of appearance of value, a fit embodiment of abstract, undifferentiated, and therefore equal human labor. On the other hand, since the difference between magnitudes of value is purely quantitative, the money commodity must be capable of merely quantitative differences. It must therefore be divisible at will, and just as capable of being put back together. Gold and silver have these properties by Nature.

[^6]: Karl Marx, cited above, p. 135. “I metalli ... naturalmente moneta.” [“The metals ... are by their nature money.”] (Galiani, “Della moneta” in Custodi’s Collection: Parte Moderna t. iii.)

[^7]: For further details on this subject see the chapter on “The precious metals” in my work cited above.

The use-value of the money commodity becomes two-fold. Besides its special use-value as a commodity (gold, for instance, serves to fill teeth, to form the raw material of luxury articles, etc.), it acquires a formal use-value, which comes from its specific social function.

<!-- ch02-p013 -->
Since all commodities are merely particular equivalents of money, and money is their universal equivalent, they play the parts of particular commodities in relation to money as the universal commodity.[^8]

[^8]: “Il danaro è la merce universale" [“Money is the universal commodity”] (Verri, cited above, p. 16).

We have seen that the money form is only the reflection, thrown onto one single commodity, of the value relations between all the rest. So the claim that money is a commodity[^9] is a new discovery only for those who, when they analyze money, start from its fully developed shape. The act of exchange gives the commodity that is turned into money not its value, but its specific form of value. By confusing these two distinct things, some writers have been led to hold that the value of gold and silver is imaginary.[^10] The fact that money can, in certain functions, be replaced by mere symbols of itself gave rise to that other mistaken notion, that money is itself a mere symbol. Nevertheless, under this error lurked a sense that the money form of an object is not an inseparable part of that object, but is simply the form in which certain social relations show themselves. In this sense every commodity is a symbol, since, insofar as it is value, it is only the material envelope of the human labor spent on it.[^11] But if one declares that the social characters taken on by objects, or the material forms taken on by the social qualities of labor under the régime of a definite mode of production, are mere symbols, one declares in the same breath that these characteristics are arbitrary fictions approved by the so-called universal consent of mankind. This suited the kind of explanation in favor during the 18th century. People could not account for the origin of the puzzling forms taken on by social relations between man and man. So they tried to strip these forms of their strange appearance by tracing them back to an origin in convention.

London, 1695, p. 7.) “Silver and gold, coined or uncoined, though they are used for a measure of all other things, are no less a commodity than wine, oil, tobacco, cloth, or stuffs.” (“A Discourse concerning Trade, and that in particular of the East Indies,” etc. London, 1689, p. 2.) “The stock and riches of the kingdom cannot properly be confined to money, nor ought gold and silver to be excluded from being merchandise.” ("The East-India Trade a Most Profitable Trade.” London, 1677, p. 4.)

It has already been remarked above that the equivalent form of a commodity does not imply how the magnitude of its value is determined. (In “20 yards of linen = 1 coat”, the linen’s value is expressed, so it is in the relative form of value, and the coat serves as the material that expresses it, so it is in the equivalent form.) So although we may know that gold is money, and so directly exchangeable for all other commodities, that fact by no means tells us how much 10 lbs. of gold, for instance, is worth. Money, like every other commodity, cannot express the magnitude of its value except relatively, in other commodities. Its value is determined by the labor-time required to produce it, and is expressed by the quantity of any other commodity that costs the same amount of labor-time.[^12] This quantitative fixing of its relative value takes place at the source of its production, by means of barter. When it steps into circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying) as money, its value is already given. In the last decades of the 17th century it had already been shown that money is a commodity, but this step marks only the infancy of the analysis. The difficulty lies not in understanding that money is a commodity, but in discovering how, why, and by what means a commodity becomes money.[^13]

He must, however, be given credit for one discovery, namely, that money is “a pleasant commodity.”

We have already seen, from the most elementary expression of value, x commodity A = y commodity B, that the object in which the magnitude of the value of another object is represented appears to have the equivalent form independently of this relation, as a social property given to it by Nature. We followed this false appearance up to the point where it becomes firmly established. That point is reached as soon as the universal equivalent form becomes identified with the bodily form of a particular commodity, and so is crystallized into the money form. What appears to happen is not that gold becomes money because all other commodities express their values in it. On the contrary, it appears that all other commodities universally express their values in gold because it is money. The intermediate steps of the process vanish in the result and leave no trace behind. Commodities find their own value already completely represented, without any initiative on their part, in another commodity existing alongside them. These objects, gold and silver, just as they come out of the bowels of the earth, are at once the direct incarnation of all human labor. Hence the magic of money. In the form of society we are now considering, the behavior of men in the social process of production is purely atomic. Hence their relations to each other in production take on a material character independent of their control and of their conscious individual action. These facts show themselves at first in products, as a general rule, taking the form of commodities. We have seen how the progressive development of a society of commodity-producers stamps one privileged commodity with the character of money. Hence the riddle presented by money is only the riddle presented by commodities; only now it strikes us in its most glaring form.
