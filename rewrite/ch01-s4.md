### Section 4: The Fetishism of Commodities and the Secret Thereof

<!-- ch01-p131 -->
At first sight a commodity seems a very trivial thing, and easy to understand. Analysis shows that it is, in reality, a very strange thing, full of metaphysical subtleties and theological niceties. So far as it is a use-value, there is nothing mysterious about it. That holds whether we look at it from the point of view that its properties make it capable of satisfying human wants, or from the point of view that those properties are the product of human labor. It is as clear as noon-day that man, by his industry, changes the forms of the materials supplied by Nature in such a way as to make them useful to him. The form of wood, for instance, is altered when a table is made out of it. Yet, for all that, the table goes on being that common, everyday thing, wood. But as soon as it steps forward as a commodity, it is changed into something transcendent. It not only stands with its feet on the ground, but, in relation to all other commodities, it also stands on its head, and out of its wooden brain it spins grotesque ideas, far more wonderful than “table-turning” ever was.[^26a]

At that time spiritualism, and table-turning in particular, became the rage among the European aristocracy. So the mystical character of commodities does not come from their use-value. Just as little does it come from the nature of the factors that determine value. For, in the first place, however varied the useful kinds of labor, or productive activities, may be, it is a physiological fact that they are functions of the human organism. And each such function, whatever its nature or form, is essentially the spending of human brain, nerves, muscles, etc. Secondly, take the thing that forms the groundwork for fixing the amount of value, namely the duration of that spending, or the quantity of labor. Here it is quite clear that there is a tangible difference between its quantity and its quality. In all states of society, the labor-time that it costs to produce the means of subsistence must necessarily be an object of interest to mankind, though not of equal interest at different stages of development.[^27] And lastly, from the moment that men work for one another in any way, their labor takes on a social form.

It was called Tagwerk [day’s work], Tagwanne [a day’s work] (jurnale, or terra jurnalis, or diornalis [day’s land]), Mannsmaad [a man’s mowing], etc. (See G. L. von Maurer, “Einleitung zur Geschichte der Mark, &c. Verfassung,” Munchen, 1854, p. 129 and following.)

Where, then, does the puzzling character of the product of labor come from, as soon as it takes the form of a commodity? Clearly from this form itself. The equality of all sorts of human labor is expressed objectively by the fact that their products are all equally values. The measure of the spending of labor-power by its duration takes the form of the quantity of value of the products of labor. And finally, the mutual relations of the producers, within which the social character of their labor asserts itself, take the form of a social relation between the products.

<!-- ch01-p134 -->
A commodity is therefore a mysterious thing, simply because in it the social character of men’s labor appears to them as an objective character stamped on the product of that labor. It is mysterious because the relation of the producers to the sum total of their own labor is presented to them as a social relation that exists not between themselves, but between the products of their labor. This is the reason why the products of labor become commodities: social things whose qualities are at the same time perceptible and imperceptible to the senses. In the same way, we perceive the light from an object not as a subjective stimulation of our optic nerve, but as the objective form of something outside the eye itself. But in the act of seeing, at any rate, light actually passes from one thing to another, from the external object to the eye. There is a physical relation between physical things. But it is different with commodities. There, the existence of the things as commodities, and the value relation between the products of labor that stamps them as commodities, have absolutely no connection with their physical properties, or with the material relations that arise from those properties. There it is a definite social relation between men that takes on, in their eyes, the fantastic form of a relation between things. So, to find an analogy, we must turn to the mist-covered regions of the religious world. In that world the products of the human brain appear as independent beings endowed with life, which enter into relations both with one another and with the human race. So it is in the world of commodities with the products of men’s hands. This I call the Fetishism that attaches itself to the products of labor as soon as they are produced as commodities, and which is therefore inseparable from the production of commodities.

<!-- ch01-p135 -->
This Fetishism of commodities has its origin, as the analysis so far has already shown, in the peculiar social character of the labor that produces them.

<!-- ch01-p136 -->
As a general rule, useful articles become commodities only because they are products of the labor of private individuals, or groups of individuals, who carry on their work independently of each other. The sum total of the labor of all these private individuals forms the total labor of society. The producers do not come into social contact with each other until they exchange their products. Because of this, the specific social character of each producer’s labor does not show itself except in the act of exchange. In other words, the labor of the individual asserts itself as a part of the labor of society only through the relations that the act of exchange sets up directly between the products, and indirectly, through them, between the producers. To the producers, therefore, the relations that connect the labor of one individual with that of the rest appear, not as direct social relations between individuals at work, but as what they really are: material relations between persons and social relations between things. It is only by being exchanged that the products of labor acquire, as values, one uniform social status, distinct from their varied forms of existence as useful objects. This division of a product into a useful thing and a value becomes important in practice only when exchange has spread so far that useful articles are produced for the purpose of being exchanged. At that point, their character as values therefore has to be taken into account in advance, during production. From this moment, the labor of the individual producer acquires socially a two-fold character. On the one hand, as a definite useful kind of labor, it must satisfy a definite social want. In this way it holds its place as part and parcel of the collective labor of all, as a branch of a social division of labor that has sprung up spontaneously. On the other hand, it can satisfy the many wants of the individual producer himself only insofar as the mutual exchangeability of all kinds of useful private labor is an established social fact, and the private useful labor of each producer therefore ranks as equal with that of all others. The most different kinds of labor can be made equal only by abstracting from their inequalities, or by reducing them to their common denominator, namely the spending of human labor-power, or abstract human labor. When this two-fold social character of the individual’s labor is reflected in his brain, it appears to him only in the forms that the exchange of products impresses on that labor in everyday practice. In this way, the socially useful character of his own labor takes the form of the condition that the product must be not only useful, but useful for others. And the social character that his particular labor has, of being equal to all other particular kinds of labor, takes the form that all the physically different articles that are products of labor have one common quality, namely that of having value.

<!-- ch01-p137 -->
So when we bring the products of our labor into relation with each other as values, it is not because we see these things as mere material containers of uniform human labor. Quite the opposite. Whenever we exchange our different products and so treat them as equal values, by that very act we also treat the different kinds of labor spent on them as equal, as human labor. We are not aware of this, but we do it.[^28] Value, then, does not walk around with a label saying what it is. Rather, it is value that turns every product into a social hieroglyph, a sign whose meaning is hidden. Later on, we try to decode the hieroglyph and get behind the secret of our own social products. For stamping a useful object as a value is just as much a social product as language is. Science has recently discovered that the products of labor, so far as they are values, are only the material expressions of the human labor spent in producing them. This discovery does mark an epoch in the history of human development. But it by no means clears away the mist through which the social character of labor looks to us like a property of the products themselves. In the particular form of production we are dealing with, namely the production of commodities, private labor is carried on independently, and its specific social character consists in the equality of every kind of such labor as human labor; in the product, this character therefore takes the form of value. To the producers, despite the discovery just mentioned, this fact seems just as real and final as another fact: that after science discovered the gases that make up air, the air itself stayed exactly as it was.

(Galiani: Della Moneta, p. 221, V. III. of Custodi’s collection of “Scrittori Classici Italiani di Economia Politica.” Parte Moderna, Milano 1803.)

When producers make an exchange, what first of all concerns them in practice is the question: how much of some other product do they get for their own? In what proportions are the products exchangeable? When custom has made these proportions fairly stable, they appear to result from the nature of the products. For instance, one ton of iron and two ounces of gold then appear to be of equal value just as naturally as a pound of gold and a pound of iron appear to be of equal weight, in spite of their different physical and chemical qualities. Once the character of having value has been impressed on products, it becomes fixed only because they act and react on each other as quantities of value. These quantities vary continually, independently of the will, foresight and action of the producers. To the producers, their own social action takes the form of the action of objects, which rule the producers instead of being ruled by them. It takes a fully developed production of commodities before a scientific conviction springs up, from accumulated experience alone, that all the different kinds of private labor are continually being reduced to the quantitative proportions in which society requires them. These kinds of private labor are carried on independently of each other, and yet as branches of the social division of labor that have developed spontaneously. And why? Because, in the midst of all the accidental and ever-fluctuating exchange relations between the products, the labor-time socially necessary for their production forcibly asserts itself like an overriding law of Nature. In the same way, the law of gravity asserts itself when a house falls down around our ears.[^29] That the magnitude of value is determined by labor-time is therefore a secret, hidden under the apparent fluctuations in the relative values of commodities. Discovering this secret removes all appearance of mere chance from the way the magnitude of the values of products is determined. Yet it in no way changes the way in which that determining takes place.

It is just nothing but a law of Nature, founded on the want of knowledge of those whose action is the subject of it.” (Friedrich Engels: “Umrisse zu einer Kritik der Nationalökonomie,” in the “Deutsch-Französische Jahrbücher,” edited by Arnold Ruge and Karl Marx. Paris. 1844.)

Man’s reflections on the forms of social life, and therefore also his scientific analysis of those forms, take a course directly opposite to that of their actual historical development. He begins after the event, with the results of the process of development already lying ready before him. Certain characters stamp products as commodities, and these characters must be established before there can be any circulation of commodities (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying). They have already acquired the stability of natural, self-evident forms of social life before man tries to decipher them. And what he tries to decipher is not their historical character, for in his eyes they are unchangeable, but their meaning. Consequently, it was only the analysis of the prices of commodities that led to determining the magnitude of value, and only the common expression of all commodities in money that led to establishing their character as values. But it is just this final money form of the world of commodities that actually conceals, instead of revealing, the social character of private labor and the social relations between the individual producers. When I state that coats or boots stand in a relation to linen because linen is the universal incarnation of abstract human labor, the absurdity of the statement is obvious. Nevertheless, the producers of coats and boots compare those articles with linen, or, what is the same thing, with gold or silver, as the universal equivalent. When they do, they express the relation between their own private labor and the collective labor of society in the same absurd form.

<!-- ch01-p140 -->
The categories of bourgeois economics consist of forms of just this kind. They are forms of thought that express, with social validity, the conditions and relations of a definite, historically determined mode of production, namely the production of commodities. So the whole mystery of commodities, all the magic and necromancy that surrounds the products of labor as long as they take the form of commodities, vanishes as soon as we come to other forms of production.

<!-- ch01-p141 -->
Since Robinson Crusoe’s experiences are a favorite theme with political economists,[^30] let us take a look at him on his island. Frugal though he is, he still has a few wants to satisfy, and so he must do a little useful work of various sorts, such as making tools and furniture, taming goats, fishing and hunting. We take no account of his prayers and the like, since they are a source of pleasure to him and he looks on them as so much recreation. In spite of the variety of his work, he knows that his labor, whatever its form, is only the activity of one and the same Robinson, and so that it consists of nothing but different modes of human labor. Necessity itself forces him to divide his time accurately between his different kinds of work. Whether one kind takes up more room in his overall activity than another depends on the difficulties, greater or less as the case may be, that he must overcome to get the useful effect he aims at. Our friend Robinson soon learns this by experience. He has rescued a watch, a ledger, and pen and ink from the wreck, and, like a true-born Briton, he starts to keep a set of books. His stock-book contains a list of the useful objects that belong to him, of the operations needed to produce them, and lastly of the labor-time that definite quantities of those objects have, on average, cost him. All the relations between Robinson and the objects that form this wealth of his own creation are here so simple and clear that anyone can understand them without effort, even Mr. Sedley Taylor. And yet those relations contain everything essential to the determination of value.

[^30]: Even Ricardo has his stories in Robinson’s style. “He makes the primitive hunter and the primitive fisher straightway, as owners of commodities, exchange fish and game in the proportion in which labour time is incorporated in these exchange values. On this occasion he commits the anachronism of making these men apply to the calculation, so far as their implements have to be taken into account, the annuity tables in current use on the London Exchange in the year 1817. The parallelograms of Mr. Owen appear to be the only form of society, besides the bourgeois form, with which he was acquainted.” (Karl Marx: “Zur Kritik, &c.,” pp. 38, 39)

Let us now move from Robinson’s island, bathed in light, to the European middle ages, shrouded in darkness. Here, instead of the independent man, we find everyone dependent: serfs and lords, vassals and suzerains (overlords), laymen and clergy. Personal dependence here marks the social relations of production just as much as it marks the other spheres of life that are organized on the basis of that production. But for the very reason that personal dependence forms the groundwork of society, there is no need for labor and its products to take on a fantastic form different from their reality. In the transactions of society they take the shape of services in kind and payments in kind. Here the immediate social form of labor is the particular and natural form of labor, and not its general abstract form, as it is in a society based on the production of commodities. Compulsory labor is measured by time just as properly as commodity-producing labor is. But every serf knows that what he spends in the service of his lord is a definite quantity of his own personal labor-power. The tithe (the tenth part of produce owed to the church) that has to be handed over to the priest is more of a plain fact than his blessing is. So, no matter what we may think of the parts played by the different classes of people themselves in this society, the social relations between individuals in the performance of their labor appear, at any rate, as their own mutual personal relations. They are not disguised under the shape of social relations between the products of labor.

<!-- ch01-p143 -->
For an example of labor in common, or directly associated labor, we have no need to go back to the spontaneously developed form of it that we find on the threshold of the history of all civilized peoples.[^31] We have one close at hand in the patriarchal industries of a peasant family that produces grain, cattle, yarn, linen, and clothing for home use. As far as the family is concerned, these different articles are so many products of its labor, but in relation to one another they are not commodities. The different kinds of labor that result in the various products, such as tilling the soil, tending cattle, spinning, weaving and making clothes, are, in themselves and just as they are, direct social functions. They are so because they are functions of the family. And the family, just as much as a society based on the production of commodities, has a spontaneously developed system of division of labor. The distribution of the work within the family, and the regulation of the labor-time of its several members, depend on differences of age and sex as well as on natural conditions that vary with the seasons. In this case the labor-power of each individual, by its very nature, works merely as a definite portion of the whole labor-power of the family. And therefore the measure of the spending of individual labor-power by its duration appears here, by its very nature, as a social character of their labor.

It is the primitive form that we can prove to have existed amongst Romans, Germanic peoples, and Celts, and even to this day we find numerous examples, ruins though they be, in India. A more exhaustive study of Asiatic, and especially of Indian forms of common property, would show how from the different forms of primitive common property, different forms of its dissolution have been developed. Thus, for instance, the various original types of Roman and Germanic private property are deducible from different forms of Indian common property.” (Karl Marx, “Zur Kritik, &c.,” p. 10.)

Let us now picture to ourselves, for a change, a community of free individuals, who carry on their work with the means of production held in common. In this community the labor-power of all the different individuals is consciously applied as the combined labor-power of the community. All the characteristics of Robinson’s labor are repeated here, but with this difference: they are social instead of individual. Everything Robinson produced was exclusively the result of his own personal labor, and therefore simply an object of use for himself. The total product of our community is a social product. One portion of it serves as fresh means of production and remains social. But another portion is consumed by the members as means of subsistence. So this portion has to be distributed among them. The way it is distributed will vary with the productive organization of the community and with the degree of historical development the producers have reached. We will assume, but only for the sake of a parallel with the production of commodities, that each individual producer’s share in the means of subsistence is determined by his labor-time. In that case labor-time would play a double part. Allotting it according to a definite social plan keeps the proper proportion between the different kinds of work to be done and the various wants of the community. On the other hand, labor-time also serves as a measure of the part of the common labor borne by each individual, and of his share in the part of the total product meant for individual consumption. In this case the social relations of the individual producers, both to their labor and to its products, are perfectly simple and understandable, and that holds not only for production but also for distribution.

<!-- ch01-p145 -->
The religious world is only the reflection of the real world. Take a society based on the production of commodities. In it the producers in general enter into social relations with one another by treating their products as commodities and values, and in this way reduce their individual private labor to the standard of uniform human labor. For such a society, Christianity, with its *cultus* [worship] of abstract man, is the most fitting form of religion, more especially in its bourgeois developments, Protestantism, Deism, etc. In the ancient Asiatic and other ancient modes of production, we find that turning products into commodities, and therefore turning men into producers of commodities, holds a subordinate place. Its importance, however, grows as the primitive communities come nearer and nearer to their dissolution. Trading nations, properly so called, exist in the ancient world only in its gaps, like the gods of Epicurus in the Intermundia [the spaces between the worlds], or like Jews in the pores of Polish society. Compared with bourgeois society, those ancient social organisms of production are extremely simple and transparent. But they are founded either on the immature development of man as an individual, who has not yet cut the umbilical cord that joins him to his fellow men in a primitive tribal community, or on direct relations of subjection. They can arise and exist only when the development of the productive power of labor has not risen beyond a low stage. And they can arise and exist only when, therefore, the social relations within the sphere of material life, between man and man and between man and Nature, are correspondingly narrow. This narrowness is reflected in the ancient worship of Nature and in the other elements of the popular religions. In any case, the religious reflection of the real world can finally vanish only when the practical relations of everyday life offer man nothing but perfectly understandable and reasonable relations with his fellow men and with Nature.

<!-- ch01-p146 -->
The life-process of society, which is based on the process of material production, does not strip off its mystical veil until it is treated as production by freely associated men and is consciously regulated by them according to a settled plan. This, however, requires that society have a certain material groundwork, or set of conditions of existence, and these in their turn are the spontaneous product of a long and painful process of development.

<!-- ch01-p147 -->
Political economy has indeed analyzed value and its magnitude, however incompletely,[^32] and has discovered what lies beneath these forms. But it has never once asked the question why labor is represented by the value of its product, and labor-time by the magnitude of that value.[^33] These formulas bear it stamped on them in unmistakable letters that they belong to a state of society in which the process of production has mastery over man, instead of being controlled by him. To the bourgeois mind, such formulas appear to be as much a self-evident necessity imposed by Nature as productive labor itself. So the bourgeoisie treat the forms of social production that came before the bourgeois form in much the same way as the Fathers of the Church treated pre-Christian religions.[^34]

[^32]: The inadequacy of Ricardo’s analysis of the magnitude of value – and his analysis is by far the best – will appear from the 3rd and 4th books of this work. As regards value in general, it is the weak point of the classical school of political economy (the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production) that nowhere does it distinguish, explicitly and with full awareness, between labor as it appears in the value of a product and the same labor as it appears in the use-value of that product. Of course the distinction is made in practice, since this school treats labor at one time under its quantitative aspect, at another under its qualitative aspect. But it has not the least idea that when the difference between various kinds of labor is treated as purely quantitative, this implies their qualitative unity or equality, and therefore their reduction to abstract human labor. For instance, Ricardo declares that he agrees with Destutt de Tracy in this proposition: “As it is certain that our physical and moral faculties are alone our original riches, the employment of those faculties, labour of some kind, is our only original treasure, and it is always from this employment that all those things are created which we call riches... It is certain, too, that all those things only represent the labour which has created them, and if they have a value, or even two distinct values, they can only derive them from that (the value) of the labour from which they emanate.” (Ricardo, “The Principles of Pol. Econ.,” 3 Ed. London 1821, p. 334.) We would only point out here that Ricardo puts his own deeper interpretation on Destutt’s words. What Destutt really says is that, on the one hand, all things that make up wealth represent the labor that creates them, but that, on the other hand, they acquire their “two different values” (use-value and exchange-value) from “the value of labour.” He thus falls into the commonplace error of the vulgar economists, who assume the value of one commodity (in this case labor) in order to determine the values of the rest. But Ricardo reads him as if he had said that labor (not the value of labor) is embodied both in use-value and in exchange-value. Nevertheless, Ricardo himself pays so little attention to the two-fold character of the labor that has a two-fold embodiment that he devotes the whole of his chapter on “Value and Riches, Their Distinctive Properties,” to a laborious examination of the trivialities of a J.B. Say. And at the end he is quite astonished to find that Destutt agrees with him, on the one hand, that labor is the source of value, and on the other hand agrees with J. B. Say about the notion of value.

[^33]: It is one of the chief failings of classical political economy that it has never succeeded, through its analysis of commodities, and in particular of their value, in discovering the form under which value becomes exchange-value. Even Adam Smith and Ricardo, the best representatives of the school, treat the form of value as a thing of no importance, as having no connection with the inner nature of commodities. The reason for this is not only that their attention is entirely absorbed by the analysis of the magnitude of value. It lies deeper. The form of value of the product of labor is not only the most abstract form that the product takes in bourgeois production, but also the most universal one. It stamps that production as a particular kind of social production, and in this way gives it its special historical character. If, then, we treat this mode of production as one fixed by Nature for all eternity, for every state of society, we necessarily overlook what is the differentia specifica [specific difference] of the form of value, and therefore of the commodity form, and of its further developments, the money form, the capital form, etc. So we find that economists who are thoroughly agreed that labor-time is the measure of the magnitude of value have the strangest and most contradictory ideas of money, the perfected form of the general equivalent. This shows up strikingly when they deal with banking, where the commonplace definitions of money no longer hold water. This led to the rise of a restored mercantile system (Ganilh, etc.), which sees in value nothing but a social form, or rather the unsubstantial ghost of that form. Let me state here, once and for all, that by classical political economy I mean the economics that, since the time of W. Petty, has investigated the real relations of production in bourgeois society. I contrast it with vulgar economics, which deals with appearances only. Vulgar economics endlessly chews over the materials that scientific economics provided long ago, and looks there for plausible explanations of the most glaring phenomena, for everyday bourgeois use. For the rest, it confines itself to systematizing, in a pedantic way, the trite ideas held by the self-satisfied bourgeoisie about their own world, which is to them the best of all possible worlds, and to proclaiming those ideas as everlasting truths.

Les institutions de la féodalité sont des institutions artificielles celles de la bourgeoisie sont des institutions naturelles. Ils ressemblent en ceci aux théologiens, qui eux aussi établissent deux sortes de religions. Toute religion qui n’est pas la leur, est une invention des hommes tandis que leur propre religion est une émanation de Dieu ‒ Ainsi il y a eu de l’histoire, mais il n’y en a plus.” [“Economists have a peculiar way of proceeding. For them there are only two kinds of institutions, artificial and natural. The institutions of feudalism are artificial institutions; those of the bourgeoisie are natural institutions. In this they are like the theologians, who likewise set up two kinds of religion. Every religion that is not theirs is an invention of men, while their own religion is an emanation from God. ... Thus there has been history, but there is no longer any”] (Karl Marx. Misère de la Philosophie. Réponse a la Philosophie de la Misère par M. Proudhon, 1847, p. 113.) Truly comical is M. Bastiat, who imagines that the ancient Greeks and Romans lived by plunder alone. But when people plunder for centuries, there must always be something at hand for them to seize; the objects of plunder must be continually reproduced. So it would appear that even the Greeks and Romans had some process of production, and therefore an economy, which formed the material basis of their world just as much as bourgeois economy forms that of our modern world. Or perhaps Bastiat means that a mode of production based on slavery is based on a system of plunder. In that case he is treading on dangerous ground. If a giant thinker like Aristotle erred in his judgment of slave labor, why should a dwarf economist like Bastiat be right in his judgment of wage labor? I take this opportunity to answer briefly an objection that a German paper in America raised against my work, “Zur Kritik der Pol. Oekonomie, 1859.” My view is that each special mode of production and the social relations corresponding to it, in short, the economic structure of society, is the real basis on which the legal and political superstructure is raised, and to which definite social forms of thought correspond. It is that the mode of production determines the character of social, political, and intellectual life generally. In the judgment of that paper, all this is very true for our own times, in which material interests predominate, but not for the middle ages, in which Catholicism reigned supreme, nor for Athens and Rome, where politics reigned supreme. In the first place, it strikes one as an odd thing for anyone to suppose that these well-worn phrases about the middle ages and the ancient world are unknown to anyone else. This much, however, is clear: the middle ages could not live on Catholicism, nor the ancient world on politics. On the contrary, it is the way in which they earned their living that explains why politics played the chief part in the ancient world, and Catholicism in the middle ages. For the rest, it takes only a slight acquaintance with the history of the Roman republic, for example, to be aware that its secret history is the history of its landed property. On the other hand, Don Quixote long ago paid the penalty for wrongly imagining that knight-errantry was compatible with all economic forms of society.

<!-- ch01-p148 -->
How far some economists are misled by the Fetishism inherent in commodities, or by the objective appearance of the social characteristics of labor, is shown, among other ways, by the dull and tedious quarrel over the part played by Nature in the formation of exchange-value. Exchange-value is a definite social way of expressing the amount of labor spent on an object. So Nature has no more to do with it than it has with fixing the exchange rate.

<!-- ch01-p149 -->
The mode of production in which the product takes the form of a commodity, or is produced directly for exchange, is the most general and most embryonic form of bourgeois production. So it makes its appearance early in history, though not in the same dominant and characteristic way as it does nowadays. Hence its Fetish character is comparatively easy to see through. But when we come to more concrete forms, even this appearance of simplicity vanishes. Where did the illusions of the monetary system come from? To the monetary system, gold and silver, when serving as money, did not represent a social relation between producers, but were natural objects with strange social properties. And modern economics, which looks down with such scorn on the monetary system: does its superstition not come out as clear as noon-day whenever it deals with capital? How long is it since economics discarded the illusion of the Physiocrats (a school of 18th-century French economists), that rents grow out of the soil and not out of society?

<!-- ch01-p150 -->
But, not to get ahead of ourselves, we will content ourselves with one more example relating to the commodity form. If commodities could speak, they would say: Our use-value may be a thing that interests men. It is no part of us as objects. What does belong to us as objects, however, is our value. Our natural dealings with each other as commodities prove it. In each other’s eyes we are nothing but exchange-values. Now listen to how those commodities speak through the mouth of the economist.

So far no chemist has ever discovered exchange-value either in a pearl or in a diamond. The economic discoverers of this chemical element, by the way, lay special claim to critical sharpness. They find, however, that the use-value of objects belongs to them independently of their material properties, while their value, on the other hand, forms a part of them as objects. What confirms them in this view is a peculiar circumstance. The use-value of objects is realized without exchange, through a direct relation between the objects and man, while their value, on the other hand, is realized only through exchange, that is, through a social process. Who does not think here of our good friend Dogberry, the constable in Shakespeare’s *Much Ado About Nothing*, who informs neighbor Seacoal that “To be a well-favoured [good-looking] man is the gift of fortune; but reading and writing comes by Nature.”[^37]

[^37]: The author of “Observations” and S. Bailey accuse Ricardo of turning exchange-value from something relative into something absolute. The opposite is the case. He has explained the apparent relation between objects such as diamonds and pearls, the relation in which they appear as exchange-values. And he has revealed the true relation hidden behind the appearances, namely their relation to each other as mere expressions of human labor. If Ricardo’s followers answer Bailey somewhat rudely, and by no means convincingly, the reason is to be found in this: they could not find in Ricardo’s own works any key to the hidden relations that exist between value and its form, exchange-value.
