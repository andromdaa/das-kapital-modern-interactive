## Chapter 18: Various Formula for the Rate of Surplus-Value

<!-- ch18-p001 -->
We have seen that the rate of surplus-value (surplus-value being the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it) is represented by the following formulas:

<!-- ch18-p002 -->
```text
     Surplus-value      s           Surplus-value          Surplus-labour
I.                                                     =
    Variable Capital (  v ) =  Value of labour-power      Necessary labour
```

<!-- ch18-p003 -->
The first two of these formulas express, as a ratio of values, what the third expresses as a ratio of the times during which those values are produced. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) These formulas complement one another, and they are strictly precise and correct. We therefore find them worked out in classical political economy in substance, but not consciously. (Political economy was the science of economics as it was then called; classical political economy means the economists from Petty to Ricardo who investigated the real relations of production.) There we meet with the following derived formulas.

<!-- ch18-p004 -->
```text
     Surplus-labour              Surplus-value           Surplus-product
II.                      =                           =
      Working day             Value of the Product         Total Product
```

<!-- ch18-p005 -->
Here one and the same ratio is expressed as a ratio of labor-times, as a ratio of the values in which those labor-times are embodied, and as a ratio of the products in which those values exist. It is of course understood that “Value of the Product” means only the value newly created in a working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor), leaving out the constant part of the value of the product.

<!-- ch18-p006 -->
In all of these formulas (II.), the actual degree of exploitation of labor, or the rate of surplus-value, is expressed falsely. Let the working day be 12 hours. Then, making the same assumptions as in earlier examples, the real degree of exploitation of labor is represented in the following proportions.

<!-- ch18-p007 -->
```text
  6 hours surplus-labour        Surplus-value of 3 sh.
                           =                              = 100%
6 hours necessary labour       Variable Capital of 3 sh.
From formulae II. we get very differently,
  6 hours surplus-labour         Surplus-value of 3 sh.
                             =                           = 50%
 Working day of 12 hours         Value created of 6 sh.
```

<!-- ch18-p008 -->
In reality, these derived formulas express only the proportion in which the working day, or the value produced by it, is divided between capitalist and worker. If they are to be treated as direct expressions of the degree of self-expansion of capital (capital being value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger), the following mistaken law would hold good: Surplus labor or surplus-value can never reach 100%.[^1] Surplus labor (the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist) is only a fraction of the working day, or surplus-value is only a fraction of the value created. So the surplus labor must necessarily always be less than the working day, or the surplus-value always less than the total value created. But to reach the ratio of 100:100 they must be equal. For the surplus labor to absorb the whole day (that is, an average day of any week or year), the necessary labor must sink to zero. (Necessary labor is the part of the working day in which the worker produces the equivalent of the value of their own labor-power, that is, of their wages.) But if the necessary labor vanishes, so does the surplus labor, since the surplus labor is only a function of the necessary labor. The ratio

can therefore never reach the limit 100/100, still less rise to 100 + x/100. But this is not so for the rate of surplus-value, the real degree of exploitation of labor. Take, for example, the estimate of L. de Lavergne. According to it, the English agricultural worker gets only 1/4, and the capitalist (farmer), on the other hand, 3/4 of the product[^2] or its value, leaving aside the question of how the booty is later divided between the capitalist, the landlord, and others. According to this, the surplus labor of the English agricultural worker is to his necessary labor as 3:1, which gives a rate of exploitation of 300%.

[^2]: Of course, the part of the product that merely replaces the constant capital advanced is left out of this calculation. (Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production. Its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) Mr. L. de Lavergne, a blind admirer of England, tends to estimate the capitalist’s share too low rather than too high.

<!-- ch18-p011 -->
Through the use of formulas II., the favorite method of treating the working day as fixed in length became a settled habit, because in these formulas surplus labor is always compared with a working day of given length. The same holds good when only the division of the value produced is kept in view. The working day that has already been realized in a given value must necessarily be a day of given length.

<!-- ch18-p012 -->
There is a habit of representing surplus-value and value of labor-power as fractions of the value created. (Labor-power is a person’s capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist. The value of labor-power is the value of the means of subsistence needed to keep the worker, and to raise the next generation of workers, able to work.) This habit originates in the capitalist mode of production itself, and its meaning will be revealed later. It hides the very transaction that characterizes capital, namely the exchange of variable capital for living labor-power, and the resulting exclusion of the worker from the product. (Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power. It reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies.) Instead of the real fact, we get the false appearance of an association, in which worker and capitalist divide the product in proportion to the different elements that each of them contributes to making it.[^3]

[^3]: All well-developed forms of capitalist production are forms of cooperation (many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist’s command). So nothing is easier, of course, than to ignore their antagonistic character and to turn them, with a word, into some form of free association. A. de Laborde does this in “De l’Esprit d’Association dans tous les intérêts de la communauté”. Paris 1818. H. Carey, the Yankee, occasionally performs this conjuring trick with equal success, even with the relations that result from slavery.

<!-- ch18-p013 -->
Moreover, formulas II. can at any time be turned back into formulas I. If, for instance, we have

<!-- ch18-p014 -->
Surplus labor of 6 hours\
Working day of 12 hours

<!-- ch18-p015 -->
then the necessary labor-time is 12 hours minus the surplus labor of 6 hours, and we get the following result,

<!-- ch18-p016 -->
```text
  Surplus-labour of 6 hours       100
                             =
Necessary labour of 6 hours       100
```

<!-- ch18-p017 -->
There is a third formula, which I have already anticipated now and then; it is

<!-- ch18-p018 -->
```text
            Surplus-value             Surplus-labour        Unpaid labour
III.                             =                      =
       Value of labour-power         Necessary labour        Paid labour
```

<!-- ch18-p019 -->
After the investigations we have given above, it is no longer possible to be misled by the formula

<!-- ch18-p020 -->
Unpaid labor,\
Paid labor

<!-- ch18-p021 -->
into concluding that the capitalist pays for labor and not for labor-power. This formula is only a popular expression for

<!-- ch18-p022 -->
Surplus labor,\
Necessary labor

<!-- ch18-p023 -->
The capitalist pays the value of the labor-power, so far as price coincides with value. (A price is the money name of the value, the labor, embodied in a commodity.) In exchange he receives the disposal of the living labor-power itself. His usufruct, his right to use it, is spread over two periods. During one period the worker produces a value that is only equal to the value of his labor-power; he produces its equivalent. This the capitalist receives in return for advancing the price of the labor-power, a product ready-made in the market. During the other period, the period of surplus labor, the use of the labor-power creates a value for the capitalist that costs him no equivalent.[^4] This expenditure of labor-power comes to him free of charge. It is in this sense that surplus labor can be called unpaid labor.

[^4]: Although the Physiocrats could not see through the mystery of surplus-value, this much was clear to them, namely that it is “une richesse indépendante et disponible qu’il (the owner) n’a point achetée et qu’il vend.” [a wealth that is independent and available, which he ... has not bought and which he sells] (Turgot: “Réflexions sur la Formation et la Distribution des Richesses,” p.11.)

<!-- ch18-p024 -->
Capital, therefore, is not only, as Adam Smith says, the command over labor. It is essentially the command over unpaid labor. All surplus-value, whatever particular form (profit, interest, or rent) it may later crystallize into, is in substance the materialization of unpaid labor. The secret of the self-expansion of capital comes down to the power to dispose of a definite quantity of other people’s unpaid labor.
