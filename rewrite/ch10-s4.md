### Section 4: Day and Night Work. The Relay System

<!-- ch10-p085 -->
Considered from the standpoint of creating surplus-value, constant capital, the means of production, exists only to absorb labor, and with every drop of labor a proportional amount of surplus labor. (Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production. Its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) As long as the means of production fail to do this, their mere existence is a relative loss to the capitalist, because for as long as they lie idle they represent a useless advance of capital. And this loss becomes positive and absolute as soon as the break in their use makes extra outlay necessary when work starts again. Extending the working day beyond the limits of the natural day, into the night, is only a partial remedy. It quenches the vampire thirst for the living blood of labor only to a slight degree. So the built-in tendency of capitalist production is to appropriate labor during all the 24 hours of the day. But it is physically impossible to exploit the same individual labor-power constantly, by night as well as by day. To overcome this physical obstacle, an alternation becomes necessary between the workers whose powers are exhausted by day and those who are used up by night. This alternation can be arranged in various ways. For example, it may be arranged so that part of the workers are employed on day-work one week and on night-work the next. It is well known that this relay system, this alternation of two sets of workers, held full sway in the full-blooded youth of the English cotton industry, and that today it still flourishes, among other places, in the cotton spinning of the Moscow district. This 24-hour process of production exists today as a system in many branches of industry in Great Britain that are still “free”: in the blast-furnaces, forges, plate-rolling mills, and other metalworking establishments in England, Wales, and Scotland. Here the working time includes not only the 24 hours of the 6 working days, but also a great part of the 24 hours of Sunday. The workers are men and women, adults and children of both sexes. The ages of the children and young persons run through every grade in between, from 8 (in some cases from 6) to 18.[^60]

[^60]: Children’s Employment Commission. Third Report. London, 1864, pp. iv., v., vi.

In some branches of industry, the girls and women work through the night together with the male workers.[^61]

[^61]: “Both in Staffordshire and in South Wales young girls and women are employed on the pit banks and on the coke heaps, not only by day but also by night. This practice has been often noticed in Reports presented to Parliament, as being attended with great and notorious evils. These females employed with the men, hardly distinguished from them in their dress, and begrimed with dirt and smoke, are exposed to the deterioration of character, arising from the loss of self-respect, which can hardly fail to follow from their unfeminine occupation.” (cited above, 194, p. xxvi. Compare Fourth Report (1865), 61, p. xiii.) The same is true in glass-works.

<!-- ch10-p087 -->
Leaving aside the generally harmful effects of night-labor,[^62] a process of production that runs unbroken through the 24 hours offers very welcome opportunities to exceed the limits of the normal working day, for example in the branches of industry already mentioned, which are extremely exhausting. The official working day usually means 12 hours for each worker, by night or by day. But the overwork beyond this amount is in many cases, to use the words of the English official report, “truly fearful.”[^63]

[^62]: A steel manufacturer who employs children on night-labor remarked: “It seems but natural that boys who work at night cannot sleep and get proper rest by day, but will be running about.” (cited above, Fourth Report, 63, p. xiii.) On the importance of sunlight for keeping up and growing the body, a physician writes: “Light also acts upon the tissues of the body directly in hardening them and supporting their elasticity. The muscles of animals, when they are deprived of a proper amount of light, become soft and inelastic, the nervous power loses its tone from defective stimulation, and the elaboration of all growth seems to be perverted.... In the case of children, constant access to plenty of light during the day, and to the direct rays of the sun for a part of it, is most essential to health. Light assists in the elaboration of good plastic [tissue-forming] blood, and hardens the fibre after it has been laid down. It also acts as a stimulus upon the organs of sight, and by this means brings about more activity in the various cerebral functions.” Dr. W. Strange, Senior Physician of the Worcester General Hospital, from whose work on “Health” (1864) this passage is taken, writes in a letter to Mr. White, one of the commissioners: “I have had opportunities formerly, when in Lancashire, of observing the effects of nightwork upon children, and I have no hesitation in saying, contrary to what some employers were fond of asserting, those children who were subjected to it soon suffered in their health.” (cited above, 284., p. 55.) That such a question should become the material of serious controversy shows plainly how capitalist production acts on the brain-functions of capitalists and their retainers.

> “The practice of boys working at all by day and night turns either in the usual course of things, or at pressing times, seems inevitably to open the door to their not unfrequently working unduly long hours. These hours are, indeed, in some cases, not only cruelly but even incredibly long for children. Amongst a number of boys it will, of course, not unfrequently happen that one or more are from some cause absent. When this happens, their place is made up by one or more boys, who work in the other turn. That this is a well understood system is plain ... from the answer of the manager of some large rolling-mills, who, when I asked him how the place of the boys absent from their turn was made up, ‘I daresay, sir, you know that as well as I do,’ and admitted the fact.”[^65]

[^65]: cited above.

> “At a rolling-mill where the proper hours were from 6 a.m. to 5½ p.m., a boy worked about four nights every week till 8½ p.m. at least ... and this for six months. Another, at 9 years old, sometimes made three 12-hour shifts running, and, when 10, has made two days and two nights running.” A third, “now 10 ... worked from 6 a.m. till 12 p.m. three nights, and till 9 p.m. the other nights.” “Another, now 13, ... worked from 6 p.m. till 12 noon next day, for a week together, and sometimes for three shifts together, e.g., from Monday morning till Tuesday night.” “Another, now 12, has worked in an iron foundry at Stavely from 6 a.m. till 12 p.m. for a fortnight on end; could not do it any more.” “George Allinsworth, age 9, came here as cellar-boy last Friday; next morning we had to begin at 3, so I stopped here all night. Live five miles off. Slept on the floor of the furnace, over head, with an apron under me, and a bit of a jacket over me. The two other days I have been here at 6 a.m. Aye! it *is* hot in here. Before I came here I was nearly a year at the same work at some works in the country. Began there, too, at 3 on Saturday morning – always did, but was very gain [near] home, and could sleep at home. Other days I began at 6 in the morning, and gi’en [given] over at 6 or 7 in the evening,” etc.[^66]

The level of culture of these “labor-powers” must naturally be what appears in the following dialogues with one of the commissioners: Jeremiah Haynes, age 12 — “Four times four is 8; 4 fours are 16. A king is him that has all the money and gold. We have a king (told it is a Queen), they call her the Princess Alexandra. Told that she married the Queen’s son. I don’t know where he lives.” “Christ was a wicked man.” “This girl spelt God as dog, and did not know the name of the queen.” (“Ch. Employment Comm. V. Report, 1866” p. 55, n. 278.) The same system prevails in the glass and paper works as in the metal works already cited. In the paper factories, where the paper is made by machinery, night-work is the rule for all processes except rag-sorting. In some cases night-work, by relays, goes on without a break through the whole week, usually from Sunday night until midnight of the following Saturday. Each week, those on day-work work 5 days of 12 hours and 1 day of 18 hours; those on night-work, 5 nights of 12 hours and 1 of 6 hours. In other cases each set works 24 hours in a row on alternate days, one set working 6 hours on Monday and 18 on Saturday to make up the 24 hours. In still other cases an in-between system prevails, under which everyone employed on the paper-making machinery works 15 or 16 hours every day of the week. This system, says Commissioner Lord, “seems to combine all the evils of both the 12 hours’ and the 24 hours’ relays.” Children under 13, young persons under 18, and women work under this night system. Sometimes, under the 12-hour system, they are forced to work a double turn of 24 hours, because those who ought to relieve them fail to turn up. The evidence proves that boys and girls very often work overtime, which not infrequently stretches to 24 or even 36 hours of unbroken toil. In the continuous and unvarying process of glazing there are girls of 12 who work 14 hours a day for the whole month, “without any regular relief or cessation beyond 2 or, at most, 3 breaks of half an hour each for meals.” In some mills where regular night-work has been entirely given up, overwork goes on to a terrible extent, “and that often in the dirtiest, and in the hottest, and in the most monotonous of the various processes.” (“Ch. Employment Comm. Report IV., 1865,” p. xxxviii, and xxxix.)

Let us now hear how capital itself regards this 24-hour system. The extreme forms of the system, its abuse in the “cruel and incredible” extension of the working day, are naturally passed over in silence. Capital speaks of the system only in its “normal” form.

<!-- ch10-p092 -->
Messrs. Naylor & Vickers are steel manufacturers who employ between 600 and 700 persons. Only 10 percent of these are under 18, and of those, only 20 boys under 18 work in night sets. The firm expresses itself as follows:

<!-- ch10-p093 -->
> “The boys do not suffer from the heat. The temperature is probably from 86° to 90°.... At the forges and in the rolling mills the hands work night and day, in relays, but all the other parts of the work are day-work, i.e.*,* from 6 a.m. to 6 p.m. In the forge the hours are from 12 to 12. Some of the hands always work in the night, without any alternation of day and night work.... We do not find any difference in the health of those who work regularly by night and those who work by day, and probably people can sleep better if they have the same period of rest than if it is changed.... About 20 of the boys under the age of 18 work in the night sets.... We could not well do without lads under 18 working by night. The objection would be the increase in the cost of production.... Skilled hands and the heads in every department are difficult to get, but of lads we could get any number.... But from the small proportion of boys that we employ, the subject *(i.e*., of restrictions on night-work) is of little importance or interest to us.”[^67]

[^67]: Fourth Report, &c., 1865, 79, p. xvi.

<!-- ch10-p094 -->
Mr. J. Ellis belongs to the firm of Messrs. John Brown & Co., steel and iron works, which employs about 3,000 men and boys. Part of the firm’s operations, namely iron and heavier steel work, goes on night and day by relays. Mr. Ellis states “that in the heavier steel work one or two boys are employed to a score or two men.” Their concern employs more than 500 boys under 18, of whom about 1/3, or 170, are under the age of 13. On the proposed change in the law, Mr. Ellis says:

<!-- ch10-p095 -->
> “I do not think it would be very objectionable to require that no person under the age of 18 should work more than 12 hours in the 24. But we do not think that any line could be drawn over the age of 12, at which boys could be dispensed with for night-work. But we would sooner be prevented from employing boys under the age of 13, or even so high as 14, at all, than not be allowed to employ boys that we do have at night. Those boys who work in the day sets must take their turn in the night sets also, because the men could not work in the night sets only; it would ruin their health.... We think, however, that night-work in alternate weeks is no harm.”

<!-- ch10-p096 -->
(Messrs. Naylor & Vickers, on the other hand, in keeping with the interest of their business, thought that night-labor changed at regular intervals might possibly do more harm than continual night-labor.)

<!-- ch10-p097 -->
> “We find the men who do it, as well as the others who do other work only by day.... Our objections to not allowing boys under 18 to work at night, would be on account of the increase of expense, but this is the only reason.”

<!-- ch10-p098 -->
> (What cynical naïveté!) “We think that the increase would be more than the trade, with due regard to its being successfully carried out, could fairly bear. (What mealy-mouthed wording!) Labour is scarce here, and might fall short if there were such a regulation.” (that is, Ellis Brown & Co. might fall into the fatal predicament of having to pay labor-power its full value.)[^68]

The “Cyclops Steel and Iron Works” of Messrs. Cammell & Co. are set up on the same large scale as those of John Brown & Co., mentioned above. On the same subject Mr. E. F. Sanderson, of the firm of Sanderson, Bros., & Co., steel rolling-mills and forges, Attercliffe, says:

<!-- ch10-p101 -->
> “Great difficulty would be caused by preventing boys under 18 from working at night. The chief would be the increase of cost from employing men instead of boys. I cannot say what this would be, but probably it would not be enough to enable the manufacturers to raise the price of steel, and consequently it would fall on them, as of course the men (what odd-headed folk!) would refuse to pay it.”

<!-- ch10-p102 -->
Mr. Sanderson does not know how much he pays the children, but

<!-- ch10-p103 -->
> “perhaps the younger boys get from 4s. to 5s. a week.... The boys’ work is of a kind for which the strength of the boys is generally (‘generally,’ of course not always) quite sufficient, and consequently there would be no gain in the greater strength of the men to counterbalance the loss, or it would be only in the few cases in which the metal is heavy. The men would not like so well not to have boys under them, as men would be less obedient. Besides, boys must begin young to learn the trade. Leaving day-work alone open to boys would not answer this purpose.”

<!-- ch10-p104 -->
And why not? Why could boys not learn their handicraft in the daytime? Your reason?

<!-- ch10-p105 -->
> “Owing to the men working days and nights in alternate weeks, the men would be separated half the time from their boys, and would lose half the profit which they make from them. The training which they give to an apprentice is considered as part of the return for the boys’ labour, and thus enables the man to get it at a cheaper rate. Each man would want half of this profit.”

<!-- ch10-p106 -->
In other words, Messrs. Sanderson would have to pay part of the adult men’s wages out of their own pockets, instead of paying it with the night-work of the boys. Messrs. Sanderson’s profit would then fall to some extent, and this is the good Sandersonian reason why boys cannot learn their handicraft in the day.[^70] Besides this, it would throw night-labor on those who worked in place of the boys, and they would not be able to stand it. In fact the difficulties would be so great that they would very likely lead to night-work being given up altogether, and “as far as the work itself is concerned,” says E. F. Sanderson, “this would suit as well, but –” But Messrs. Sanderson have something else to make besides steel. Steel-making is simply a pretext for surplus-value making. The smelting furnaces, rolling-mills, etc., the buildings, machinery, iron, coal, etc., have something more to do than turn themselves into steel. They are there to absorb surplus labor, and naturally they absorb more in 24 hours than in 12. In fact, by the grace of God and the law, they give the Sandersons a check drawn on the working time of a certain number of workers for all the 24 hours of the day. And as soon as their function of absorbing labor is interrupted, they lose their character as capital, and so are a pure loss for the Sandersons.

But why should these Sandersons claim a privilege not enjoyed by the other capitalists, who work only during the day, and whose buildings, machinery, and raw material therefore lie “idle” during the night? E. F. Sanderson answers in the name of all the Sandersons:

<!-- ch10-p109 -->
> “It is true that there is this loss from machinery lying idle in those manufactories in which work only goes on by day. But the use of furnaces would involve a further loss in our case. If they were kept up there would be a waste of fuel (instead of, as now, a waste of the living substance of the workers), and if they were not, there would be loss of time in laying the fires and getting the heat up (while the loss of sleeping time, even to children of 8, is a gain of working time for the Sanderson tribe), and the furnaces themselves would suffer from the changes of temperature.” (While those same furnaces suffer nothing from the day and night change of labor.)[^71]

[^71]: cited above, 85, p. xvii. The glass manufacturers have similar tender scruples: regular meal-times for the children are impossible, they say, because then a certain quantity of heat radiated by the furnaces would be “a pure loss” or “wasted.” Commissioner White answers them. His answer is unlike that of Ure, Senior, etc., and of their puny German plagiarists in Roscher’s style, who are touched by the “abstinence,” “self-denial,” and “saving” of the capitalists in spending their gold, and by their Timur-Tamerlanish lavishness with human life! “A certain amount of heat beyond what is usual at present might also be going to waste, if meal-times were secured in these cases, but it seems likely not equal in money-value to the waste of animal power now going on in glass-houses throughout the kingdom from growing boys not having enough quiet time to eat their meals at ease, with a little rest afterwards for digestion.” (cited above, p. xiv.) And this in the year of progress 1865! Leaving aside the strength spent in lifting and carrying, such a child, in the sheds where bottle and flint glass are made, walks 15-20 miles in every 6 hours while doing his work! And the work often lasts 14 or 15 hours! In many of these glass works, as in the Moscow spinning mills, the system of 6 hours’ relays is in force. “During the working part of the week six hours is the utmost unbroken period ever attained at any one time for rest, and out of this has to come the time spent in coming and going to and from work, washing, dressing, and meals, leaving a very short period indeed for rest, and none for fresh air and play, unless at the expense of the sleep necessary for young boys, especially at such hot and fatiguing work.... Even the short sleep is obviously liable to be broken by a boy having to wake himself if it is night, or by the noise, if it is day.” Mr. White gives cases where a boy worked 36 hours in a row; others where boys of 12 drudged on until 2 in the morning, and then slept in the works till 5 a.m. (3 hours!) only to start work again. “The amount of work,” say Tremenheere and Tufnell, who drafted the general report, “done by boys, youths, girls, and women, in the course of their daily or nightly spell of labour, is certainly extraordinary.” (cited above, xliii. and xliv.) Meanwhile, late at night, self-denying Mr. Glass-Capital, primed with port-wine, reels out of his club on his way home, droning idiotically: “Britons never, never shall be slaves!”
