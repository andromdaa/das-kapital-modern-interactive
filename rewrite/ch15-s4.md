### Section 4: The Factory

<!-- ch15-p097 -->
At the beginning of this chapter we looked at what we may call the body of the factory, that is, machinery organized into a system. We saw there how machinery, by annexing the labor of women and children, increases the number of human beings who form the material for capitalist exploitation. We saw how it confiscates the whole of the worker’s disposable time by stretching the hours of labor beyond all measure. And we saw how, finally, its progress, which allows an enormous increase of production in shorter and shorter periods, serves as a means of systematically getting more work done in a shorter time, or of exploiting labor-power more intensely. We now turn to the factory as a whole, and to the factory in its most perfect form.

<!-- ch15-p098 -->
Dr. Ure, the Pindar of the automatic factory, describes it, on the one hand, as

<!-- ch15-p099 -->
> “Combined co-operation of many orders of workpeople, adult and young, in tending with assiduous skill, a system of productive machines, continuously impelled by a central power” (the prime mover); on the other hand, as “a vast automaton, composed of various mechanical and intellectual organs, acting in uninterrupted concert for the production of a common object, all of them being subordinate to a self-regulated moving force.”

<!-- ch15-p100 -->
These two descriptions are far from identical. In the first, the collective worker, or social body of labor, appears as the dominant subject, and the mechanical automaton as the object. In the second, the automaton itself is the subject, and the workers are merely conscious organs, set alongside the unconscious organs of the automaton and, together with them, subordinated to the central moving power. The first description applies to every possible use of machinery on a large scale. The second is characteristic of its use by capital, and therefore of the modern factory system. Ure therefore prefers to describe the central machine, from which the motion comes, not only as an automaton but as an autocrat. “In these spacious halls the benignant power of steam summons around him his myriads of willing menials.”[^97]

[^97]: Ure, cited above, p. 18.

<!-- ch15-p101 -->
Along with the tool, the worker’s skill in handling it passes over to the machine. The capabilities of the tool are freed from the limits that are inseparable from human labor-power. This sweeps away the technical foundation on which the division of labor in manufacture is based. So in the automatic factory, the hierarchy of specialized workers that characterizes manufacture is replaced by a tendency to equalize and reduce to one and the same level every kind of work that the minders of the machines have to do;[^98] in place of the artificially produced differences between the detail workers, there step the natural differences of age and sex.

[^98]: Ure, cited above, P. 3 1. See Karl Marx, cited above, pp. 140-141.

<!-- ch15-p102 -->
To the extent that division of labor reappears in the factory, it is primarily a distribution of the workers among the specialized machines. It is also a distribution of masses of workers, who are not, however, organized into groups, among the various departments of the factory, in each of which they work at a number of similar machines placed together. So their cooperation is only simple. The organized group peculiar to manufacture is replaced by the connection between the head worker and his few assistants. The essential division is into workers who are actually employed on the machines (among whom are included a few who look after the engine), and mere attendants of these workers (almost exclusively children). More or less all the “Feeders,” who supply the machines with the material to be worked, are counted among the attendants. In addition to these two principal classes, there is a class of persons, unimportant in numbers, whose job is to look after the whole of the machinery and repair it from time to time: engineers, mechanics, joiners, etc. This is a superior class of workers, some of them scientifically educated, others brought up to a trade. It is distinct from the class of factory workers, and merely lumped together with it.[^99] This division of labor is purely technical.

[^99]: It looks very much like deliberate misleading by statistics (misleading that could be proved in detail in other cases too) when the English factory legislation excludes from its operation the class of workers last mentioned in the text, while the parliamentary returns expressly include in the category of factory workers not only engineers, mechanics, etc., but also managers, salesmen, messengers, warehousemen, packers, etc., in short everybody except the owner of the factory himself.

<!-- ch15-p103 -->
To work at a machine, the worker should be taught from childhood, so that he may learn to adapt his own movements to the uniform and unceasing motion of an automaton. When the machinery as a whole forms a system of many different machines, working at the same time and in concert, the cooperation based on it requires the distribution of various groups of workers among the different kinds of machines. But the use of machinery does away with the need to fix this distribution permanently, in the way manufacture does, by constantly tying a particular man to a particular function.[^100] Since the motion of the whole system does not come from the worker but from the machinery, people can be changed at any time without interrupting the work. The most striking proof of this is the *relay system* (a system of shifts in which workers, especially children, were switched in and out at different times, which made the legal hours hard to check), which the manufacturers put into operation during their revolt from 1848-1850. Lastly, young people learn machine work so quickly that there is no need to bring up a special class of factory workers to be employed exclusively by machinery.[^101] As for the work of the mere attendants, it can to some extent be replaced in the mill by machines,[^102] and because it is extremely simple, it allows a rapid and constant change of the individuals burdened with this drudgery.

[^100]: Ure admits this. He says that “in case of need” the workers can be moved at the manager’s will from one machine to another, and he exclaims in triumph: “Such a change is in flat contradiction with the old routine, that divides the labour, and to one workman assigns the task of fashioning the head of a needle, to another the sharpening of the point.” He would have done much better to ask himself why this “old routine” is departed from in the automatic factory only “in case of need.”

[^101]: When distress is very great, as for instance during the American Civil War, the factory worker is now and then set by the bourgeois to do the roughest of work, such as road-making, etc. The English “ateliers nationaux” [national workshops] of 1862 and the following years, set up for the benefit of the destitute cotton workers, differ from the French ones of 1848 in this: in the French ones, the workers had to do unproductive work at the expense of the state; in the English ones, they had to do productive municipal work to the advantage of the bourgeois, and at that, more cheaply than the regular workers, with whom they were thus thrown into competition. “The physical appearance of the cotton operatives is unquestionably improved. This I attribute ... as to the men, to outdoor labour on public works.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1863,” p. 59.) The writer is referring here to the Preston factory workers, who were employed on Preston Moor.

[^102]: An example: the various mechanical devices introduced into woolen mills since the Act of 1844 to replace the labor of children. As soon as it happens that the manufacturers’ own children have to go through a course of schooling as helpers in the mill, this almost unexplored territory of mechanics will soon make remarkable progress. “Of machinery, perhaps self-acting mules are as dangerous as any other kind. Most of the accidents from them happen to little children, from their creeping under the mules to sweep the floor whilst the mules are in motion. Several ‘minders’ have been fined for this offence, but without much general benefit. If machine makers would only invent a self-sweeper, by whose use the necessity for these little children to creep under the machinery might be prevented, it would be a happy addition to our protective measures.” (“Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 31st. Oct., 1866,” p. 63.)

<!-- ch15-p104 -->
So although, technically speaking, machinery throws the old system of division of labor overboard, that system hangs on in the factory as a traditional habit handed down from manufacture. Afterwards capital systematically remolds it and establishes it in a more hideous form, as a means of exploiting labor-power. The lifelong specialty of handling one and the same tool now becomes the lifelong specialty of serving one and the same machine. Machinery is misused in order to turn the worker, from his very childhood, into a part of a detail machine.[^103] In this way, not only are the costs of his reproduction considerably reduced, but at the same time his helpless dependence on the factory as a whole, and therefore on the capitalist, is made complete. Here as everywhere else, we must distinguish between the increased productivity due to the development of the social process of production and the increased productivity due to the capitalist exploitation of that process. In handicrafts and manufacture, the worker makes use of a tool; in the factory, the machine makes use of him. There, the movements of the instrument of labor come from him; here, it is the movements of the machine that he must follow. In manufacture the workers are parts of a living mechanism. In the factory we have a lifeless mechanism that is independent of the worker, who becomes its mere living appendage.

[^103]: So much, then, for Proudhon’s wonderful idea: he “construes” machinery not as a synthesis of instruments of labor, but as a synthesis of detail operations for the benefit of the worker himself.

<!-- ch15-p105 -->
> “The miserable routine of endless drudgery and toil in which the same mechanical process is gone through over and over again, is like the labour of Sisyphus. The burden of labour, like the rock, keeps ever falling back on the worn-out labourer.”[^104]

[^104]: F. Engels, cited above, p. 217. Even an ordinary and optimistic Free-trader like Mr. Molinari goes so far as to say: “Un homme s’use plus vite en surveillant, quinze heures par jour, l’évolution uniforme d’un mécanisme, qu’en exercant, dans le même espace de temps, sa force physique. Ce travail de surveillance qui servirait peut-être d’utile gymnastique à l’intelligence, s’il n’était pas trop prolongé, détruit à la longue, par son excès, et l’intelligence, et le corps même.” [A man wears out faster by watching the uniform movement of a mechanism for fifteen hours a day than by using his physical strength for the same length of time. This work of watching, which might perhaps serve as useful exercise for the mind if it did not go on too long, in the long run destroys, by its excess, both the mind and the body itself.] (G. de Molinari: “Études Économiques.” Paris, 1846.)

<!-- ch15-p106 -->
At the same time that factory work exhausts the nervous system to the utmost, it does away with the many-sided play of the muscles and confiscates every atom of freedom, both in bodily and in intellectual activity.[^105] Even the lightening of the labor becomes a sort of torture, since the machine does not free the worker from work, but deprives the work of all interest. Every kind of capitalist production, insofar as it is not only a labor process but also a process of creating surplus-value, has this in common: it is not the worker who employs the instruments of labor, but the instruments of labor that employ the worker. But it is only in the factory system that this inversion for the first time acquires a technical and tangible reality. By being turned into an automaton, the instrument of labor confronts the worker, during the labor process, in the shape of capital, of dead labor, which dominates living labor-power and pumps it dry. The separation of the intellectual powers of production from manual labor, and the conversion of those powers into the might of capital over labor, is, as we have already shown, finally completed by modern industry built on the foundation of machinery. The special skill of each individual, insignificant factory worker vanishes as an infinitely small quantity before the science, the gigantic physical forces, and the mass of labor that are embodied in the factory mechanism and that, together with that mechanism, make up the power of the “master.” So this “master,” in whose brain the machinery and his monopoly of it are inseparably united, contemptuously tells his “hands,” whenever he falls out with them:

[^105]: F. Engels, cited above, p. 216.

<!-- ch15-p107 -->
> “The factory operatives should keep in wholesome remembrance the fact that theirs is really a low species of skilled labour; and that there is none which is more easily acquired, or of its quality more amply remunerated, or which by a short training of the least expert can be more quickly, as well as abundantly, acquired.... The master’s machinery really plays a far more important part in the business of production than the labour and the skill of the operative, which six months’ education can teach, and a common labourer can learn.”[^106]

[^106]: “The Master Spinners’ and Manufacturers’ Defence Fund. Report of the Committee.” Manchester, 1854, p. 17. We shall see later that the “master” can sing quite another song when he is threatened with the loss of his “living” automaton.

<!-- ch15-p108 -->
The technical subordination of the worker to the uniform motion of the instruments of labor, and the peculiar makeup of the body of workers, consisting as it does of individuals of both sexes and of all ages, give rise to a barrack discipline. In the factory this discipline is worked out into a complete system. It fully develops the labor of overseeing mentioned earlier, and so divides the workers into factory workers and overseers, into the privates and sergeants of an industrial army. “The main difficulty [in the automatic factory] ... lay ... above all in training human beings to renounce their desultory [irregular] habits of work, and to identify themselves with the unvarying regularity of the complex automaton. To devise and administer a successful code of factory discipline, suited to the necessities of factory diligence, was the Herculean enterprise, the noble achievement of Arkwright! Even at the present day, when the system is perfectly organised and its labour lightened to the utmost, it is found nearly impossible to convert persons past the age of puberty, into useful factory hands.”[^107] In the factory code, capital, like a private legislator and at his own good will, formulates his autocracy over his workers. This code comes without the division of responsibility that the bourgeoisie approves of so much in other matters, and without the representative system that it approves of even more. It is only the capitalist caricature of the social regulation of the labor process that becomes necessary in cooperation on a large scale and in the common use of instruments of labor, especially of machinery. The overseer’s book of penalties takes the place of the slave-driver’s lash. All punishments naturally come down to fines and deductions from wages, and the law-giving talent of the factory Lycurgus arranges matters so that breaking his laws is, if possible, more profitable to him than keeping them.[^108] Here we shall merely touch on the material conditions under which factory labor is carried on. Every organ of sense is injured equally by the artificially raised temperature, by the dust-laden air, and by the deafening noise, not to mention the danger to life and limb among the thickly crowded machinery, which, with the regularity of the seasons, issues its list of the killed and wounded in the industrial battle.[^109] Economy in the social means of production, matured and forced as in a hothouse by the factory system, is turned, in the hands of capital, into systematic robbery of what the worker needs for life while he is at work: robbery of space, light, and air, and of protection for his person against the dangerous and unhealthy accompaniments of the production process, not to mention robbery of facilities for the worker’s comfort.[^110] Is Fourier wrong when he calls factories “tempered bagnos [convict prisons]”?[^111]

[^107]: Ure, cited above, p. 15. Anyone who knows the life history of Arkwright will never call this barber-genius “noble.” Of all the great inventors of the 18th century, he was beyond dispute the greatest thief of other people’s inventions and the meanest fellow.

[^108]: “The slavery in which the bourgeoisie has bound the proletariat [the class of wage workers], comes nowhere more plainly into daylight than in the factory system. In it all freedom comes to an end both at law and in fact. The workman must be in the factory at half past five. If he come a few minutes late, he is punished; if he come 10 minutes late, he is not allowed to enter until after breakfast, and thus loses a quarter of a day’s wage. He must eat, drink and sleep at word of command.... The despotic bell calls him from his bed, calls him from breakfast and dinner. And how does he fare in the mill? There the master is the absolute law-giver. He makes what regulations he pleases; he alters and makes additions to his code at pleasure; and if he insert the veriest nonsense, the courts say to the workman: Since you have entered into this contract voluntarily, you must now carry it out .... These workmen are condemned to live, from their ninth year till their death, under this mental and bodily torture.” (F. Engels, cited above, p. 217, and following.) What “the courts say,” I will illustrate with two examples. The first occurred at Sheffield at the end of 1866. In that town a worker had bound himself for 2 years to a steelworks. After a quarrel with his employer he left the works, and declared that under no circumstances would he work for that employer any more. He was prosecuted for breach of contract and sentenced to two months’ imprisonment. (If the employer breaks the contract, he can be proceeded against only in a civil action, and risks nothing but money damages.) After the worker had served his two months, the employer invited him to return to the works, as the contract required. The worker said no: he had already been punished for the breach. The employer prosecuted again, and the court condemned him again, although one of the judges, Mr. Shee, publicly denounced this as a legal monstrosity, by which a man can be punished periodically, as long as he lives, over and over again for the same offense or crime. This judgment was given not by the “Great Unpaid,” the provincial Dogberries (after the constable in Shakespeare’s *Much Ado About Nothing*), but by one of the highest courts of justice in London. — [Added *in the 4th German edition. — This* has now been done away with. With few exceptions, for example when public gas-works are involved, the worker in England is now put on an equal footing with the employer in case of breach of contract and can be sued only in a civil action. — F. E.] The second case occurred in Wiltshire at the end of November 1863. About 30 power-loom weavers, employed by one Harrup, a cloth manufacturer at Leower’s Mill, Westbury Leigh, went on strike because master Harrup had the agreeable habit of making deductions from their wages for being late in the morning: 6d. for 2 minutes, 1s. for 3 minutes, and 1s. 6d. for ten minutes. This works out at 9s. per hour and £4 10s. 0d. per day, while the weavers’ wages, averaged over a year, never exceeded 10s. to 12s. a week. Harrup also appointed a boy to announce the starting time with a whistle, which he often blew before six o’clock in the morning. If the workers were not all there at the moment the whistle stopped, the doors were closed, and those workers who were outside were fined. And as there was no clock on the premises, the unfortunate workers were at the mercy of the young Harrup-inspired timekeeper. The workers on strike, mothers of families as well as girls, offered to go back to work if the timekeeper were replaced by a clock and a more reasonable scale of fines were introduced. Harrup summoned 19 women and girls before the magistrates for breach of contract. To the utter indignation of everyone present, they were each fined 6d., plus 2s. 6d. for costs. A crowd of people followed Harrup out of the court, hissing him. A favorite practice of manufacturers is to punish the workers by making deductions from their wages for faults in the material worked on. In 1866 this method gave rise to a general strike in the English pottery districts. The reports of the Ch. Empl. Com. (1863-1866) give cases where the worker not only receives no wages, but, through his labor and the penal regulations, ends up as the debtor of his worthy employer into the bargain. The recent cotton crisis also supplied edifying examples of the shrewdness the factory autocrats showed in making deductions from wages. Mr. R. Baker, the Inspector of Factories, says: “I have myself had lately to direct prosecutions against one cotton mill occupier for having in these pinching and painful times deducted 10d. a piece from some of the young workers employed by him, for the surgeon’s certificate (for which he himself had only paid 6d.), when only allowed by the law to deduct 3d., and by custom nothing at all .... And I have been informed of another, who, in order to keep without [outside] the law, but to attain the same object, charges the poor children who work for him a shilling each, as a fee for learning them the art and mystery of cotton spinning, so soon as they are declared by the surgeon fit and proper persons for that occupation. There may therefore be undercurrent causes for such extraordinary exhibitions as strikes, not only wherever they arise, but particularly at such times as the present, which without explanation, render them inexplicable to the public understanding.” He is referring here to a strike of power-loom weavers at Darwen, June, 1863. (“Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 30 April, 1863,” pp. 50-51.) The reports always go beyond their official dates.

[^109]: The protection that the Factory Acts give against dangerous machinery has had a beneficial effect. “But ... there are other sources of accident which did not exist twenty years since; one especially, viz., the increased speed of the machinery. Wheels, rollers, spindles and shuttles are now propelled at increased and increasing rates; fingers must be quicker and defter in their movements to take up the broken thread, for, if placed with hesitation or carelessness, they are sacrificed.... A large number of accidents are caused by the eagerness of the workpeople to get through their work expeditiously. It must be remembered that it is of the highest importance to manufacturers that their machinery should be in motion, i.e., producing yarns and goods. Every minute’s stoppage is not only a loss of power, but of production, and the workpeople are urged by the overlookers, who are interested in the quantity of work turned off, to keep the machinery in motion, and it is no less important to those of the operatives who are paid by the weight or piece, that the machines should be kept in motion. Consequently, although it is strictly forbidden in many, nay in most factories, that machinery should be cleaned while in motion, it is nevertheless the constant practice in most, if not in all, that the workpeople do, unreproved, pick out waste, wipe rollers and wheels, &c., while their frames are in motion. Thus from this cause only, 906 accidents have occurred during the six months.... Although a great deal of cleaning is constantly going on day by day, yet Saturday is generally the day set apart for the thorough cleaning of the machinery, and a great deal of this is done while the machinery is in motion.” Since cleaning is not paid for, the workers try to get it done as quickly as possible. Hence “the number of accidents which occur on Fridays, and especially on Saturdays, is much larger than on any other day. On the former day the excess is nearly 12 per cent. over the average number of the four first days of the week, and on the latter day the excess is 25 per cent. over the average of the preceding five days; or, if the number of working-hours on Saturday being taken into account — 7½ hours on Saturday as compared with 10½ on other days — there is an excess of 65 per cent. on Saturdays over the average of the other five days.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1866,” pp. 9, 15, 16, 17.)

[^110]: In Part I. of Book III. I shall give an account of a recent campaign by the English manufacturers against the clauses in the Factory Acts that protect the “hands” against dangerous machinery. For now, let this one quotation from the official report of Leonard Horner be enough: “I have heard some mill-owners speak with inexcusable levity of some of the accidents; such, for instance, as the loss of a finger being a trifling matter. A working-man’s living and prospects depend so much upon his fingers, that any loss of them is a very serious matter to him. When I have heard such inconsiderate remarks made, I have usually put this question: Suppose you were in want of an additional workman, and two were to apply, both equally well qualified in other respects, but one had lost a thumb or a forefinger, which would you engage? There never was a hesitation as to the answer....” The manufacturers have “mistaken prejudices against what they have heard represented as a pseudo-philanthropic legislation.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st Oct., 1855.”) These manufacturers are clever folk, and not without reason were they enthusiastic for the slave-holders’ rebellion.

[^111]: In the factories that have been subject longest to the Factory Acts, with their compulsory limitation of the hours of labor and other regulations, many of the older abuses have vanished. The very improvement of the machinery demands, to a certain extent, “improved construction of the buildings,” and this is an advantage to the workers. (See “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1863,” p. 109.)
