### Section 3: The Proximate Effects of Machinery on the Workman

<!-- ch15-p037 -->
As we have shown, the starting-point of modern industry is the revolution in the instruments of labor, and this revolution reaches its most highly developed form in the organized system of machinery in a factory. Before we ask how human material is built into this objective organism, let us look at some general effects of this revolution on the worker himself.

#### A. Appropriation of Supplementary Labor-Power by Capital. The Employment of Women and Children

<!-- ch15-p038 -->
To the extent that machinery makes muscular power unnecessary, it becomes a means of employing workers who have little muscular strength, and workers whose bodies are not yet fully developed but whose limbs are all the more supple. So the labor of women and children was the first thing that capitalists who used machinery went looking for. That mighty substitute for labor and for workers was at once turned into a means of increasing the number of wage workers. It did this by enrolling every member of the worker’s family, without distinction of age or sex, under the direct rule of capital. Forced work for the capitalist took the place not only of children’s play, but also of free labor at home, within moderate limits, for the family’s own support.[^38]

[^38]: During the cotton crisis caused by the American Civil War, the English Government sent Dr. Edward Smith to Lancashire, Cheshire, and other places to report on the sanitary condition of the cotton factory workers. He reported that from a health point of view, and apart from the workers’ banishment from the factory atmosphere, the crisis had several advantages. The women now had enough free time to breastfeed their infants, instead of poisoning them with “Godfrey’s cordial.” They had time to learn to cook. Unfortunately, they acquired this art at a time when they had nothing to cook. But from this we see how capital, for the purposes of its self-expansion, has seized for itself the labor that the family needs at home. The crisis was also used to teach sewing to the workers’ daughters in sewing schools. An American revolution and a worldwide crisis, so that the working girls who spin for the whole world might learn to sew!

<!-- ch15-p039 -->
The value of labor-power was determined not only by the labor-time needed to keep the individual adult worker alive, but also by the labor-time needed to keep his family alive. Machinery throws every member of that family onto the labor market, and so it spreads the value of the man’s labor-power over his whole family. In this way it lowers the value of his labor-power. Buying the labor-power of a family of four workers may perhaps cost more than buying the labor-power of the head of the family used to cost. But in return, four days’ labor takes the place of one, and their price falls in proportion to the excess of the surplus labor of four over the surplus labor of one. For the family to live, four people must now not only labor, but also perform surplus labor for the capitalist. So we see that machinery, while it increases the human material that is the main object of capital’s power to exploit,[^39] at the same time raises the degree of exploitation.

[^39]: “The numerical increase of labourers has been great, through the growing substitution of female for male, and above all, of childish for adult labour. Three girls of 13, at wages of from 6 shillings to 8 shillings a week, have replaced the one man of mature age, of wages varying from 18 shillings to 45 shillings.” (Th. de Quincey: “The Logic of Political Econ.,” London, 1844. Note to p. 147.) Some family functions, such as nursing and breastfeeding children, cannot be suppressed entirely, so the mothers whom capital has confiscated must try substitutes of some sort. Domestic work, such as sewing and mending, has to be replaced by buying ready-made articles. So the smaller amount of labor spent in the house goes together with a larger amount of money spent. The cost of keeping the family rises and cancels out the greater income. On top of this, thrift and good judgment in using and preparing the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) become impossible. Plenty of material on these facts, which official political economy hides, can be found in the Reports of the Inspectors of Factories, in those of the Children’s Employment Commission, and above all in the Reports on Public Health.

<!-- ch15-p040 -->
Machinery also completely revolutionizes the contract between the worker and the capitalist, the contract that formally fixes their relations to each other. Taking the exchange of commodities as our basis, we first assumed that capitalist and worker met as free persons, as independent owners of commodities: the one owning money and means of production, the other owning labor-power. But now the capitalist buys children and young persons who are under age. Before, the worker sold his own labor-power, which he disposed of, in name at least, as a free agent. Now he sells his wife and child. He has become a slave-dealer.[^40] The demand for children’s labor often looks, in its form, like the inquiries for Black slaves that used to be read among the advertisements in American newspapers.

[^40]: The great fact is that the male factory workers forced capital to shorten the hours of labor of women and children in English factories. In striking contrast with this, the latest reports of the Children’s Employment Commission show traits of the working parents, in their traffic in children, that are truly revolting and thoroughly like slave-dealing. But the Pharisee of a capitalist, as the same reports show, denounces this brutality, which he himself creates, keeps going, and exploits, and which he moreover baptizes “freedom of labour.” “Infant labour has been called into aid ... even to work for their own daily bread. Without strength to endure such disproportionate toil, without instruction to guide their future life, they have been thrown into a situation physically and morally polluted. The Jewish historian has remarked upon the overthrow of Jerusalem by Titus that it was no wonder it should have been destroyed, with such a signal destruction, when an inhuman mother sacrificed her own offspring to satisfy the cravings of absolute hunger.” (“Public Economy Concentrated.” Carlisle, 1833, p. 66.)

> “My attention,” says an English factory inspector, “was drawn to an advertisement in the local paper of one of the most important manufacturing towns of my district, of which the following is a copy: Wanted, 12 to 20 young persons, not younger than what can pass for 13 years. Wages, 4 shillings a week. Apply &c.”[^41]

[^41]: A. Redgrave in “Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 31st October, 1858,” pp. 40, 41.

<!-- ch15-p042 -->
The phrase “what can pass for 13 years,” refers to the fact that under the Factory Act, children under 13 years may work only 6 hours. An officially appointed surgeon must certify their age. So the manufacturer asks for children who look as if they were already 13 years old. The English statistics of the last 20 years show, in an astonishing way, a decrease, often by leaps and bounds, in the number of children under 13 years employed in factories. According to the evidence of the factory inspectors themselves, this decrease was for the most part the work of the certifying surgeons. They overstated the children’s age, to suit the capitalist’s greed for exploitation and the parents’ sordid need to traffic in their children. In the notorious district of Bethnal Green, a public market is held every Monday and Tuesday morning, where children of both sexes from 9 years of age upwards hire themselves out to the silk manufacturers. “The usual terms are 1s. 8d. a week (this belongs to the parents) and ‘2d. for myself and tea.’ The contract is binding only for the week. The scene and language while this market is going on are quite disgraceful.”[^42] It has also happened in England that women have taken “children from the workhouse and let any one have them out for 2s. 6d. a week.”[^43] In spite of legislation, more than 2,000 boys in Great Britain are sold by their parents to serve as live chimney-sweeping machines (although plenty of machines exist to replace them).[^44] Machinery revolutionized the legal relations between the buyer and the seller of labor-power, so that the transaction as a whole lost the appearance of a contract between free persons. This gave the English Parliament an excuse, based on legal principles, for the state to interfere with factories. Whenever the law limits children’s labor to 6 hours in industries that were not regulated before, the manufacturers always renew their complaints. They claim that many parents take their children out of the industry brought under the Act, in order to sell them where “freedom of labour” still rules, that is, where children under 13 years are forced to work like grown-ups and so can be sold off at a higher price. But capital is by nature a leveler: in every sphere of production it demands equal conditions for exploiting labor. So the legal limitation of children’s labor in one branch of industry becomes the cause of its limitation in others.

[^42]: “Children’s Employment Commission, Fifth Report,” London, 1866, p. 81, n. 31. *[Added in the 4th German edition. — The Bethnal Green silk industry is now almost destroyed. — F. E.*]

[^43]: “Children’s Employment Commission, Third Report,” London, 1864, p. 53, n. 15.

[^44]: Cited above, Fifth Report, p. 22, n. 137.

We have already referred to the physical decline of the children and young persons, and also of the women, whom machinery subjects to the exploitation of capital: first directly, in the factories that shoot up on its basis, and then indirectly, in all the other branches of industry. Here, therefore, we dwell on only one point: the enormous death rate of the factory workers’ children during the first few years of their life. England is divided into registration districts. In sixteen of them, for every 100,000 children alive under the age of one year, there are on average only 9,000 deaths a year (in one district only 7,047). In 24 districts the deaths are over 10,000, but under 11,000; in 39 districts, over 11,000, but under 12,000; in 48 districts over 12,000, but under 13,000; in 22 districts over 20,000; in 25 districts over 21,000; in 17 over 22,000; in 11 over 23,000; in Hoo, Wolverhampton, Ashton-under-Lyne, and Preston, over 24,000; in Nottingham, Stockport, and Bradford, over 25,000; in Wisbeach, 16,000; and in Manchester, 26,125.[^45] An official medical inquiry in the year 1861 showed that, apart from local causes, the high death-rates are mainly due to the mothers being employed away from their homes. They are also mainly due to the neglect and mistreatment that follow from the mother’s absence, such as, among other things, too little nourishment, unsuitable food, and dosing with opiates. Besides this, an unnatural estrangement arises between mother and child, and as a result children are deliberately starved and poisoned.[^46] In those agricultural districts “where a minimum in the employment of women exists, the death-rate is on the other hand very low.”[^47] The Inquiry Commission of 1861, however, led to an unexpected result: in some purely agricultural districts bordering on the North Sea, the death-rate of children under one year old almost equaled that of the worst factory districts. So Dr. Julian Hunter was commissioned to investigate this phenomenon on the spot. His report is included in the “Sixth Report on Public Health.”[^48] Until then it had been supposed that the children were being decimated by malaria and other diseases typical of low-lying, marshy districts. But the inquiry showed the very opposite. The same cause that drove away malaria, namely, turning the land from a swamp in winter and a thin pasture in summer into fruitful grain land, created the exceptional death-rate of the infants.[^49] The 70 doctors whom Dr. Hunter questioned in that district were “wonderfully in accord” on this point. In fact, the revolution in the way of cultivating the land had brought in the industrial system.

Married women, who work in gangs along with boys and girls, are placed at the farmer’s disposal for an agreed sum of money by a man called the “undertaker,” who contracts for the whole gang. “These gangs will sometimes travel many miles from their own village; they are to be met morning and evening on the roads, dressed in short petticoats, with suitable coats and boots, and sometimes trousers, looking wonderfully strong and healthy, but tainted with a customary immorality and heedless of the fatal results which their love of this busy and independent life is bringing on their unfortunate offspring who are pining at home.”[^50]

[^50]: Cited above, p. 456.

<!-- ch15-p045 -->
Every phenomenon of the factory districts is reproduced here, including, but to a greater extent, barely disguised infanticide and dosing children with opiates.[^51]

[^51]: In the agricultural districts, as in the factory districts, the use of opium among the grown-up workers, both male and female, is spreading daily. “To push the sale of opiate... is the great aim of some enterprising wholesale merchants. By druggists it is considered the leading article.” (Cited above, p. 459.) Infants that take opiates “shrank up into little old men,” or “wizened like little monkeys.” (Cited above, p. 460.) Here we see how India and China took their revenge on England.

“Happy indeed,” exclaims Mr. Baker, the factory inspector, in his official report, “happy indeed will it be for the manufacturing districts of England, when every married woman having a family is prohibited from working in any textile works at all.”[^53]

F. Engels, in his “Lage der Arbeitenden Klasse Englands” [The Condition of the Working Class in England], and other writers have described so thoroughly the moral degradation caused by the capitalist exploitation of women and children that I need only mention the subject here. But there is also the intellectual desolation that is artificially produced by turning immature human beings into mere machines for making surplus-value. This state of mind is clearly different from the natural ignorance that leaves the mind fallow without destroying its capacity for development, its natural fertility. In the end, this desolation forced even the English Parliament to make elementary education a compulsory condition for the “productive” employment of children under 14 years in every industry subject to the Factory Acts. The spirit of capitalist production stands out clearly in several things: in the ridiculous wording of the so-called education clauses in the Factory Acts; in the lack of any administrative machinery, a lack that again makes the compulsion an illusion; in the manufacturers’ own opposition to these education clauses; and in the tricks and dodges they use to evade them.

<!-- ch15-p049 -->
> “For this the legislature is alone to blame, by having passed a delusive law, which, while it would seem to provide that the children employed in factories shall be *educated,* contains no enactment by which that professed end can be secured. It provides nothing more than that the children shall on certain days of the week, and for a certain number of hours (three) in each day, be inclosed within the four walls of a place called a school, and that the employer of the child shall receive weekly a certificate to that effect signed by a person designated by the subscriber as a schoolmaster or schoolmistress.”[^54]

[^54]: L. Horner in “Reports of Insp. of Fact. for 30th June, 1857,” p. 17.

<!-- ch15-p050 -->
Before the amended Factory Act of 1844 was passed, it was not unusual for the schoolmaster or schoolmistress to sign the certificates of school attendance with a cross, because they themselves could not write.

<!-- ch15-p051 -->
> “On one occasion, on visiting a place called a school, from which certificates of school attendance, had issued, I was so struck with the ignorance of the master, that I said to him: ‘Pray, sir, can you read?’ His reply was: ‘Aye, summat [somewhat]!’ and as a justification of his right to grant certificates, he added: ‘At any rate, I am before [ahead of] my scholars.’”

<!-- ch15-p052 -->
When the Bill of 1844 was being prepared, the inspectors did not fail to point out the disgraceful state of the places called schools, whose certificates they were obliged to accept as compliance with the law. But all they managed to obtain was this: that since the passing of the Act of 1845,

<!-- ch15-p053 -->
> the figures in the school certificate must be filled up in the handwriting of the schoolmaster, who must also sign his Christian and surname in full.”[^55]

[^55]: L. Horner in “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1855,” pp. 18, 19.

<!-- ch15-p054 -->
Sir John Kincaid, factory inspector for Scotland, reports experiences of the same kind.

<!-- ch15-p055 -->
> “The first school we visited was kept by a Mrs. Ann Killin. Upon asking her to spell her name, she straightway made a mistake, by beginning with the letter C, but correcting herself immediately, she said her name began with a K. On looking at her signature, however, in the school certificate books, I noticed that she spelt it in various ways, while her handwriting left no doubt as to her unfitness to teach. She herself also acknowledged that she could not keep the register ... In a second school I found the schoolroom 15 feet long, and 10 feet wide, and counted in this space 75 children, who were gabbling something unintelligible”[^56] But it is not only in the miserable places above referred to that the children obtain certificates of school attendance without having received instruction of any value, for in many schools where there is a competent teacher, his efforts are of little avail from the distracting crowd of children of all ages, from infants of 3 years old and upwards; his livelihood, miserable at the best, depending on the pence received from the greatest number of children whom it is possible to cram into the space. To this is to be added scanty school furniture, deficiency of books, and other materials for teaching, and the depressing effect upon the poor children themselves of a close, noisome atmosphere. I have been in many such schools, where I have seen rows of children doing absolutely nothing; and this is certified as school attendance, and, in statistical returns, such children are set down as being educated.”[^57]

[^56]: Sir John Kincaid in “Rep. of Insp. of Fact. for 31st Oct., 1858,” pp. 31, 32.

[^57]: L. Horner in “Reports, &c., for 31st Oct., 1857,” pp. 17, 18.

<!-- ch15-p058 -->
This looks horribly grotesque in print works, which are regulated by a special Act. Under that Act,

<!-- ch15-p059 -->
> “every child, before being employed in a print work must have attended school for at least 30 days, and not less than 150 hours, during the six months immediately preceding such first day of employment, and during the continuance of its employment in the print works, it must attend for a like period of 30 days, and 150 hours during every successive period of six months.... The attendance at school must be between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m. No attendance of less than 2½ hours, nor more than 5 hours on any one day, shall be reckoned as part of the 150 hours. Under ordinary circumstances the children attend school morning and afternoon for 30 days, for at least 5 hours each day, and upon the expiration of the 30 days, the statutory total of 150 hours having been attained, having, in their language, made up their book, they return to the print work, where they continue until the six months have expired, when another instalment of school attendance becomes due, and they again seek the school until the book is again made up.... Many boys having attended school for the required number of hours, when they return to school after the expiration of their six months’ work in the print work, are in the same condition as when they first attended school as print-work boys, that they have lost all they gained by their previous school attendance.... In other print works the children’s attendance at school is made to depend altogether upon the exigencies [demands] of the work in the establishment. The requisite number of hours is made up each six months, by instalments consisting of from 3 to 5 hours at a time, spreading over, perhaps, the whole six months.... For instance, the attendance on one day might be from 8 to 11 a.m., on another day from 1 p.m. to 4 p.m., and the child might not appear at school again for several days, when it would attend from 3 p.m. to 6 p.m.; then it might attend for 3 or 4 days consecutively, or for a week, then it would not appear in school for 3 weeks or a month, after that upon some odd days at some odd hours when the operative who employed it chose to spare it; and thus the child was, as it were, buffeted from school to work, from work to school, until the tale [count] of 150 hours was told.”[^59]

[^59]: A. Redgrave in “Rep. of Insp. of Fact., 31st. Oct., 1857,” pp. 41-42. In the industries where the Factory Act proper (not the Print Works Act referred to in the text) has been in force for some time, the obstacles in the way of the education clauses have been overcome in recent years. In industries not under the Act, the views of Mr. J. Geddes, a glass manufacturer, are still widely held. He told Mr. White, one of the Inquiry Commissioners: “As far as I can see, the greater amount of education which a part of the working-class has enjoyed for some years past is an evil. It is dangerous, because it makes them independent.” (“Children’s Empl. Comm., Fourth Report,” London, 1865, p. 253.)

By adding women and children in excessive numbers to the ranks of the workers, machinery at last breaks down the resistance that the male workers in the manufacturing period still put up against the despotism of capital.[^60]

[^60]: “Mr. E., a manufacturer ... informed me that he employed females exclusively at his power-looms ... gives a decided preference to married females, especially those who have families at home dependent on them for support; they are attentive, docile, more so than unmarried females, and are compelled to use their utmost exertions to procure the necessaries of life. Thus are the virtues, the peculiar virtues of the female character to be perverted to her injury – thus all that is most dutiful and tender in her nature is made a means of her bondage and suffering.” (Ten Hours’ Factory Bill. The Speech of Lord Ashley, March 15th, London, 1844, p. 20.)
