# Part 4: Production of Relative Surplus-Value

## Chapter 12: The Concept of Relative Surplus-Value

<!-- ch12-p001 -->
Up to this point we have treated the part of the working day that merely produces an equivalent for the value paid by the capitalist for the worker's labor-power as a constant quantity. And that is in fact what it is, under given conditions of production and at a given stage in the economic development of society. (The working day is the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor. Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) Beyond this time, his necessary labor-time, the worker, as we saw, could go on working for 2, 3, 4, 6, etc., hours. The rate of surplus-value and the length of the working day depended on how far this extension went. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it. The rate of surplus-value is the ratio of surplus-value to variable capital, s/v, that is, of surplus labor to necessary labor; it is also called the degree of exploitation of labor-power.) Although the necessary labor-time was constant, we saw, on the other hand, that the total working day was variable. Now suppose we have a working day whose length is given, and whose division between necessary labor and surplus labor is given too. (Surplus labor is the part of the working day beyond necessary labor, in which the worker produces surplus-value for the capitalist.) Let the whole line a c, a–b–c, represent, for example, a working day of 12 hours; the part a b, 10 hours of necessary labor; and the part b c, 2 hours of surplus labor. How, then, can the production of surplus-value be increased, that is, how can the surplus labor be extended, without any extension of a c, or independently of any such extension?

or to one hour's labor-time. If we now move the point b to b' in a c, the working day of 12 hours, then b c becomes b' c. The surplus labor increases by one half, from 2 hours to 3 hours, although the working day stays at 12 hours as before. But this extension of the surplus labor-time from b c to b' c, from 2 hours to 3 hours, is clearly impossible unless the necessary labor-time shrinks at the same time from a b to a b', from 10 hours to 9 hours. The extension of the surplus labor would correspond to a shortening of the necessary labor. In other words, part of the labor-time that was previously in reality spent for the worker's own benefit would be turned into labor-time for the benefit of the capitalist. What would change is not the length of the working day, but its division into necessary labor-time and surplus labor-time.

<!-- ch12-p005 -->
On the other hand, clearly the length of the surplus labor is given when the length of the working day and the value of labor-power are given. The value of labor-power, that is, the labor-time needed to produce labor-power, determines the labor-time needed to reproduce that value. If one working-hour is embodied in sixpence, and the value of a day's labor-power is five shillings, the worker must work 10 hours a day to replace the value that capital has paid for his labor-power, or to produce an equivalent for the value of the means of subsistence he needs each day. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. Means of subsistence are the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live.) Given the value of these means of subsistence, the value of his labor-power is given;[^1] and given the value of his labor-power, the length of his necessary labor-time is given. The length of the surplus labor, however, is found by subtracting the necessary labor-time from the total working day. Ten hours subtracted from twelve leave two, and it is not easy to see how, under the given conditions, the surplus labor can possibly be extended beyond two hours. No doubt the capitalist can pay the worker four shillings and sixpence, or even less, instead of five shillings. Nine hours' labor-time would be enough to reproduce this value of four shillings and sixpence. So three hours of surplus labor instead of two would go to the capitalist, and the surplus-value would rise from one shilling to eighteen-pence. But this result would be obtained only by pushing the worker's wages below the value of his labor-power. (Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) With the four shillings and sixpence that he produces in nine hours, he can get one-tenth less of the necessaries of life than before, and so the proper reproduction of his labor-power is crippled. In this case the surplus labor would be extended only by overstepping its normal limits; its territory would be enlarged only by seizing part of the territory of necessary labor-time. This method plays an important part in actual practice. Despite that, we cannot consider it here, because we have assumed that all commodities, including labor-power, are bought and sold at their full value. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, that is, sold, not for the producer's own use.) Granted this, it follows that the labor-time needed to produce labor-power, or to reproduce its value, cannot be reduced by a fall in the worker's wages below the value of his labor-power, but only by a fall in this value itself. Given the length of the working day, the extension of the surplus labor must necessarily come from cutting the necessary labor-time; the cut cannot come from the extension. In the example we have taken, the value of labor-power must actually fall by one-tenth so that the necessary labor-time can be reduced by one-tenth, that is, from ten hours to nine, and so that the surplus labor can consequently be extended from two hours to three.

(J. Vanderlint, cited above, p. 15.) “Le simple ouvrier, qui n’a que ses bras et son industrie, n’a rien qu’autant qu’il parvient à vendre à d’autres sa peine... In every kind of work it must happen, and it does in fact happen, that the worker's wage is limited to what he needs to obtain his subsistence.] (Turgot, “Réflexions, &c.,” *Oeuvres*, éd. Daire t. I, p. 10.) “The price of the necessaries of life is, in fact, the cost of producing labour.” (Malthus, “Inquiry into, &c., Rent,” London, 1815, p. 48, note.)

Such a fall in the value of labor-power implies, however, that the same necessaries of life that used to be produced in ten hours can now be produced in nine hours. But this is impossible without an increase in the productivity of labor. For example, suppose a shoemaker with given tools makes one pair of boots in one working day of twelve hours. If he has to make two pairs in the same time, the productivity of his labor must be doubled. This cannot be done except by a change in his tools, or in his way of working, or in both. So the conditions of production, that is, his mode of production, and the labor process itself, must be revolutionized. (The labor process is the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things.) By an increase in the productivity of labor, we mean, in general, a change in the labor process of such a kind that it shortens the labor-time socially necessary to produce a commodity (the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor), and gives a given quantity of labor the power to produce a greater quantity of use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties).[^2] Until now, in dealing with surplus-value that arises from simply extending the working day, we have assumed the mode of production to be given and unchanging. But when surplus-value has to be produced by turning necessary labor into surplus labor, it is by no means enough for capital to take over the labor process in the form in which history has handed it down, and then simply to make that process last longer. The technical and social conditions of the process, and so the very mode of production, must be revolutionized before the productivity of labor can be increased. Only by that means can the value of labor-power be made to sink, and the part of the working day needed to reproduce that value be shortened.

[^2]: Quando si perfezionano le arti, che non è altro che la scoperta di nuove vie, onde si possa compiere una manufattura con meno gente o (che è lo stesso) in minor tempo di prima.” [When the crafts are perfected, which is nothing other than the discovery of new ways by which a product can be made with fewer people or (which is the same thing) in less time than before] (Galiani, cited above, p. 159.) “L’économie sur les frais de production ne peut donc être autre chose que l’économie sur la quantité de travail employé pour produire.” [So saving on the costs of production can be nothing other than saving on the quantity of labor employed to produce] (Sismondi, “Études,” t. I. p. 22.)

<!-- ch12-p007 -->
I call the surplus-value produced by lengthening the working day *absolute surplus-value*. On the other hand, I call the surplus-value that arises from cutting the necessary labor-time, and from the corresponding change in the relative lengths of the two parts of the working day, *relative surplus-value*.

<!-- ch12-p008 -->
To bring about a fall in the value of labor-power, the increase in the productivity of labor must take hold of those branches of industry whose products determine the value of labor-power, and so either belong to the class of customary means of subsistence, or can take the place of those means. But the value of a commodity is determined not only by the quantity of labor that the worker directly spends on that commodity, but also by the labor contained in the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things). For instance, the value of a pair of boots depends not only on the cobbler's labor, but also on the value of the leather, wax, thread, etc. So a fall in the value of labor-power is also brought about by an increase in the productivity of labor, and by a corresponding cheapening of commodities, in those industries that supply the instruments of labor and the raw material which form the material elements of the constant capital needed to produce the necessaries of life. (Constant capital is the part of capital spent on means of production; its value is passed on to the product but does not grow.) But an increase in the productivity of labor in those branches of industry that supply neither the necessaries of life nor the means of production for such necessaries leaves the value of labor-power undisturbed.

<!-- ch12-p009 -->
Of course, the cheapened commodity causes only a fall in the value of labor-power to that extent, that is, a fall proportional to how far that commodity is used in reproducing labor-power. Shirts, for instance, are a necessary means of subsistence, but they are only one out of many. The whole of the necessaries of life, however, consists of various commodities, each the product of a separate industry, and the value of each of those commodities enters as a component part into the value of labor-power. The value of labor-power decreases as the labor-time needed to reproduce it decreases. The total decrease is the sum of all the different cuts in labor-time made in those various separate industries. Here we treat this general result as if it were the immediate result directly aimed at in each individual case. When an individual capitalist cheapens shirts, for instance, by increasing the productivity of labor, he by no means necessarily aims at reducing the value of labor-power and shortening the necessary labor-time to that extent. But only insofar as he ultimately contributes to this result does he help to raise the general rate of surplus-value.[^3] The general and necessary tendencies of capital must be distinguished from their forms of appearance.

[^3]: “Let us suppose ... the products ... of the manufacturer are doubled by improvement in machinery ... he will be able to clothe his workmen by means of a smaller proportion of the entire return ... and thus his profit will be raised. But in no other way will it be influenced.” (Ramsay, cited above, pp. 168, 169.)

<!-- ch12-p010 -->
We do not intend to consider here how the built-in laws of capitalist production show themselves in the movements of individual masses of capital. There they assert themselves as coercive laws of competition, and they are brought home to the mind and consciousness of the individual capitalist as the motives that direct his operations. But this much is clear: a scientific analysis of competition is not possible until we have a grasp of the inner nature of capital. In the same way, the apparent motions of the heavenly bodies can be understood only by someone who knows their real motions, which the senses cannot directly perceive. Nevertheless, to help understand the production of relative surplus-value better, we may add the following remarks, in which we assume nothing more than the results we have already reached.

<!-- ch12-p011 -->
If one hour's labor is embodied in sixpence, a value of six shillings will be produced in a working day of 12 hours. Suppose that, with the prevailing productivity of labor, 12 articles are produced in these 12 hours. Let the value of the means of production used up in each article be sixpence. Under these circumstances each article costs one shilling: sixpence for the value of the means of production, and sixpence for the value newly added in working with those means. Now let some one capitalist manage to double the productivity of labor, and to produce 24 such articles instead of 12 in the working day of 12 hours. If the value of the means of production stays the same, the value of each article will fall to ninepence: sixpence for the value of the means of production and threepence for the value newly added by the labor. Despite the doubled productivity of labor, the day's labor creates, as before, a new value of six shillings and no more, but this value is now spread over twice as many articles. Each article now has 1/24th of this value embodied in it instead of 1/12th, threepence instead of sixpence. Or, what comes to the same thing, only half an hour's labor-time, instead of a whole hour's, is now added to the means of production while they are being turned into each article. The individual value of these articles is now below their social value. In other words, they have cost less labor-time than the great bulk of the same article produced under the average social conditions. On average, each article costs one shilling and represents 2 hours of social labor; but under the changed mode of production it costs only ninepence, or contains only 1½ hours' labor. The real value of a commodity, however, is not its individual value but its social value. That is to say, its real value is measured not by the labor-time the article costs the producer in each individual case, but by the labor-time socially required to produce it. So if the capitalist who applies the new method sells his commodity at its social value of one shilling, he sells it for threepence above its individual value, and so realizes an extra surplus-value of threepence. On the other hand, for him the working day of 12 hours is now represented by 24 articles instead of 12. So, to get rid of the product of one working day, the demand must be double what it was, that is, the market must become twice as large. Other things being equal, his commodities can command a larger market only if their prices are lowered. (Price is the money name of the value, that is, of the labor, embodied in a commodity.) He will therefore sell them above their individual value but below their social value, say at tenpence each. In this way he still squeezes an extra surplus-value of one penny out of each. He pockets this increase in surplus-value whether or not his commodities belong to the class of necessary means of subsistence that help to determine the general value of labor-power. So, independently of this last circumstance, each individual capitalist has a motive to cheapen his commodities by increasing the productivity of labor.

<!-- ch12-p012 -->
Nevertheless, even in this case, the increased production of surplus-value arises from cutting the necessary labor-time and from the corresponding extension of the surplus labor.[^4] Let the necessary labor-time amount to 10 hours, the value of a day's labor-power to five shillings, the surplus labor-time to 2 hours, and the daily surplus-value to one shilling. But the capitalist now produces 24 articles, which he sells at tenpence apiece, making twenty shillings in all. Since the value of the means of production is twelve shillings, 14 2/5 of these articles merely replace the constant capital advanced. The labor of the 12 hours' working day is represented by the remaining 9 3/5 articles. Since the price of the labor-power is five shillings, 6 articles represent the necessary labor-time, and 3 3/5 articles the surplus labor. The ratio of the necessary labor to the surplus labor, which under average social conditions was 5:1, is now only 5:3. We can reach the same result in the following way. The value of the product of the working day of 12 hours is twenty shillings. Of this sum, twelve shillings belong to the value of the means of production, a value that merely reappears. That leaves eight shillings, which are the expression in money of the value newly created during the working day. This sum is greater than the sum that expresses average social labor of the same kind: twelve hours of that labor are expressed by only six shillings. The exceptionally productive labor acts as intensified labor; in equal periods of time it creates greater values than average social labor of the same kind. (See Ch. I. Sect 2. p. 44.) But our capitalist still goes on paying only five shillings, as before, as the value of a day's labor-power. So the worker now needs to work only 7½ hours instead of 10 to reproduce this value. His surplus labor is therefore increased by 2½ hours, and the surplus-value he produces grows from one shilling into three. So the capitalist who applies the improved method of production takes a greater part of the working day for surplus labor than the other capitalists in the same trade do. He does individually what the whole body of capitalists engaged in producing relative surplus-value do collectively. On the other hand, however, this extra surplus-value vanishes as soon as the new method of production has become general, and has thus made the difference between the individual value of the cheapened commodity and its social value vanish. The law that value is determined by labor-time brings the individual capitalist who applies the new method of production under its rule, by compelling him to sell his goods below their social value. This same law, acting as a coercive law of competition, forces his competitors to adopt the new method.[^5] So the general rate of surplus-value is ultimately affected by the whole process only when the increase in the productivity of labor has taken hold of the branches of production that are connected with the necessary means of subsistence, and has cheapened the commodities that form part of them and are therefore elements of the value of labor-power.

[^4]: “A man’s profit does not depend upon his command of the produce of other men’s labour, but upon his command of labour itself. If he can sell his goods at a higher price, while his workmen’s wages remain unaltered, he is clearly benefited.... A smaller proportion of what he produces is sufficient to put that labour into motion, and a larger proportion consequently remains for himself.” (“Outlines of Pol. Econ.” London, 1832, pp. 49, 50.)

The value of commodities is in inverse ratio to the productivity of labor. So too is the value of labor-power, because it depends on the values of commodities. Relative surplus-value, on the contrary, is directly proportional to that productivity. It rises when productivity rises and falls when productivity falls. Assuming the value of money to be constant, an average social working day of 12 hours always produces the same new value, six shillings, however this sum may be divided between surplus-value and wages. But suppose that increased productivity makes the value of the necessaries of life fall, and so reduces the value of a day's labor-power from five shillings to three. Then the surplus-value increases from one shilling to three. Ten hours were needed to reproduce the value of the labor-power; now only six are needed. Four hours have been set free, and can be annexed to the territory of surplus labor. So there is a built-in inclination and constant tendency in capital to raise the productivity of labor, in order to cheapen commodities, and by cheapening commodities to cheapen the worker himself.[^6]

[^6]: “In whatever proportion the expenses of a labourer are diminished, in the same proportion will his wages be diminished, if the restraints upon industry are at the same time taken off.” (“Considerations Concerning Taking off the Bounty on Corn Exported,” etc., London, 1753, p. 7.) “The interest of trade requires, that corn and all provisions should be as cheap as possible; for whatever makes them dear, must make labour dear also ... in all countries, where industry is not restrained, the price of provisions must affect the price of labour. This will always be diminished when the necessaries of life grow cheaper.” (cited above, p. 3.) “Wages are decreased in the same proportion as the powers of production increase. Machinery, it is true, cheapens the necessaries of life, but it also cheapens the labourer.” (“A Prize Essay on the Comparative Merits of Competition and Co-operation.” London, 1834, p. 27.)

The value of a commodity is, in itself, of no interest to the capitalist. What alone interests him is the surplus-value that lives in it and can be realized by sale. Realizing the surplus-value necessarily brings with it the refunding of the value that was advanced. Now, relative surplus-value increases in direct proportion to the development of the productivity of labor, while, on the other hand, the value of commodities falls in the same proportion. One and the same process cheapens commodities and increases the surplus-value contained in them. So here we have the solution of the riddle: why does the capitalist, whose sole concern is the production of exchange-value, constantly try to push down the exchange-value of commodities? (Exchange-value is the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for.) With this riddle Quesnay, one of the founders of political economy (the science of economics as it was then called), tormented his opponents, and they could give him no answer.

<!-- ch12-p015 -->
“You acknowledge,” he says, “that the more expenses and the cost of labour can, in the manufacture of industrial products, be reduced without injury to production, the more advantageous is such reduction, because it diminishes the price of the finished article. And yet, you believe that the production of wealth, which arises from the labour of the workpeople, consists in the augmentation of the exchange-value of their products.”[^7]

[^7]: “Ils conviennent que plus on peut, sans préjudice, épargner de frais ou de travaux dispendieux dans la fabrication des ouvrages des artisans, plus cette épargne est profitable par la diminution des prix de ces ouvrages. Cependant ils croient que la production de richesse qui résulte des travaux des artisans consiste dans l’augmentation de la valeur vénale de leurs ouvrages.” (Quesnay: “Dialogues sur le Commerce et les Travaux des Artisans.” pp. 188, 189.)

<!-- ch12-p016 -->
So the shortening of the working day is by no means the aim of capitalist production when labor is economized by increasing its productivity.[^8] The only aim is to shorten the labor-time needed to produce a definite quantity of commodities. When the productivity of his labor has been increased, the worker produces, say, 10 times as many commodities as before, and so spends one-tenth as much labor-time on each. This fact by no means prevents him from going on working 12 hours as before, or from producing 1,200 articles in those 12 hours instead of 120. Indeed, more than that, his working day may be lengthened at the same time, so that he produces, say, 1,400 articles in 14 hours. So in the treatises of economists of the type of MacCulloch, Ure, Senior, and *tutti quanti* [all the rest of them], we may read on one page that the worker owes capital a debt of gratitude for developing his productivity, because this shortens the necessary labor-time, and on the next page that he must prove his gratitude by working 15 hours in future instead of 10. Within the limits of capitalist production, the object of all development of the productivity of labor is to shorten the part of the working day in which the worker must labor for his own benefit, and, by that very shortening, to lengthen the other part of the day, in which he is at liberty to work for the capitalist free of charge. How far this result can also be reached without cheapening commodities will appear when we examine the particular methods of producing relative surplus-value. We now go on to that examination.
