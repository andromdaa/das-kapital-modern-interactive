#### B. The currency of money[^26]

<!-- ch03-p047 -->
The change of form C–M–C brings about the circulation of the material products of labor. It requires that a given value, in the shape of a commodity, begin the process, and that it also end the process in the shape of a commodity. The movement of the commodity is therefore a circuit. On the other hand, the form of this movement rules out any circuit made by the money. The result is not that the money returns, but that it moves further and further away from its starting-point. As long as the seller holds on tight to his money, which is the changed shape of his commodity, that commodity is still in the first phase of its metamorphosis and has completed only half its course. But as soon as he completes the process, as soon as he follows up his sale with a purchase, the money leaves its owner’s hands again. It is true that if the weaver, after buying the Bible, sells more linen, money comes back into his hands. But this return is not due to the circulation of the first 20 yards of linen. That circulation ended with the money in the hands of the seller of the Bible. Money returns to the weaver’s hands only because the process of circulation is renewed or repeated with a fresh commodity, and this renewed process ends with the same result as the one before it. So the movement that the circulation of commodities directly gives to money takes the form of a constant motion away from its starting-point, a course from the hands of one commodity-owner into those of another. This course makes up its currency (cours de la monnaie [course of money]).

[^26]: *Translator’s note*. — Here this word is used in its original meaning: the course or track that money follows as it passes from hand to hand. This course is essentially different from circulation.

<!-- ch03-p048 -->
The currency of money is the constant and monotonous repetition of the same process. The commodity is always in the hands of the seller; the money, as a means of purchase, is always in the hands of the buyer. And money serves as a means of purchase by realizing the price of the commodity. This realization moves the commodity from the seller to the buyer, and moves the money from the buyer’s hands into the seller’s, where it goes through the same process again with another commodity. This one-sided character of the money’s motion arises out of the two-sided character of the commodity’s motion, but that fact is veiled over. The very nature of the circulation of commodities produces the opposite appearance. The first metamorphosis of a commodity is visibly a movement not only of the money but also of the commodity itself. In the second metamorphosis, on the contrary, the movement appears to us as the movement of the money alone. In the first phase of its circulation the commodity changes places with the money. Then the commodity, as a useful object, falls out of circulation into consumption.[^27] In its place we have its value-shape, the money. The commodity then goes through the second phase of its circulation, not in its own natural shape, but in the shape of money. So the continuity of the movement is kept up by the money alone. The same movement that, for the commodity, consists of two opposite processes is, when seen as the movement of the money, always one and the same process: a continual change of places with ever fresh commodities. So the result that the circulation of commodities brings about, namely the replacing of one commodity by another, appears to have been achieved not through the change of form of the commodities, but rather by the money acting as a medium of circulation (that is, in its role of passing from hand to hand to carry out sales and purchases). It looks as though an action of the money circulates the commodities, which to all appearance are motionless in themselves, and moves them from hands in which they are non-use-values to hands in which they are use-values, always in the direction opposite to the money’s. The money is continually withdrawing commodities from circulation and stepping into their places, and in this way it continually moves further and further from its starting-point. So although the movement of the money is merely the expression of the circulation of commodities, the opposite appears to be the actual fact, and the circulation of commodities seems to be the result of the movement of the money.[^28]

[^27]: Even when the commodity is sold over and over again (something that, for the present, does not exist for us), when it is finally sold for the last time it falls out of the sphere of circulation into that of consumption. There it serves either as means of subsistence or as means of production.

[^28]: “Il (l’argent) n’a d’autre mouvement que celui qui lui est imprimé par les productions.” [“It” (money) “has no other motion than the one given to it by the products”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 885.)

Again, money functions as a medium of circulation only because in money the values of commodities have an independent reality. So its movement as the medium of circulation is in fact merely the movement of commodities while they change their forms. This fact must therefore show itself plainly in the currency of money. Thus the linen, for instance, first changes its commodity-form into its money-form. The second term of its first metamorphosis, C–M, the money form, then becomes the first term of its final metamorphosis, M–C, its change back into the Bible. But each of these two changes of form is carried out by an exchange between commodity and money, by their *changing places with each other*. The same coins come into the seller’s hand *as the form of the commodity that has been parted with*, and leave it as *the form of the commodity that can be parted with absolutely*. They change places twice. The first metamorphosis of the linen puts these coins into the weaver’s pocket; the second draws them out of it. The two opposite changes that the same commodity goes through are reflected in the displacement of the same coins, repeated twice, but in opposite directions.

<!-- ch03-p050 -->
If, on the contrary, only one phase of the metamorphosis is gone through, if there are only sales or only purchases, then a given piece of money changes its place only once. Its second change of place always expresses the second metamorphosis of the commodity, its change back from money. The frequent repetition of the displacement of the same coins reflects not only the series of metamorphoses that a single commodity has gone through, but also the intertwining of the countless metamorphoses in the world of commodities in general. It goes without saying that all this applies only to the simple circulation of commodities, the only form we are now considering.

<!-- ch03-p051 -->
Every commodity, when it first steps into circulation and goes through its first change of form, does so only to fall out of circulation again and be replaced by other commodities. Money, on the contrary, as the medium of circulation, stays continually within the sphere of circulation and moves about in it. So the question arises: how much money does this sphere constantly absorb?

<!-- ch03-p052 -->
In a given country, numerous one-sided metamorphoses of commodities take place every day at the same time, but in different places; in other words, numerous sales and numerous purchases. The commodities are equated beforehand in imagination, by their prices, with definite quantities of money. And in the form of circulation we are now considering, money and commodities always come bodily face to face, one at the positive pole of purchase, the other at the negative pole of sale. Since this is so, the amount of the medium of circulation required is clearly fixed beforehand by the sum of the prices of all these commodities. In fact, the money in reality represents the quantity or sum of gold that was expressed ideally beforehand by the sum of the prices of the commodities. The equality of these two sums is therefore self-evident. We know, however, that if the values of commodities stay constant, their prices vary with the value of gold (the material of money): they rise as it falls, and fall as it rises. Now if, because of such a rise or fall in the value of gold, the sum of the prices of commodities falls or rises, the quantity of money in currency must fall or rise to the same extent. In this case, it is true, the change in the quantity of the medium of circulation is caused by the money itself. Yet it is caused not by money’s function as a medium of circulation, but by its function as a measure of value. First the price of the commodities varies inversely with the value of the money, and then the quantity of the medium of circulation varies directly with the price of the commodities. Exactly the same thing would happen if, for instance, instead of the value of gold falling, gold were replaced by silver as the measure of value; or if, instead of the value of silver rising, gold were to push silver out of its place as the measure of value. In the one case, more silver would be current than gold was before; in the other case, less gold would be current than silver was before. In each case the value of the material of money, that is, the value of the commodity that serves as the measure of value, would have changed. So, too, would the prices of commodities, which express their values in money, and so, too, would the quantity of money current, whose function is to realize those prices. We have already seen that the sphere of circulation has an opening through which gold (or the material of money generally) enters it as a commodity with a given value. So when money takes up its functions as a measure of value, when it expresses prices, its value is already fixed. If its value now falls, this first shows itself in a change in the prices of those commodities that are directly bartered for the precious metals at the places where those metals are produced. Most other commodities, especially in the less developed stages of civil society, will go on for a long time being valued by the former, outdated and illusory value of the measure of value. Nevertheless, one commodity infects another through their common value-relation. So their prices, expressed in gold or in silver, gradually settle into the proportions fixed by their comparative values, until finally the values of all commodities are estimated in terms of the new value of the metal that makes up money. This process goes along with a continued increase in the quantity of the precious metals, an increase caused by their streaming in to replace the articles directly bartered for them at their sources of production. So in the same proportion as commodities in general acquire their true prices, as their values come to be estimated according to the fallen value of the precious metal, the quantity of that metal needed to realize those new prices is provided beforehand. Some economists in the 17th, and particularly in the 18th century, observed the results that followed the discovery of fresh supplies of gold and silver from one side only. This one-sided observation led them to the false conclusion that the prices of commodities had gone up because of the increased quantity of gold and silver serving as the medium of circulation. From now on we shall take the value of gold as given, as in fact it is at that moment whenever we estimate the price of a commodity.

<!-- ch03-p053 -->
On this assumption, then, the quantity of the medium of circulation is fixed by the sum of the prices to be realized. If we further assume that the price of each commodity is given, the sum of the prices clearly depends on the mass of commodities in circulation. It takes very little racking of brains to understand that if one quarter of wheat costs £2,100 quarters will cost £200, 200 quarters £400, and so on. Consequently, the quantity of money that changes places with the wheat when it is sold must increase with the quantity of that wheat.

<!-- ch03-p054 -->
If the mass of commodities stays constant, the quantity of circulating money varies with the ups and downs in the prices of those commodities. It increases and decreases because the sum of the prices increases or decreases as a result of the change of price. To produce this effect, it is by no means necessary that the prices of all commodities rise or fall at the same time. A rise or a fall in the prices of a number of leading articles is enough, in the one case to increase, in the other to reduce, the sum of the prices of all commodities, and therefore to put more or less money into circulation. The effect on the quantity of the medium of circulation stays the same whether the change in price corresponds to an actual change of value in the commodities, or is the result of mere fluctuations in market-prices. Suppose the following articles are sold, or partly metamorphosed, at the same time in different places: say, one quarter of wheat, 20 yards of linen, one Bible, and 4 gallons of brandy. If the price of each article is £2, and the sum of the prices to be realized is therefore £8, it follows that £8 in money must go into circulation. If, on the other hand, these same articles are links in the following chain of metamorphoses: 1 quarter of wheat – £2 – 20 yards of linen – £2 – 1 Bible – £2 – 4 gallons of brandy – £2, a chain we already know well, then the £2 make the different commodities circulate one after the other. After realizing their prices one after another, and therefore the sum of those prices, £8, they come to rest at last in the pocket of the distiller. So the £2 make four moves. This repeated change of place of the same pieces of money corresponds to the double change in form of the commodities, to their motion in opposite directions through two stages of circulation, and to the interlacing of the metamorphoses of different commodities.[^29] The process of metamorphosis consists of these opposite and complementary phases, and they are gone through not at the same time but one after another. So time is needed to complete the series. Hence the velocity of the currency of money is measured by the number of moves made by a given piece of money in a given time. Suppose the circulation of the 4 articles takes a day. The sum of the prices to be realized in the day is £8, the number of moves of the two pieces of money is four, and the quantity of money circulating is £2. So for a given period of time during the process of circulation, we have the following relation: the quantity of money functioning as the medium of circulation is equal to the sum of the prices of the commodities divided by the number of moves made by coins of the same denomination. This law holds generally.

[^29]: “Ce sont les productions qui le (l’argent) mettent en mouvement et le font circuler ... La célérité de son mouvement (c. de l’argent) supplée à sa quantité. Lorsqu’il en est besoin il ne fait que glisser d’une main dans l’autre sans s’arrêter un instant.” [“It is products which set it” (money) “in motion and make it circulate ... The speed of its” (money’s) “motion makes up for its quantity. When necessary, it does nothing but slide from one hand into the other, without stopping for a moment”] (Le Trosne, cited above, pp. 915, 916.)

<!-- ch03-p055 -->
The total circulation of commodities in a given country during a given period is made up of two parts. On the one hand, there are numerous isolated and simultaneous partial metamorphoses, sales which are at the same time purchases, in which each coin changes its place only once, or makes only one move. On the other hand, there are numerous distinct series of metamorphoses, partly running side by side and partly merging with each other; in each of these series each coin makes a number of moves, more or fewer according to circumstances. Given the total number of moves made by all the circulating coins of one denomination, we can work out the average number of moves made by a single coin of that denomination, or the average velocity of the currency of money. The quantity of money thrown into circulation at the beginning of each day is of course fixed by the sum of the prices of all the commodities circulating at the same time, side by side. But once in circulation, coins are, so to speak, made responsible for one another. If one speeds up, the other either slows down or drops out of circulation altogether. For circulation can absorb only such a quantity of gold as, multiplied by the average number of moves made by one single coin or element, equals the sum of the prices to be realized. So if the number of moves made by the separate pieces increases, the total number of those pieces in circulation decreases. If the number of moves decreases, the total number of pieces increases. Since the quantity of money that circulation can absorb is given for a given average velocity of currency, all you need to do to withdraw a given number of sovereigns from circulation is to throw the same number of one-pound notes into it, a trick well known to all bankers.

<!-- ch03-p056 -->
Just as the currency of money, considered generally, is only a reflection of the circulation of commodities, or of the opposite metamorphoses they go through, so, too, the velocity of that currency reflects the speed with which commodities change their forms. It reflects the continual interlacing of one series of metamorphoses with another, the hurried social interchange of matter, the rapid disappearance of commodities from the sphere of circulation, and the equally rapid substitution of fresh ones in their places. So in the velocity of the currency we have the flowing unity of the opposite and complementary phases: the unity of the conversion of the useful aspect of commodities into their value-aspect and their conversion back from the value-aspect to the useful aspect, or the unity of the two processes of sale and purchase. On the other hand, the slowing down of the currency reflects the separation of these two processes into isolated, opposed phases. It reflects the stagnation in the change of form, and therefore in the social interchange of matter. The circulation itself, of course, gives no clue to the origin of this stagnation; it merely displays the phenomenon itself. At the same time as the currency slows down, the general public sees money appear and disappear less often at the edges of circulation. So it naturally blames this slowing down on a shortage in the quantity of the medium of circulation.[^30]

[^30]: “Money being ... the common measure of buying and selling, everybody who hath anything to sell, and cannot procure chapmen [buyers] for it, is presently [at once] apt to think, that want [lack] of money in the kingdom, or country, is the cause why his goods do not go off; and so, want of money is the common cry; which is a great mistake... What do these people want, who cry out for money? ... The farmer complains ... he thinks that were more money in the country; he should have a price for his goods. Then it seems money is not his want, but a price for his corn and cattel, which he would sell, but cannot... Why cannot he get a price? ... (1) Either there is too much corn and cattel in the country, so that most who come to market have need of selling, as he hath, and few of buying; or (2) There wants the usual vent [outlet] abroad by transportation [export]..., or (3) The consumption fails, as when men, by reason of poverty, do not spend so much in their houses as formerly they did; wherefore it is not the increase of specific money, which would at all advance the farmer’s goods, but the removal of any of these three causes, which do truly keep down the market... The merchant and shopkeeper want money in the same manner, that is, they want a vent for the goods they deal in, by reason that the markets fail” ... [A nation] “never thrives better, than when riches are tost from hand to hand.” (Sir Dudley North: “Discourses upon Trade,” London 1691, pp. 11-15, passim.) Herrenschwand’s fanciful notions come down merely to this: that the antagonism which has its origin in the nature of commodities, and is reproduced in their circulation, can be removed by increasing the medium of circulation. But if, on the one hand, it is a popular delusion to blame stagnation in production and circulation on too little medium of circulation, it by no means follows, on the other hand, that an actual scarcity of the medium, caused, for example, by bungling legislative interference with the regulation of currency, may not give rise to such stagnation.

<!-- ch03-p057 -->
The total quantity of money functioning as the medium of circulation during a given period is fixed, on the one hand, by the sum of the prices of the circulating commodities, and on the other hand, by how quickly the opposite phases of the metamorphoses follow one another. On this speed depends what proportion of the sum of the prices each single coin can, on average, realize. But the sum of the prices of the circulating commodities depends on the quantity of the commodities as well as on their prices. These three factors, however (the state of prices, the quantity of circulating commodities, and the velocity of the currency of money), are all variable. So the sum of the prices to be realized, and therefore the quantity of the medium of circulation that depends on that sum, will vary with the numerous variations of these three factors in combination. Of these variations we shall consider only those that have been the most important in the history of prices.

<!-- ch03-p058 -->
While prices stay constant, the quantity of the medium of circulation may increase because the number of circulating commodities increases, or because the velocity of currency decreases, or because of a combination of the two. On the other hand, the quantity of the medium of circulation may decrease with a decreasing number of commodities, or with an increasing speed of their circulation.

<!-- ch03-p059 -->
With a general rise in the prices of commodities, the quantity of the medium of circulation will stay constant, provided the number of commodities in circulation decreases in proportion to the increase in their prices; or provided the velocity of currency increases at the same rate as prices rise, while the number of commodities in circulation stays constant. The quantity of the medium of circulation may decrease, because the number of commodities decreases faster than prices rise, or because the velocity of currency increases faster than prices rise.

<!-- ch03-p060 -->
With a general fall in the prices of commodities, the quantity of the medium of circulation will stay constant, provided the number of commodities increases in proportion to their fall in price, or provided the velocity of currency decreases in the same proportion. The quantity of the medium of circulation will increase, provided the number of commodities increases faster, or the speed of circulation decreases faster, than prices fall.

<!-- ch03-p061 -->
The variations of the different factors may balance each other out, so that despite their continued instability, the sum of the prices to be realized and the quantity of money in circulation stay constant. Consequently we find, especially if we take long periods into account, that the deviations of the quantity of money current in any country from its average level are much smaller than we would at first sight expect. This of course leaves aside the excessive disturbances that arise periodically from industrial and commercial crises or, less often, from fluctuations in the value of money.

<!-- ch03-p062 -->
The law that the quantity of the medium of circulation is fixed by the sum of the prices of the commodities circulating and the average velocity of currency[^31] may also be stated as follows: given the sum of the values of commodities, and the average speed of their metamorphoses, the quantity of precious metal current as money depends on the value of that precious metal. There is a mistaken opinion that, on the contrary, it is prices that are fixed by the quantity of the medium of circulation, and that this quantity depends on the quantity of the precious metals in a country.[^32] Those who first held this opinion based it on the absurd hypothesis that commodities have no price, and money no value, when they first enter into circulation, and that, once they are in circulation, a fractional part of the jumble of commodities is exchanged for a fractional part of the heap of precious metals.[^33]

Now, as the proportion of the number of farthings requisite in commerce is to be taken from the number of people, the frequency of their exchanges: as also, and principally, from the value of the smallest silver pieces of money; so in like manner, the proportion of money [gold and silver specie] requisite in our trade, is to be likewise taken from the frequency of commutations [exchanges], and from the bigness of the payments.” (William Petty, “A Treatise of Taxes and Contributions.” London 1667, p. 17.)
