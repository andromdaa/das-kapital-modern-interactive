## Chapter 27: Expropriation of the Agricultural Population From the Land

<!-- ch27-p001 -->
In England, serfdom had practically disappeared in the last part of the 14th century. At that time, and to a still larger extent in the 15th century, the immense majority of the population[^1] consisted of free peasant proprietors, whatever the feudal title under which their right of property was hidden. On the larger lords’ estates, the old bailiff, himself a serf, was displaced by the free farmer. The wage workers of agriculture were partly peasants, who used their leisure time to work on the large estates, and partly an independent special class of wage workers, few in number both relatively and absolutely. In practice these last were also peasant farmers at the same time, since besides their wages they were allotted 4 or more acres of arable land, together with their cottages. Besides, they and the rest of the peasants had the right to use the common land, which gave pasture to their cattle and supplied them with timber, firewood, turf, etc.[^2] In all countries of Europe, feudal production is characterized by the division of the soil among the greatest possible number of sub-vassals. The power of the feudal lord, like that of the sovereign, depended not on the length of his rent roll but on the number of his subjects, and the number of his subjects depended on the number of peasant proprietors.[^3] So although after the Norman Conquest the land of England was divided into gigantic baronies, one of which often included some 900 of the old Anglo-Saxon lordships, it was strewn with small peasant properties, with great lords’ estates scattered among them only here and there. Such conditions, together with the prosperity of the towns so characteristic of the 15th century, made possible that wealth of the people which Chancellor Fortescue paints so eloquently in his “Laudes legum Angliae” [In Praise of the Laws of England]; but they ruled out the possibility of capitalist wealth.

The average income of these small landlords... was estimated at between £60 and £70 a year. It was computed that the number of persons who tilled their own land was greater than the number of those who farmed the land of others.” Macaulay: “History of England,” 10th ed., 1854, I. pp. 333, 334. (Bourgeois means belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it.) It is very convenient to be “liberal” at the expense of the middle ages.

The prelude to the revolution that laid the foundation of the capitalist mode of production was played in the last third of the 15th century and the first decade of the 16th. A mass of free proletarians was hurled onto the labor market by the breaking-up of the bands of feudal retainers who, as Sir James Steuart well says, “everywhere uselessly filled house and castle.” (Proletarians are members of the proletariat: the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power.) The royal power, itself a product of bourgeois development, forcibly hastened the breaking-up of these bands of retainers in its striving for absolute sovereignty. Yet although it did so, it was by no means the only cause. In insolent conflict with king and parliament, the great feudal lords created an incomparably larger proletariat. They did it by forcibly driving the peasantry from the land, to which the peasants had the same feudal right as the lord himself, and by seizing the common lands. The direct impulse for these evictions came from the rapid rise of the Flemish wool manufacture and the corresponding rise in the price of wool in England. The old nobility had been devoured by the great feudal wars. The new nobility was the child of its time, a time for which money was the power of all powers. Its cry, therefore, was: turn arable land into sheep pastures! Harrison, in his “Description of England, prefixed to Holinshed’s Chronicles,” describes how the expropriation of small peasants is ruining the country. (Expropriation means taking away people’s property, here their land and means of production.) “What care our great encroachers?” The dwellings of the peasants and the cottages of the workers were razed to the ground or doomed to decay. “If,” says Harrison, “the old records of euerie manour be sought... it will soon appear that in some manour seventeene, eighteene, or twentie houses are shrunk... that England was neuer less furnished with people than at the present... Of cities and townes either utterly decaied or more than a quarter or half diminished, though some one be a little increased here or there; of townes pulled downe for sheepe-walks, and no more but the lordships now standing in them... I could saie somewhat.” The complaints of these old chroniclers are always exaggerated, but they faithfully reflect the impression that the revolution in the conditions of production made on the people of the time. If we compare the writings of Chancellor Fortescue and Thomas More, we see the gulf between the 15th and the 16th century. As Thornton rightly puts it, the English working class was thrown, without any transition, from its golden age into its iron age.

Legislation was terrified at this revolution. It had not yet reached that height of civilization where the “wealth of the nation” (that is, the formation of capital – value that is used to make more value – and the reckless exploitation and impoverishing of the mass of the people) counts as the *ultima Thule* [farthest limit] of all statecraft. In his history of Henry VII., Bacon says: “Inclosures at that time (1489) began to be more frequent, whereby arable land (which could not be manured [cultivated] without people and families) was turned into pasture, which was easily rid [handled] by a few herdsmen; and tenancies for years, lives, and at will (whereupon much of the yeomanry lived) were turned into demesnes. This bred a decay of people, and (by consequence) a decay of towns, churches, tithes, and the like... In remedying of this inconvenience the king’s wisdom was admirable, and the parliament’s at that time... they took a course to take away depopulating enclosures, and depopulating pasturage.” An Act of Henry VII., 1489, cap. 19, forbade the destruction of all “houses of husbandry” [farmhouses] to which at least 20 acres of land belonged. An Act, 25 Henry VIII., renewed the same law. Among other things, this Act states that many farms and large flocks of cattle, especially of sheep, are concentrated in the hands of a few men. As a result, it says, the rent of land has risen greatly and tillage has fallen off, churches and houses have been pulled down, and marvelous numbers of people have been deprived of the means to maintain themselves and their families. The Act therefore orders the rebuilding of the decayed farmsteads, and fixes a proportion between grain land and pasture land, etc. An Act of 1533 states that some owners possess 24,000 sheep, and limits the number anyone may own to 2,000.[^4] The cry of the people, and the legislation directed for 150 years after Henry VII. against the expropriation of the small farmers and peasants, were both equally fruitless. Bacon, without knowing it, reveals to us the secret of why they failed. “The device of King Henry VII.,” says Bacon, in his “Essays, Civil and Moral,” Essay 29, “was profound and admirable, in making farms and houses of husbandry of a standard; that is, maintained with such a proportion of land unto them as may breed a subject to live in convenient plenty, and no servile condition, and to keep the plough in the hands of the owners and not mere hirelings.”[^5] What the capitalist system demanded, on the other hand, was a degraded and almost servile condition of the mass of the people, their transformation into mercenaries, and the transformation of their means of labor into capital. During this period of transformation, legislation also tried to keep the 4 acres of land by the cottage of the agricultural wage worker, and forbade him to take lodgers into his cottage. In the reign of James I., in 1627, Roger Crocker of Front Mill was condemned for having built a cottage on the manor of Front Mill without 4 acres of land attached to it permanently. As late as the reign of Charles I., in 1638, a royal commission was appointed to enforce the old laws, especially the one about the 4 acres of land. Even in Cromwell’s time, building a house within 4 miles of London was forbidden unless it came with 4 acres of land. As late as the first half of the 18th century, complaint is made if the agricultural worker’s cottage does not have one or two acres of land attached to it. Nowadays he is lucky if it has a little garden, or if he may rent a few roods far away from his cottage. “Landlords and farmers,” says Dr. Hunter, “work here hand in hand. A few acres to the cottage would make the labourers too independent.”[^6]

In the 16th century, the process of forcible expropriation of the people received a new and frightful impulse from the Reformation and from the colossal plundering of church property that followed from it. At the time of the Reformation, the Catholic church was the feudal owner of a great part of the English land. The suppression of the monasteries, etc., hurled their inmates into the proletariat. The church estates were to a large extent given away to greedy royal favorites, or sold at a nominal price to speculating farmers and townspeople. These drove out the hereditary sub-tenants in a mass and threw their holdings together into one. The poorer folk’s legally guaranteed property in a part of the church’s tithes was silently confiscated.[^7] “Pauper ubique jacet” [The pauper lies everywhere], cried Queen Elizabeth after a journey through England. In the 43rd year of her reign the nation was obliged to recognize pauperism officially by introducing a poor-rate. (Pauperism is the condition of the lowest layer of the surplus population, unable to find work and dependent on poor relief.) “The authors of this law seem to have been ashamed to state the grounds of it, for [contrary to traditional usage] it has no preamble whatever.”[^8] By the 16th of Charles I., ch. 4, it was declared perpetual, and in fact it took a new and harsher form only in 1834.[^9] These immediate results of the Reformation were not its most lasting ones. The property of the church formed the religious bulwark of the traditional conditions of landed property. With its fall, these conditions could no longer hold.[^10]

Even in 1698, Fletcher of Saltoun declared in the Scottish parliament: “The number of beggars in Scotland is reckoned at not less than 200,000. The only remedy that I, a republican on principle, can suggest, is to restore the old state of serfdom, to make slaves of all those who are unable to provide for their own subsistence.” Eden, cited above, Book I., ch. 1, pp. 60-61, says: “The decrease of villenage [serfdom] seems necessarily to have been the era of the origin of the poor. Manufactures and commerce are the two parents of our national poor.” Eden, like our Scottish republican on principle, errs only in this: it was not the abolition of villenage, but the abolition of the agricultural worker’s property in the soil, that made him a proletarian, and in the end a pauper. In France, where the expropriation was carried out in another way, the ordonnance of Moulins, 1571, and the Edict of 1656 correspond to the English poor-laws.

Even in the last decade of the 17th century, the yeomanry, the class of independent peasants, were more numerous than the class of farmers. They had formed the backbone of Cromwell’s strength. Even by Macaulay’s own admission, they compared favorably with the drunken squires and with the squires’ servants, the country clergy, who had to marry their masters’ cast-off mistresses. About 1750 the yeomanry had disappeared,[^11] and in the last decade of the 18th century so had the last trace of the agricultural worker’s common land. Here we leave aside the purely economic causes of the agricultural revolution. We deal only with the forcible means employed.

After the restoration of the Stuarts, the landowners carried out by legal means an act of usurpation that was carried out everywhere on the Continent without any legal formality. They abolished the feudal tenure of land, that is, they got rid of all its obligations to the State. They “indemnified” the State by taxes on the peasantry and the rest of the mass of the people. They claimed for themselves the rights of modern private property in estates to which they had only a feudal title. And finally, they passed those laws of settlement (laws that let a parish send poor newcomers back to their parish of origin) which, with the necessary changes, had the same effect on the English agricultural worker as the edict of the Tatar Boris Godunof had on the Russian peasantry.

The “glorious Revolution” brought into power, along with William of Orange, the landlords and capitalists who appropriate surplus-value.[^12] (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.) They opened the new era by practicing thefts of state lands on a colossal scale, thefts that until then had been carried out more modestly. These estates were given away, sold at a ridiculous figure, or even annexed to private estates by direct seizure.[^13] All this happened without the slightest regard for legal etiquette. The Crown lands appropriated by fraud in this way, together with the robbed Church estates (so far as these had not been lost again during the republican revolution), form the basis of the present-day princely domains of the English oligarchy.[^14] The bourgeois capitalists favored the operation with a view, among other things, to promoting free trade in land, to extending modern agriculture based on the large-farm system, and to increasing their supply of free agricultural proletarians ready to hand. Besides, the new landed aristocracy was the natural ally of the new bankocracy, of the newly hatched high finance, and of the large manufacturers, who then depended on protective duties. The English bourgeoisie acted for its own interest just as wisely as the Swedish bourgeoisie did. The Swedes reversed the process: hand in hand with their economic allies, the peasantry, they helped the kings take back the Crown lands from the oligarchy by force. This happened from 1604 on, under Charles X. and Charles XI.

Communal property – always distinct from the State property just dealt with – was an old Germanic institution that lived on under cover of feudalism. We have seen how the forcible seizure of this property, generally accompanied by the turning of arable land into pasture, begins at the end of the 15th century and extends into the 16th. But at that time the process was carried on by means of individual acts of violence, against which legislation fought in vain for a hundred and fifty years. The advance made by the 18th century shows itself in this: the law itself now becomes the instrument of the theft of the people’s land, although the large farmers use their own little independent methods as well.[^15] The parliamentary form of the robbery is that of Acts for enclosures of Commons. In other words, these are decrees by which the landlords grant themselves the people’s land as private property, decrees of expropriation of the people. Sir F. M. Eden, in his crafty special pleading, tries to present communal property as the private property of the great landlords who have taken the place of the feudal lords. But he refutes himself when he himself demands a “general Act of Parliament for the enclosure of Commons” (thereby admitting that a parliamentary *coup d’état* is needed to turn it into private property), and moreover calls on the legislature to compensate the expropriated poor.[^16]

While the place of the independent yeoman was taken by tenants at will – small farmers on yearly leases, a servile rabble dependent on the landlords’ pleasure – the systematic robbery of the Communal lands, next to the theft of the State domains, helped especially to swell those large farms that in the 18th century were called capital farms[^17] or merchant farms,[^18] and to “set free” the agricultural population as proletarians for manufacturing industry.
