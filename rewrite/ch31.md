## Chapter 31: The Genesis of the Industrial Capitalist

<!-- ch31-p001 -->
The industrial[^*1] capitalist did not come into being in the same gradual way as the farmer. No doubt many small guild-masters, and even more independent small artisans, or even wage workers, turned themselves into small capitalists, and then (by gradually extending their exploitation of wage labor and the accumulation that went with it) into full-blown capitalists. (Accumulation of capital means turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows.) In the infancy of capitalist production, things often went as they had in the infancy of medieval towns. There the question of which of the escaped serfs should be master and which servant was largely decided by how early or how late they had fled. The snail's pace of this method in no way matched the commercial needs of the new world market that the great discoveries at the end of the 15th century had created. But the middle ages had handed down two distinct forms of capital (value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger). These forms mature in the most varied economic formations of society, and before the era of the capitalist mode of production they are considered capital quand même [all the same]. They are usurer's capital and merchant's capital.

[^*1]: Industrial is used here as opposed to agricultural. In the “categoric” sense, the farmer is an industrial capitalist just as much as the manufacturer is.

<!-- ch31-p002 -->
> “At present, all the wealth of society goes first into the possession of the capitalist ... he pays the landowner his rent, the labourer his wages [the price paid for labor-power], the tax and tithe gatherer their claims, and keeps a large, indeed the largest, and a continually augmenting share, of the annual produce of labour for himself. The capitalist may now be said to be the first owner of all the wealth of the community, though no law has conferred on him the right to this property... this change has been effected by the taking of interest on capital ... and it is not a little curious that all the law-givers of Europe endeavoured to prevent this by statutes, viz., statutes against usury.... The power of the capitalist over all the wealth of the country is a complete change in the right of property, and by what law, or series of laws, was it effected?”[^2]

[^2]: “The Natural and Artificial Rights of Property Contrasted.” London, 1832, pp. 98-99. The author of this anonymous work is “Th. Hodgskin.”

<!-- ch31-p003 -->
The author should have remembered that revolutions are not made by laws.

<!-- ch31-p004 -->
The money capital formed through usury and trade was prevented from turning into industrial capital: in the countryside by the feudal constitution, in the towns by the guild organization.[^3] These fetters vanished with the breakup of feudal society, with the expropriation (the taking away of people's property, here land and means of production) and partial eviction of the country population. The new manufacturing workshops were set up at seaports, or at inland points beyond the control of the old municipalities and their guilds. Hence, in England, a bitter struggle of the corporate towns against these new nurseries of industry.

[^3]: Even as late as 1794, the small cloth-makers of Leeds sent a deputation to Parliament with a petition for a law forbidding any merchant to become a manufacturer. (Dr. Aikin, cited above.)

<!-- ch31-p005 -->
The discovery of gold and silver in America; the extermination of the native population, its enslavement, and its entombment in the mines; the beginning of the conquest and looting of the East Indies; the turning of Africa into a game preserve for the commercial hunting of Black people: these signaled the rosy dawn of the era of capitalist production. These idyllic proceedings are the chief elements of primitive accumulation (the historical process that separated the producers from their means of production, above all by driving peasants off the land, and so created the conditions for capitalism). Close on their heels comes the commercial war of the European nations, with the whole globe as its stage. It begins with the revolt of the Netherlands against Spain, takes on giant dimensions in England's Anti-Jacobin War, and is still going on in the opium wars against China, etc.

<!-- ch31-p006 -->
The different elements of primitive accumulation are now distributed, more or less in chronological order, over Spain, Portugal, Holland, France, and England in particular. In England at the end of the 17th century, they come together in a systematic combination that takes in the colonies, the national debt, the modern system of taxation, and the protectionist system. These methods depend in part on brute force, for example the colonial system. But they all use the power of the State, the concentrated and organized force of society, to speed up, hothouse fashion, the process by which the feudal mode of production is transformed into the capitalist mode, and to shorten the transition. Force is the midwife of every old society pregnant with a new one. It is itself an economic power.

<!-- ch31-p007 -->
W. Howitt, a man who makes a specialty of Christianity, says of the Christian colonial system:

<!-- ch31-p008 -->
> “The barbarities and desperate outrages of the so-called Christian race, throughout every region of the world, and upon every people they have been able to subdue, are not to be paralleled by those of any other race, however fierce, however untaught, and however reckless of mercy and of shame, in any age of the earth.”[^4]

[^4]: William Howitt: “Colonisation and Christianity: A Popular History of the Treatment of the Natives by the Europeans in all their Colonies.” London, 1838, p. 9. There is a good compilation on the treatment of the slaves in Charles Comte, “Traité de la Législation.” 3me éd. Bruxelles, 1837. One must study this subject in detail to see what the bourgeoisie (the capitalist class) makes of itself and of the worker wherever it can, without restraint, model the world after its own image.

<!-- ch31-p009 -->
The history of the colonial administration of Holland – and Holland was the leading capitalist nation of the 17th century –

<!-- ch31-p010 -->
> “is one of the most extraordinary relations of treachery, bribery, massacre, and meanness”[^5]

[^5]: Thomas Stamford Raffles, former Lieutenant-Governor of that island: “The History of Java,” London, 1817.

<!-- ch31-p011 -->
Nothing is more characteristic than their system of stealing men to get slaves for Java. The men-stealers were trained for this purpose. The thief, the interpreter, and the seller were the chief agents in this trade, and native princes were the chief sellers. The young people who were stolen were thrown into the secret dungeons of Celebes until they were ready to be sent to the slave-ships. An official report says:

<!-- ch31-p012 -->
> “This one town of Macassar, *e.g.*, is full of secret prisons, one more horrible than the other, crammed with unfortunates, victims of greed and tyranny fettered in chains, forcibly torn from their families.”

<!-- ch31-p013 -->
To secure Malacca, the Dutch bribed the Portuguese governor. He let them into the town in 1641. They hurried at once to his house and assassinated him, so as to “abstain” from paying £21,875, the price of his treason. Wherever they set foot, devastation and depopulation followed. Banjuwangi, a province of Java, had over 80,000 inhabitants in 1750, and only 18,000 in 1811. Sweet commerce!

<!-- ch31-p014 -->
The English East India Company, as is well known, obtained not only political rule in India but also the exclusive monopoly of the tea trade, as well as of the Chinese trade in general, and of the transport of goods to and from Europe. But the coastal trade of India and between the islands, as well as the internal trade of India, was the monopoly of the higher employees of the company. The monopolies of salt, opium, betel, and other commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use) were inexhaustible mines of wealth. The employees themselves fixed the price and plundered the unhappy Hindus at will. The Governor-General took part in this private trade. His favorites received contracts on terms under which they, cleverer than the alchemists, made gold out of nothing. Great fortunes sprang up like mushrooms in a day; primitive accumulation went on without a shilling being advanced. The trial of Warren Hastings swarms with such cases. Here is one instance. A contract for opium was given to a certain Sullivan just as he was leaving on an official mission to a part of India far away from the opium district. Sullivan sold his contract to one Binn for £40,000. Binn sold it the same day for £60,000, and the final buyer, who carried out the contract, declared that even after all this he made an enormous gain. According to one of the lists laid before Parliament, the Company and its employees got £6,000,000 from the Indians as gifts from 1757-1766. Between 1769 and 1770, the English manufactured a famine by buying up all the rice and refusing to sell it again except at fabulous prices.[^6]

[^6]: In the year 1866 more than a million Hindus died of hunger in the province of Orissa alone. Nevertheless, an attempt was made to enrich the Indian treasury by the price at which the necessities of life were sold to the starving people.

<!-- ch31-p015 -->
The treatment of the native peoples was, naturally, most frightful in plantation colonies meant only for export trade, such as the West Indies, and in rich and well-populated countries that were given over to plunder, such as Mexico and India. But even in the colonies properly so called, the Christian character of primitive accumulation stayed true to itself. Those sober virtuosos of Protestantism, the Puritans of New England, by decrees of their assembly in 1703 set a bounty of £40 on every Indian scalp and every captured Native American; in 1720 a bounty of £100 on every scalp; in 1744, after Massachusetts-Bay had proclaimed a certain tribe rebels, the following prices: for a male scalp of 12 years and upwards £100 (new currency), for a male prisoner £105, for women and children prisoners £50, for scalps of women and children £50. Some decades later, the colonial system took its revenge on the descendants of the pious pilgrim fathers, who had grown rebellious in the meantime. At English instigation and for English pay they were tomahawked by Native Americans. The British Parliament proclaimed bloodhounds and scalping to be “means that God and Nature had given into its hand.”

<!-- ch31-p016 -->
The colonial system ripened trade and navigation as in a hothouse. The “societies Monopolia” of Luther were powerful levers for the concentration of capital (the growth of individual capitals through accumulation). The colonies secured a market for the budding manufacturing workshops, and, through the monopoly of that market, an increased accumulation. The treasures captured outside Europe by open looting, enslavement, and murder flowed back to the mother country and were turned into capital there. Holland, which was the first to develop the colonial system fully, already stood at the peak of its commercial greatness in 1648. It was

<!-- ch31-p017 -->
> “in almost exclusive possession of the East Indian trade and the commerce between the south-east and north-west of Europe. Its fisheries, marine and branches of manufacture surpassed those of any other country. The total capital of the Republic was probably more important than that of all the rest of Europe put together.” Gülich forgets to add that by 1648 the people of Holland were more overworked, poorer, and more brutally oppressed than those of all the rest of Europe put together.

<!-- ch31-p018 -->
Today industrial supremacy carries commercial supremacy with it. In the period of manufacture properly so called, on the other hand, it is commercial supremacy that gives industrial predominance. (Manufacture is the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop.) Hence the dominant role that the colonial system played at that time. It was “the strange God” who perched himself on the altar side by side with the old Gods of Europe, and one fine day, with a shove and a kick, tumbled them all down in a heap. It proclaimed the making of surplus-value to be the sole end and aim of humanity. (Surplus-value is the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it.)

<!-- ch31-p019 -->
The system of public credit, that is, of national debts, whose origin we find in Genoa and Venice as early as the Middle Ages, took possession of Europe in general during the manufacturing period. The colonial system, with its sea trade and commercial wars, served as a hothouse for it. So it first took root in Holland. National debts, that is, the alienation (the selling off) of the state – whether despotic, constitutional, or republican – put their stamp on the capitalist era. The only part of the so-called national wealth that actually passes into the collective possession of modern peoples is their national debt.[^7] Hence, as a necessary consequence, the modern doctrine that a nation becomes the richer the more deeply it is in debt. Public credit becomes the creed of capital. And with the rise of national debt-making, lack of faith in the national debt takes the place of the blasphemy against the Holy Ghost, which may not be forgiven.

[^7]: William Cobbett remarks that in England all public institutions are called “royal”; to make up for this, however, there is the “national” debt.

<!-- ch31-p020 -->
The public debt becomes one of the most powerful levers of primitive accumulation. As if with the stroke of a magician's wand, it gives barren money the power to breed, and so turns it into capital, without its having to expose itself to the troubles and risks that cannot be separated from its use in industry, or even in usury. The state's creditors actually give nothing away, for the sum they lend is turned into public bonds that are easily sold, and these go on functioning in their hands just as so much hard cash would. But there is more. Apart from the class of lazy annuitants (people who live on the yearly interest) created in this way, and from the improvised wealth of the financiers, the middlemen between the government and the nation – and apart also from the tax-farmers (private collectors of taxes), merchants, and private manufacturers, to whom a good part of every national loan does the service of a capital fallen from heaven – the national debt has given rise to joint-stock companies, to dealings in negotiable securities of all kinds, and to agiotage, in a word to stock-exchange gambling and the modern bankocracy.

<!-- ch31-p021 -->
At their birth, the great banks, decorated with national titles, were only associations of private speculators. These placed themselves at the side of governments and, thanks to the privileges they received, were in a position to advance money to the State. Hence the accumulation of the national debt has no surer measure than the successive rise in the stock of these banks, whose full development dates from the founding of the Bank of England in 1694. The Bank of England began by lending its money to the Government at 8%. At the same time Parliament empowered it to coin money out of the same capital, by lending it again to the public in the form of banknotes. It was allowed to use these notes for discounting bills, making advances on commodities, and buying the precious metals. It was not long before this credit-money, made by the bank itself, became the coin in which the Bank of England made its loans to the State and paid the interest on the public debt on the State's behalf. It was not enough that the bank gave with one hand and took back more with the other; even while it was receiving, it remained the eternal creditor of the nation down to the last shilling advanced. Gradually it inevitably became the storehouse of the country's metallic hoard (money withdrawn from circulation and kept as a store of value), and the center of gravity of all commercial credit. What effect the sudden rise of this brood of bankocrats, financiers, rentiers, brokers, stock-jobbers, etc., had on their contemporaries is proved by the writings of that time, for example by Bolingbroke's.[^8]

[^8]: “Si les Tartares inondaient l’Europe aujourd’hui, il faudrait bien des affaires pour leur faire entendre ce que c’est qu’un financier parmi nous.” [If the Tartars flooded into Europe today, it would take a great deal of trouble to make them understand what a financier is among us.] Montesquieu, “Esprit des lois,” t. iv., p. 33, ed. Londres, 1769.

<!-- ch31-p022 -->
With the national debt there arose an international credit system, which often hides one of the sources of primitive accumulation in this or that people. Thus the villainies of the Venetian system of robbery formed one of the secret foundations of the capital-wealth of Holland, to which Venice in her decline lent large sums of money. So it was too between Holland and England. By the beginning of the 18th century the Dutch manufacturing workshops had been far outstripped. Holland had ceased to be the leading nation in trade and industry. One of its main lines of business from 1701-1776, therefore, is the lending out of enormous amounts of capital, especially to its great rival England. The same thing is going on today between England and the United States. A great deal of capital that appears today in the United States without any birth certificate was, yesterday, in England, the capitalized blood of children.

<!-- ch31-p023 -->
The national debt is backed by the public revenue, which must cover the yearly interest payments, etc. So the modern system of taxation was the necessary complement of the system of national loans. The loans let the government meet extraordinary expenses without the taxpayers feeling it immediately, but as a consequence they make higher taxes necessary. On the other hand, the rise in taxes caused by the accumulation of debts contracted one after another forces the government always to turn to new loans for new extraordinary expenses. Modern fiscal policy, whose pivot is the taxes on the most necessary means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live), which raise their price, thus carries within itself the germ of automatic growth. Overtaxation is not an accident, but rather a principle. In Holland, therefore, where this system was first introduced, the great patriot DeWitt praised it in his “Maxims” as the best system for making the wage worker submissive, frugal, hardworking, and overburdened with labor. Here, however, we are less concerned with its destructive effect on the condition of the wage worker than with the forcible expropriation that results from it: the expropriation of peasants, artisans, and, in a word, all elements of the lower middle class. On this there are not two opinions, even among the bourgeois economists. Its expropriating effect is heightened still further by the system of protection, which forms one of its integral parts.

<!-- ch31-p024 -->
The great part that the public debt, and the fiscal system that goes with it, has played in the capitalization of wealth and the expropriation of the masses has led many writers, such as Cobbett, Doubleday and others, to look for the fundamental cause of the misery of modern peoples in this, wrongly.

<!-- ch31-p025 -->
The system of protection was an artificial means of manufacturing manufacturers, of expropriating independent workers, of turning the national means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things) and of subsistence into capital, and of forcibly cutting short the transition from the medieval to the modern mode of production. The European states tore one another to pieces over the patent of this invention. Once they had entered the service of the surplus-value makers, they did not merely make their own people pay for this purpose, indirectly through protective duties and directly through export bounties. They also forcibly rooted out all industry in their dependent countries, as England, for example, did with the Irish wool manufacture. On the continent of Europe, following Colbert's example, the process was much simplified. Here the primitive industrial capital came in part directly out of the state treasury. “Why,” cries Mirabeau, “why go so far to seek the cause of the manufacturing glory of Saxony before the war? 180,000,000 of debts contracted by the sovereigns!”[^9]

[^9]: Mirabeau, cited above, t. vi., p. 101.

<!-- ch31-p026 -->
Colonial system, public debts, heavy taxes, protection, commercial wars, etc.: these children of the true manufacturing period grow to gigantic size during the infancy of modern industry (large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system). Its birth is announced by a great slaughter of the innocents. Like the royal navy, the factories were recruited by means of the press-gang (the gangs that seized men by force for service). Sir F. M. Eden is blasé about the horrors of the expropriation of the agricultural population from the soil, from the last third of the 15th century to his own time. He rejoices with all self-satisfaction in this process, “essential” for establishing capitalist agriculture and “the due proportion between arable and pasture land.” Yet he does not show the same economic insight about the necessity of child-stealing and child-slavery for transforming manufacturing exploitation into factory exploitation, and for establishing the “true relation” between capital and labor-power. (Labor-power is a person's capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) He says:

<!-- ch31-p027 -->
> “It may, perhaps, be worthy the attention of the public to consider, whether any manufacture, which, in order to be carried on successfully, requires that cottages and workhouses should be ransacked for poor children; that they should be employed by turns during the greater part of the night and robbed of that rest which, though indispensable to all, is most required by the young; and that numbers of both sexes, of different ages and dispositions, should be collected together in such a manner that the contagion of example cannot but lead to profligacy and debauchery; will add to the sum of individual or national felicity?”[^10]

[^10]: Eden, cited above, Vol. I., Book II., Ch. 1., p. 421.

<!-- ch31-p028 -->
> “In the counties of Derbyshire, Nottinghamshire, and more particularly in Lancashire,” says Fielden, “the newly-invented machinery was used in large factories built on the sides of streams capable of turning the water-wheel. Thousands of hands were suddenly required in these places, remote from towns; and Lancashire, in particular, being, till then, comparatively thinly populated and barren, a population was all that she now wanted. The small and nimble fingers of little children being by very far the most in request, the custom instantly sprang up of procuring *apprentices* from the different parish workhouses of London, Birmingham, and elsewhere. Many, many thousands of these little, hapless creatures were sent down into the north, being from the age of 7 to the age of 13 or 14 years old. The custom was for the master to clothe his apprentices and to feed and lodge them in an “apprentice house” near the factory; overseers were appointed to see to the works, whose interest it was to work the children to the utmost, because their pay was in proportion to the quantity of work that they could exact. Cruelty was, of course, the consequence. ... In many of the manufacturing districts, but particularly, I am afraid, in the guilty county to which I belong [Lancashire], cruelties the most heart-rending were practised upon the unoffending and friendless creatures who were thus consigned to the charge of master-manufacturers; they were harassed to the brink of death by excess of labour ... were flogged, fettered and tortured in the most exquisite refinement of cruelty; ... they were in many cases starved to the bone while flogged to their work and ... even in some instances ... were driven to commit suicide.... The beautiful and romantic valleys of Derbyshire, Nottinghamshire and Lancashire, secluded from the public eye, became the dismal solitudes of torture, and of many a murder. The profits of manufacturers were enormous; but this only whetted the appetite that it should have satisfied, and therefore the manufacturers had recourse to an expedient that seemed to secure to them those profits without any possibility of limit; they began the practice of what is termed “night-working,” that is, having tired one set of hands, by working them throughout the day, they had another set ready to go on working throughout the night; the day-set getting into the beds that the night-set had just quitted, and in their turn again, the night-set getting into the beds that the day-set quitted in the morning. It is a common tradition in Lancashire, that the beds *never get cold.*”[^11]

[^11]: John Fielden, cited above, pp. 5, 6. On the earlier infamies of the factory system (production in which a system of machines, driven by a central power source, does the work and workers attend the machines), compare Dr. Aikin (1795), cited above, p. 219, and Gisborne: “Enquiry into the Duties of Men,” 1795 Vol. II. When the steam-engine moved the factories from the country waterfalls into the middle of towns, the “abstemious” surplus-value maker found the child-material ready to hand, without being forced to fetch slaves from the workhouses. When Sir R. Peel (father of the “minister of plausibility”) brought in his bill for the protection of children in 1815, Francis Horner, leading light of the Bullion Committee and intimate friend of Ricardo, said in the House of Commons: “It is notorious, that with a bankrupt’s effects, a gang, if he might use the word, of these children had been put up to sale, and were advertised publicly as part of the property. A most atrocious instance had been brought before the Court of King’s Bench two years before, in which a number of these boys, apprenticed by a parish in London to one manufacturer, had been transferred to another, and had been found by some benevolent persons in a state of absolute famine. Another case more horrible had come to his knowledge while on a [Parliamentary] Committee ... that not many years ago, an agreement had been made between a London parish and a Lancashire manufacturer, by which it was stipulated, that with every 20 sound children one idiot should be taken.”

<!-- ch31-p029 -->
With the development of capitalist production during the manufacturing period, the public opinion of Europe had lost the last remnant of shame and conscience. The nations bragged cynically of every infamy that served them as a means of capitalist accumulation. Read, for example, the naïve Annals of Commerce of the worthy A. Anderson. Here it is trumpeted as a triumph of English statecraft that at the Peace of Utrecht England extorted from the Spaniards, by the Asiento Treaty, the privilege of being allowed to carry on the trade in Black people between Africa and Spanish America as well, a trade that until then it had carried on only between Africa and the English West Indies. England thereby acquired the right to supply Spanish America until 1743 with 4,800 Black people a year. At the same time, this threw an official cloak over British smuggling. Liverpool grew fat on the slave trade. This was its method of primitive accumulation. And even to the present day, Liverpool “respectability” is the Pindar, the praise-singer, of the slave trade, which – compare the work of Aikin [1795] already quoted – “has coincided with that spirit of bold adventure which has characterised the trade of Liverpool and rapidly carried it to its present state of prosperity; has occasioned vast employment for shipping and sailors, and greatly augmented the demand for the manufactures of the country” (p. 339). Liverpool employed in the slave trade, in 1730, 15 ships; in 1751, 53; in 1760, 74; in 1770, 96; and in 1792, 132.[^12]

[^12]: In 1790 there were ten slaves for every free man in the English West Indies, fourteen for one in the French, and twenty-three for one in the Dutch. (Henry Brougham: “An Inquiry into the Colonial Policy of the European Powers.” Edin. 1803, vol. II., p. 74.)

<!-- ch31-p030 -->
While the cotton industry introduced child-slavery in England, in the United States it gave a stimulus to the transformation of the earlier, more or less patriarchal slavery into a system of commercial exploitation. In fact, the veiled slavery of the wage workers in Europe needed, as its pedestal, slavery pure and simple in the new world.

<!-- ch31-p031 -->
Tantae molis erat [So much effort did it take] to establish the “eternal laws of Nature” of the capitalist mode of production; to complete the process of separation between workers and the conditions of labor; to transform, at one pole, the social means of production and subsistence into capital, and at the opposite pole, the mass of the population into wage workers, into “free labouring poor,” that artificial product of modern society.[^13] If money, according to Augier,[^14] “comes into the world with a congenital blood-stain on one cheek,” capital comes dripping from head to foot, from every pore, with blood and dirt.[^15]

[^13]: The phrase “labouring poor” is found in English legislation from the moment when the class of wage workers becomes noticeable. This term is used in opposition, on the one hand, to the “idle poor,” beggars, etc., on the. out and out vulgar bourgeois. “The laws of commerce are the laws of Nature, and therefore the laws of God.” (E. Burke, cited above, pp. 31, 32.) No wonder that, true to the laws of God and of Nature, he always sold himself in the best market. A very good portrait of this Edmund Burke, during his liberal period, can be found in the writings of the Rev. Mr. Tucker. Tucker was a parson and a Tory, but otherwise an honorable man and a competent political economist. In the face of the infamous cowardice of character that reigns today, and believes most devoutly in “the laws of commerce,” it is our bounden duty to brand, again and again, the Burkes, who differ from their successors in only one thing — talent.

[^14]: Marie Angier: “Du Crédit Public.” Paris, 1842.

[^15]: “Capital is said by a Quarterly Reviewer to fly turbulence and strife, and to be timid, which is very true; but this is very incompletely stating the question. Capital eschews no profit, or very small profit, just as Nature was formerly said to abhor a vacuum. With adequate profit, capital is very bold. A certain 10 per cent. will ensure its employment anywhere; 20 per cent. certain will produce eagerness; 50 per cent., positive audacity; 100 per cent. will make it ready to trample on all human laws; 300 per cent., and there is not a crime at which it will scruple, nor a risk it will not run, even to the chance of its owner being hanged.
