## Chapter 30: Reaction of the Agricultural Revolution on Industry. Creation of the Home- Market for Industrial Capital

<!-- ch30-p001 -->
The expropriation (taking away people's property, here their land and means of production) and expulsion of the agricultural population was intermittent, but it was renewed again and again. As we saw, it supplied the town industries with a mass of proletarians (members of the proletariat, the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power). These proletarians had no connection at all with the corporate guilds and were not bound by them. This fortunate circumstance makes old A. Anderson (not to be confused with James Anderson), in his “History of Commerce,” believe in the direct intervention of Providence. We must still stop for a moment on this element of primitive accumulation (the historical process that separated the producers from their means of production, above all by driving peasants off the land, and so created the conditions for capitalism). The thinning-out of the independent, self-supporting peasants did not only bring about the crowding together of the industrial proletariat, in the way that Geoffrey Saint Hilaire explained the condensing of cosmic matter at one place by its thinning-out at another.[^1] Although fewer people now worked the soil, it brought forth as much produce as before, or more. This was because the revolution in the conditions of landed property was accompanied by improved methods of cultivation, greater cooperation (many workers working together, side by side, in the same or connected processes, under one capitalist's command), concentration of the means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things), etc. It was also because the agricultural wage workers were not only driven harder[^2], but the field of production on which they worked for themselves shrank more and more. Therefore, when a part of the agricultural population was set free, their former means of nourishment were also set free. These were now turned into material elements of variable capital. (Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power; it reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies.) The peasant, expropriated and cast adrift, must buy their value from his new master, the industrial capitalist, in the form of wages. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. Wages are the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself.) What holds for the means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) also holds for the raw materials of industry that depend on home agriculture. They were turned into an element of constant capital (the part of capital spent on means of production; its value is passed on to the product but does not grow). Suppose, for example, that a part of the Westphalian peasants, who in the time of Frederick II all spun flax, were forcibly expropriated and hunted off the soil, and that the other part, who stayed, were turned into day laborers for large farmers. At the same time large establishments for flax-spinning and weaving spring up, and the men who have been “set free” now work in them for wages. The flax looks exactly as before. Not a fiber of it has changed, but a new social soul has popped into its body. It now forms a part of the constant capital of the master manufacturer. Before, it was divided among a number of small producers, who grew it themselves and spun it with their families on a small scale. Now it is concentrated in the hands of one capitalist, who sets others to spin and weave it for him. The extra labor spent in flax-spinning used to be realized in extra income for numerous peasant families, or perhaps, in Frederick II’s time, in taxes *pour le roi de Prusse* [for the King of Prussia]. Now it is realized in profit for a few capitalists. The spindles and looms, once scattered over the face of the country, are now crowded together in a few great labor-barracks, together with the workers and the raw material. And spindles, looms, and raw material have now been turned from means of independent existence for the spinners and weavers into means for commanding them and sucking unpaid labor out of them.[^3] Looking at the large manufacturing workshops and the large farms, one does not see that they arose from throwing many small centers of production into one, and that they were built up by expropriating many small independent producers. Nevertheless, the popular intuition was not wrong. In the time of Mirabeau, the lion of the Revolution, the great manufacturing workshops were still called *manufactures réunies*, workshops thrown into one, as we speak of fields thrown into one. > “We are only paying attention to the grand manufactories, in which hundreds of men work under a director and which are commonly called *manufactures réunies*. Those where a very large number of labourers work, each separately and on his own account, are hardly considered; they are placed at an infinite distance from the others. This is a great error, as the latter alone make a really important object of national prosperity.... The large workshop (manufacture réunie) will enrich prodigiously one or two entrepreneurs, but the labourers will only be journeymen, paid more or less, and will not have any share in the success of the undertaking. In the discrete workshop (manufacture separée), on the contrary, no one will become rich, but many labourers will be comfortable; the saving and the industrious will be able to amass a little capital, to put by a little for a birth of a child, for an illness, for themselves or their belongings. The number of saving and industrious labourers will increase, because they will see in good conduct, in activity, a means of essentially bettering their condition, and not of obtaining a small rise in wages that can never be of any importance of the future, and whose sole result is to place men in the position to live a little better, but only from day to day.... The large workshops, undertakings of certain private persons who pay labourers from day to day to work for their gain, may be able to put these private individuals at their ease, but they will never be an object worth the attention of governments. Discrete workshops, for the most part combined with cultivation of small holdings, are the only free ones.”[^4] The expropriation and eviction of a part of the agricultural population did not only set free the workers, their means of subsistence, and material for labor for industrial capital. It also created the home market.

In fact, the events that turned the small peasants into wage workers, and turned their means of subsistence and of labor into material elements of capital, at the same time created a home market for capital. Before, the peasant family produced the means of subsistence and the raw materials, and for the most part consumed them itself. These raw materials and means of subsistence have now become commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use). The large farmer sells them, and he finds his market in the manufacturing workshops. Yarn, linen, coarse woolen goods: their raw materials had been within the reach of every peasant family, which had spun and woven them for its own use. Now they were turned into articles of manufacture, and the country districts at once served as markets for them. Until now, stray artisans had found many scattered customers in the numerous small producers working on their own account. These customers now concentrate into one great market, supplied by industrial capital.[^5] So, hand in hand with the expropriation of the self-supporting peasants, with their separation from their means of production, goes the destruction of rural domestic industry, the process of separation between manufacture and agriculture. And only the destruction of rural domestic industry can give the internal market of a country the extent and solidity that the capitalist mode of production requires. Still, the manufacturing period in the strict sense does not succeed in carrying out this transformation radically and completely. It will be remembered that manufacture in the strict sense (the stage and form of capitalist production, roughly from the mid-16th to the late 18th century, based on handicraft work divided among many workers in one workshop) conquers only part of the domain of national production. It always rests, as its ultimate basis, on the handicrafts of the towns and the domestic industry of the rural districts. If it destroys these in one form, in particular branches, at certain points, it calls them up again elsewhere, because it needs them to prepare raw material up to a certain point. It therefore produces a new class of small villagers. They work the soil as a side occupation, but their chief occupation is industrial labor, whose products they sell to the manufacturers, either directly or through merchants. This is one cause, though not the chief one, of a phenomenon that at first puzzles the student of English history.[^6] From the last third of the 15th century on, he finds constant complaints, broken only at certain intervals, about capitalist farming encroaching on the country districts and about the progressive destruction of the peasantry. On the other hand, he always finds this peasantry turning up again, though in smaller numbers and always under worse conditions. The chief reason is this: England is at one time chiefly a grower of grain, at another chiefly a breeder of cattle, in alternating periods, and the extent of peasant farming rises and falls with these periods. Modern industry (large-scale production based on machinery and the factory system) alone, and finally, supplies in machinery the lasting basis of capitalist agriculture. It alone expropriates the enormous majority of the agricultural population radically, and completes the separation between agriculture and rural domestic industry, tearing up that industry's roots, spinning and weaving.[^7] It therefore also, for the first time, conquers the entire home market for industrial capital.[^8]

    But now comes Carey and accuses England, and surely not without reason, of trying to turn every other country into a mere agricultural nation, whose manufacturer is to be England. He claims that Turkey has been ruined in this way, because “the owners and occupants of land have never been permitted by England to strengthen themselves by the formation of that natural alliance between the plough and the loom, the hammer and the harrow.” (“The Slave Trade,” p.125.) According to Carey, Urquhart himself is one of the chief agents in the ruin of Turkey, where he had spread Free-trade propaganda in the English interest. The best of it is that Carey, a great Russophile by the way, wants to prevent the process of separation by means of the very system of protection that speeds it up.
