### Section 5: The Struggle for a Normal Working Day. Compulsory Laws for the Extension of the Working Day from the Middle of the 14th to the End of the 17th Century

<!-- ch10-p110 -->
“What is a working day? What is the length of time during which capital may consume the labour-power whose daily value it buys? How far may the working day be extended beyond the working-time necessary for the reproduction of labour-power itself?” We have seen how capital answers these questions: the working day contains the full 24 hours, minus the few hours of rest without which labor-power absolutely refuses to serve again. So it goes without saying that the worker, his whole life long, is nothing but labor-power. It follows that all his disposable time is, by nature and by law, labor-time, to be devoted to the self-expansion of capital. Time for education, for intellectual development, for carrying out social functions and for social dealings, for the free play of his bodily and mental activity, even the rest time of Sunday (and that in a country of strict Sabbath-keepers!)[^72] – moonshine! But in its blind, unrestrainable passion, its werewolf hunger for surplus labor, capital oversteps not only the moral but even the merely physical maximum limits of the working day. It seizes the time needed for the growth, development, and healthy upkeep of the body. It steals the time required for taking in fresh air and sunlight. It haggles over a meal-time, and where possible it makes the meal part of the production process itself, so that food is given to the worker as to a mere means of production, as coal is fed to the boiler and grease and oil to the machinery. It cuts down the sound sleep needed to restore, repair, and refresh the bodily powers to just as many hours of stupor as an organism that is absolutely exhausted needs in order to revive. It is not the normal upkeep of labor-power that is to set the limits of the working day. It is the greatest possible daily expenditure of labor-power, no matter how diseased, forced, and painful it may be, that is to set the limits of the workers’ period of rest. Capital cares nothing for how long labor-power lives. All that concerns it is simply and solely the maximum of labor-power that can be set flowing in one working day. It reaches this goal by shortening the worker’s life, just as a greedy farmer snatches bigger harvests from the soil by robbing it of its fertility.

[^72]: In England, even now, a worker in a rural district is occasionally sentenced to prison for desecrating the Sabbath by working in his front garden. The same worker is punished for breach of contract if he stays away from his metal, paper, or glass works on a Sunday, even if he does so out of a religious whim. The orthodox Parliament will hear nothing of Sabbath-breaking if it happens in the process of expanding capital. A memorial (August 1863), in which the London day-laborers in fish and poultry shops asked for Sunday labor to be abolished, states that for the first 6 days of the week their work lasts 15 hours a day on average, and on Sunday 8-10 hours. From this same memorial we also learn that the delicate gourmets among the aristocratic hypocrites of Exeter Hall especially encourage this “Sunday labour.” These “holy ones,” so zealous *in cute curanda* [in caring for their own skin, that is, their bodily pleasures], show their Christianity by the humility with which they bear the overwork, the hardships, and the hunger of others. *Obsequium ventris istis (the labourers) perniciosius est* [Serving the belly is more ruinous to them (the workers) – paraphrase of Horace].

<!-- ch10-p111 -->
Thus, by extending the working day, the capitalist mode of production (essentially the production of surplus-value, the absorption of surplus labor) produces not only the deterioration of human labor-power, by robbing it of its normal moral and physical conditions of development and activity. It also produces the premature exhaustion and death of this labor-power itself.[^73] It extends the worker’s production time within a given period by shortening his actual lifetime.

[^73]: “We have given in our previous reports the statements of several experienced manufacturers to the effect that over-hours ... certainly tend prematurely to exhaust the working power of the men.” (cited above, 64. p. xiii.)

<!-- ch10-p112 -->
But the value of labor-power includes the value of the commodities needed to reproduce the worker, that is, to keep the working class going. Capital, in its boundless passion for self-expansion, necessarily strives for an unnatural extension of the working day. If this extension shortens the life of the individual worker, and so the length of time his labor-power lasts, then the forces used up have to be replaced more quickly, and the total cost of reproducing labor-power will be greater. It is just as with a machine: the faster the machine wears out, the greater the part of its value that has to be reproduced every day. So it would seem that capital’s own interest points toward a normal working day.

The slave-owner buys his worker as he buys his horse. If he loses his slave, he loses capital that he can only replace by spending money again in the slave market.

<!-- ch10-p114 -->
> But “the rice-grounds of Georgia, or the swamps of the Mississippi may be fatally injurious to the human constitution; but the waste of human life which the cultivation of these districts necessitates, is not so great that it cannot be repaired from the teeming preserves of Virginia and Kentucky. Considerations of economy, moreover, which, under a natural system, afford some security for humane treatment by identifying the master’s interest with the slave’s preservation, when once trading in slaves is practiced, become reasons for racking to the uttermost the toil of the slave; for, when his place can at once be supplied from foreign preserves, the duration of his life becomes a matter of less moment [importance] than its productiveness while it lasts. It is accordingly a maxim of slave management, in slave-importing countries, that the most effective economy is that which takes out of the human chattel in the shortest space of time the utmost amount of exertion it is capable of putting forth. It is in tropical culture, where annual profits often equal the whole capital of plantations, that negro life is most recklessly sacrificed. It is the agriculture of the West Indies, which has been for centuries prolific of fabulous wealth, that has engulfed millions of the African race. It is in Cuba, at this day, whose revenues are reckoned by millions, and whose planters are princes, that we see in the servile class, the coarsest fare, the most exhausting and unremitting toil, and even the absolute destruction of a portion of its numbers every year.”[^74]

[^74]: Cairnes, “The Slave Power,” pp. 110. 111.

<!-- ch10-p115 -->
*Mutato nomine de te fabula narratur* [Change the name, and the story is told about you – Horace]. For slave trade read labor market; for Kentucky and Virginia read Ireland and the agricultural districts of England, Scotland, and Wales; for Africa read Germany. We heard how overwork thinned the ranks of the bakers in London. Even so, the London labor market is always overstocked with German and other candidates for death in the bakeries. Pottery, as we saw, is one of the industries whose workers live the shortest lives. So is there any shortage of potters? Josiah Wedgwood, the inventor of modern pottery, who was himself originally an ordinary worker, told the House of Commons in 1785 that the whole trade employed from 15,000 to 20,000 people.[^75] In the year 1861 the population of just the town centers of this industry in Great Britain numbered 101,302.

[^75]: John Ward: “The Borough of Stoke-upon-Trent,” London, 1843, p. 42.

No doubt in certain periods of feverish activity the labor market shows significant gaps. In 1834, for example. But then the manufacturers proposed to the Poor Law Commissioners that they should send the “surplus-population” of the agricultural districts to the north, explaining “that the manufacturers would absorb and use it up.”[^77]

[^77]: “Those were the very words used by the cotton manufacturers.” Cited above.

> Agents were appointed with the consent of the Poor Law Commissioners. ... An office was set up in Manchester, to which lists were sent of those workpeople in the agricultural districts wanting employment, and their names were registered in books. The manufacturers attended at these offices, and selected such persons as they chose; when they had selected such persons as their ‘wants required’, they gave instructions to have them forwarded to Manchester, and they were sent, ticketed like bales of goods, by canals, or with carriers, others tramping on the road, and many of them were found on the way lost and half-starved. This system had grown up unto a regular trade. This House will hardly believe it, but I tell them, that this traffic in human flesh was as well kept up, they were in effect as regularly sold to these [Manchester] manufacturers as slaves are sold to the cotton-grower in the United States.... In 1860, ‘the cotton trade was at its zenith.’ ... The manufacturers again found that they were short of hands.... They applied to the ‘flesh agents, as they are called. Those agents sent to the southern downs of England, to the pastures of Dorsetshire, to the glades of Devonshire, to the people tending kine [cattle] in Wiltshire, but they sought in vain. The surplus-population was ‘absorbed.’”

When the French treaty was concluded, the *Bury Guardian* said that “10,000 additional hands could be absorbed by Lancashire, and that 30,000 or 40,000 will be needed.” After the “flesh agents and sub-agents” had searched the agricultural districts in vain,

> “a deputation came up to London, and waited on the right hon. gentleman [Mr. Villiers, President of the Poor Law Board] with a view of obtaining poor children from certain union houses [workhouses] for the mills of Lancashire.”[^78]

[^78]: Cited above. Mr. Villiers, despite the best of intentions on his part, was “legally” obliged to refuse the manufacturers’ requests. These gentlemen, however, got what they wanted through the obliging nature of the local poor law boards. Mr. A. Redgrave, Inspector of Factories, asserts that this time the system under which orphans and pauper children were treated “legally” as apprentices “was not accompanied with the old abuses” (on these “abuses” see Engels, cited above). He grants, though, that in one case there certainly was “abuse of this system in respect to a number of girls and young women brought from the agricultural districts of Scotland into Lancashire and Cheshire.” Under this system the manufacturer made a contract with the workhouse authorities for a certain period. He fed, clothed, and housed the children, and gave them a small allowance of money. A remark of Mr. Redgrave’s, which I will quote in a moment, seems strange. This is especially so if we consider that even among the years of prosperity of the English cotton trade, the year 1860 stands without equal, and that, besides, wages were exceptionally high. For this extraordinary demand for work had to contend with the depopulation of Ireland; with unexampled emigration from the English and Scottish agricultural districts to Australia and America; and with an actual fall in the population in some of the English agricultural districts. This fall was due partly to an actual breakdown of the workers’ vital force, and partly to the dispersal of the available population that the dealers in human flesh had already carried out. Despite all this Mr. Redgrave says: “This kind of labour, however, would only be sought after when none other could be procured, for it is a high-priced labour. The ordinary wages of a boy of 13 would be about 4s. per week, but to lodge, to clothe, to feed, and to provide medical attendance and proper superintendence for 50 or 100 of these boys, and to set aside some remuneration for them, could not be accomplished for 4s. a-head per week.” (Report of the Inspector of Factories for 30th April, 1860, p. 27.) Mr. Redgrave forgets to tell us how the worker himself can do all this for his children out of their 4s. a week in wages, when the manufacturer cannot do it for the 50 or 100 children who are housed, fed, and supervised all together. To guard against drawing false conclusions from the text, I should remark here that the English cotton industry, ever since it was placed under the Factory Act of 1850 with its regulation of labor-time, etc., must be regarded as the model industry of England. The English cotton factory worker is in every respect better off than his companion in misery on the Continent. “The Prussian factory operative labours at least ten hours per week more than his English competitor, and if employed at his own loom in his own house, his labour is not restricted to even those additional hours. (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact.,” 31st October, 1855, p. 103.) Redgrave, the Factory Inspector mentioned above, traveled on the Continent after the Industrial Exhibition in 1851, especially in France and Germany, to inquire into the conditions of the factories. Of the Prussian factory worker he says: “He receives a remuneration sufficient to procure the simple fare, and to supply the slender comforts to which he has been accustomed ... he lives upon his coarse fare, and works hard, wherein his position is subordinate to that of the English operative.” (“Rep. of Insp. of Fact.” 31st Oct., 1855, p. 85.)

What experience generally shows the capitalist is a constant excess of population, that is, an excess in relation to the needs of the moment of capital that absorbs surplus labor. This is so although the excess is made up of generations of human beings who are stunted, short-lived, and swiftly replacing each other, plucked, so to speak, before they are ripe.[^79] And indeed, experience shows the intelligent observer how swiftly and how firmly the capitalist mode of production, which historically speaking dates only from yesterday, has seized the vital power of the people by the very root. It shows how the degeneration of the industrial population is only slowed down by the constant absorption of primitive and physically uncorrupted elements from the country. It shows how even the country workers, in spite of fresh air and the principle of natural selection that works so powerfully among them and lets only the strongest survive, are already beginning to die off.[^80] Capital, which has such good reasons for denying the sufferings of the legions of workers that surround it, is in practice moved as much and as little by the prospect of the coming degradation and final depopulation of the human race as by the probable fall of the earth into the sun. In every stock-market swindle everyone knows that some time or other the crash must come. But everyone hopes that it will fall on the head of his neighbor, after he himself has caught the shower of gold and put it in a safe place. *Après moi le déluge!* [After me, the flood!] is the watchword of every capitalist and of every capitalist nation. Hence capital is reckless of the health or length of life of the worker, unless society compels it to care.[^81] To the outcry about physical and mental degradation, premature death, and the torture of overwork, it answers: Why should these trouble us, since they increase our profits? But taking things as a whole, all this does not, indeed, depend on the good or ill will of the individual capitalist. Free competition brings out the built-in laws of capitalist production in the form of external, coercive laws that have power over every individual capitalist.[^82]

In fact they resemble the 30,000 “gallant Highlanders” whom Glasgow packs together like pigs in its wynds and closes (narrow lanes and alleys), along with prostitutes and thieves.

[^82]: So we find, for example, that at the beginning of 1863, 26 firms owning large potteries in Staffordshire, among them Josiah Wedgwood, & Sons, petition in a memorial for “some legislative enactment.” Competition with other capitalists does not allow them to limit working time for children, etc., of their own free will. “Much as we deplore the evils before mentioned, it would not be possible to prevent them by any scheme of agreement between the manufacturers. ... Taking all these points into consideration, we have come to the conviction that some legislative enactment is wanted.” (“Children’s Employment Comm.” Rep. I, 1863, p. 322.) Most recently a much more striking example has turned up. During a period of feverish activity, the rise in the price of cotton had led the manufacturers in Blackburn to agree among themselves to shorten the working time in their mills for a certain fixed period. This period ended about the end of November, 1871. Meanwhile, the wealthier manufacturers, who combined spinning with weaving, used the fall in production that resulted from this agreement to expand their own business, and so made great profits at the expense of the small employers. The small employers, in their desperate straits, then turned to the factory workers, urged them earnestly to agitate for the 9 hours’ system, and promised money contributions to this end.
