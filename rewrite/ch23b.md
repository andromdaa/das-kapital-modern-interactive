<!-- ch23-p012 -->
When we dealt with the working day (the total hours a worker works in a day: necessary labor plus surplus labor), we saw that the worker is often forced to make his individual consumption a mere incident of production. In such a case, he supplies himself with necessaries in order to maintain his labor-power, just as coal and water are supplied to the steam-engine and oil to the wheel. His means of consumption are then merely the means of consumption required by a means of production. His individual consumption is directly productive consumption. This, however, appears to be an abuse that does not belong to the essence of capitalist production.[^8]

[^8]: Rossi would not protest so loudly against this, if he had really seen into the secret of “productive consumption.”

<!-- ch23-p013 -->
The matter looks quite different when we consider, not the single capitalist and the single worker, but the capitalist class and the working class; not an isolated process of production, but capitalist production in full swing and on its actual social scale. By turning part of his capital into labor-power, the capitalist increases the value of his entire capital. He kills two birds with one stone. He profits not only by what he receives from the worker, but also by what he gives to him. The capital given in exchange for labor-power is turned into necessaries. By consuming these, the existing workers reproduce their muscles, nerves, bones, and brains, and new workers are begotten. Within the limits of what is strictly necessary, therefore, the individual consumption of the working class turns the means of subsistence, which capital gave in exchange for labor-power, back into fresh labor-power that capital can exploit. It is the production and reproduction of that means of production so indispensable to the capitalist: the worker himself. So the individual consumption of the worker, whether it goes on inside the workshop or outside it, whether it is part of the process of production or not, is a factor in the production and reproduction of capital. In the same way, cleaning machinery is such a factor, whether it is done while the machinery is running or while it is standing still. The fact that the worker consumes his means of subsistence for his own purposes, and not to please the capitalist, makes no difference to the matter. A beast of burden's eating is no less a necessary factor in the process of production because the beast enjoys what it eats. The maintenance and reproduction of the working class is, and must always remain, a necessary condition for the reproduction of capital. But the capitalist may safely leave this to the worker's instincts of self-preservation and of propagation. All the capitalist cares about is to cut the worker's individual consumption down, as far as possible, to what is strictly necessary. He is far from imitating those brutal South Americans who force their workers to eat the more nourishing kind of food rather than the less nourishing kind.[^9]

[^9]: “The labourers in the mines of S. America, whose daily task (the heaviest perhaps in the world) consists in bringing to the surface on their shoulders a load of metal weighing from 180 to 200 pounds, from a depth of 450 feet, live on bread and beans only; they themselves would prefer the bread alone for food, but their masters, who have found out that the men cannot work so hard on bread, treat them like horses, and compel them to eat beans; beans, however, are relatively much richer in bone-earth (phosphate of lime) than is bread.” (Liebig, cited above, vol. 1., p. 194, note.)

<!-- ch23-p014 -->
Hence both the capitalist and his ideological representative, the political economist, count as productive only that part of the worker's individual consumption which is needed to perpetuate the class. That part must take place so that the capitalist may have labor-power to consume. Whatever the worker consumes for his own pleasure beyond that part is, in their view, unproductive consumption.[^10] If the accumulation of capital were to cause a rise of wages and an increase in the worker's consumption, without any increase in capital's consumption of labor-power, the additional capital would be consumed unproductively.[^11] In reality, the individual consumption of the worker is unproductive as far as he himself is concerned, for it reproduces nothing but the needy individual. It is productive for the capitalist and for the State, since it produces the power that creates their wealth.[^12]

[^10]: James Mill, cited above, p. 238

[^11]: “If the price of labour should rise so high that, notwithstanding the increase of capital, no more could be employed, I should say that such increase of capital would be still unproductively consumed.” (Ricardo, cited above, p. 163.)

[^12]: “The only productive consumption, properly so called, is the consumption or destruction of wealth” (he means the means of production) “by capitalists with a view to reproduction.... The workman ... is a productive consumer to the person who employs him, and to the State, but not, strictly speaking, to himself.” (Malthus’ “Definitions, &c.,” p. 30.)

<!-- ch23-p015 -->
From a social point of view, therefore, the working class, even when it is not directly engaged in the labor process, is just as much an appendage of capital as the ordinary instruments of labor are. Even its individual consumption is, within certain limits, a mere factor in the process of production. That process, however, takes good care that these self-conscious instruments do not leave it in the lurch. As fast as they make their product, it removes it from their pole to the opposite pole, that of capital. On the one hand, individual consumption provides the means for their maintenance and reproduction. On the other hand, by using up the necessaries of life, it makes sure that the worker keeps reappearing in the labor market. The Roman slave was held by chains; the wage worker is bound to his owner by invisible threads. The appearance of independence is kept up by a constant change of employers, and by the *fictio juris* [legal fiction] of a contract.

<!-- ch23-p016 -->
In earlier times, capital turned to legislation whenever necessary, to enforce its property rights over the free worker. For instance, down to 1815, mechanics employed in machine-making were forbidden to emigrate from England, under heavy pains and penalties.

<!-- ch23-p017 -->
The reproduction of the working class brings with it the accumulation of skill, which is handed down from one generation to the next.[^13] To what extent the capitalist counts the existence of such a skilled class among the factors of production that belong to him by right, and to what extent he actually regards it as the reality of his variable capital, becomes clear as soon as a crisis threatens him with its loss. As a result of the civil war in the United States and the cotton famine that came with it, most of the cotton factory workers in Lancashire were, as is well known, thrown out of work. Both from the working class itself and from other ranks of society, there arose a cry for State aid, or for voluntary national collections, so that the “superfluous” hands could emigrate to the colonies or to the United States. Thereupon *The Times* published, on the 24th March, 1863, a letter from Edmund Potter, a former president of the Manchester Chamber of Commerce. In the House of Commons this letter was rightly called the manufacturers' manifesto.[^14] We pick out here a few characteristic passages, in which the property rights of capital over labor-power are shamelessly asserted.

[^13]: “The only thing, of which one can say, that it is stored up and prepared beforehand, is the skill of the labourer.... The accumulation and storage of skilled labour, that most important operation, is, as regards the great mass of labourers, accomplished without any capital whatever.” (Th. Hodgskin: “Labour Defended, &c.,” p. 13.)

[^14]: “That letter might be looked upon as the manifesto of the manufacturers.” (Ferrand: “Motion on the Cotton Famine.” H.o.C., 27th April, 1863.)

<!-- ch23-p018 -->
> “He” (the man out of work) “may be told the supply of cotton-workers is too large ... and ... must ... in fact be reduced by a third, perhaps, and that then there will be a healthy demand for the remaining two-thirds.... Public opinion... urges emigration.... The master cannot willingly see his labour supply being removed; he may think, and perhaps justly, that it is both wrong and unsound.... But if the public funds are to be devoted to assist emigration, he has a right to be heard, and perhaps to protest.”

<!-- ch23-p019 -->
Mr. Potter then shows how useful the cotton trade is; how the “trade has undoubtedly drawn the surplus-population [the workers made ‘surplus’ to capital's needs for the time being] from Ireland and from the agricultural districts”; how immense its extent is; how in the year 1860 it yielded 5/13 ths of the total English exports; and how, after a few years, it will expand again, through the widening of the market, especially the Indian market, and by calling forth a plentiful supply of cotton at 6d. per lb. He then continues:

<!-- ch23-p020 -->
> “Some time ..., one, two, or three years, it may be, will produce the quantity.... The question I would put then is this – Is the trade worth retaining? Is it worth while to keep the machinery (he means the living labor machines) in order, and is it not the greatest folly to think of parting with that? I think it is. I allow that the workers are not a property, not the property of Lancashire and the masters; but they are the strength of both; they are the mental and trained power which cannot be replaced for a generation; the mere machinery which they work might much of it be beneficially replaced, nay improved, in a twelvemonth.[^15] Encourage or allow (!) the working-power to emigrate, and what of the capitalist?... Take away the cream of the workers, and fixed capital will depreciate in a great degree, and the floating will not subject itself to a struggle with the short supply of inferior labour.... We are told the workers wish it” (emigration). “Very natural it is that they should do so.... Reduce, compress the cotton trade by taking away its working power and reducing their wages expenditure, say one-fifth, or five millions, and what then would happen to the class above, the small shopkeepers; and what of the rents, the cottage rents.... Trace out the effects upwards to the small farmer, the better householder, and ... the landowner, and say if there could be any suggestion more suicidal to all classes of the country than by enfeebling a nation by exporting the best of its manufacturing population, and destroying the value of some of its most productive capital and enrichment .... I advise a loan (of five or six millions sterling), ... extending it may be over two or three years, administered by special commissioners added to the Boards of Guardians in the cotton districts, under special legislative regulations, enforcing some occupation or labour, as a means of keeping up at least the moral standard of the recipients of the loan... can anything be worse for landowners or masters than parting with the best of the workers, and demoralising and disappointing the rest by an extended depletive emigration, a depletion of capital and value in an entire province?”

[^15]: Let us not forget that this same capital sings quite a different song under ordinary circumstances, when the question is one of cutting wages. Then the employers cry out with one voice: “The factory operatives should keep in wholesome remembrance the fact that theirs is really a low species of skilled labour, and that there is none which is more easily acquired, or of its quality more amply remunerated, or which, by a short training of the least expert, can be more quickly, as well as abundantly, acquired ... The master’s machinery” (which, we now learn, can be replaced with advantage in 12 months) “really plays a far more important part in the business of production than the labour and skill of the operative” (who now cannot be replaced in under 30 years), “which six months’ education can reach, and a common labourer can learn.” (See earlier, p. 423.)

<!-- ch23-p021 -->
Potter, the chosen mouthpiece of the manufacturers, distinguishes two sorts of “machinery.” Each belongs to the capitalist. One stands in his factory; the other is housed outside the factory at night and on Sundays, in cottages. The one is lifeless, the other living. The lifeless machinery not only wears out and loses value from day to day, but a great part of it becomes outdated so quickly, through constant technical progress, that it can be replaced with advantage by new machinery after a few months. The living machinery, on the contrary, gets better the longer it lasts, and the more the skill handed down from one generation to the next accumulates. *The Times* answered the cotton lord as follows:

<!-- ch23-p022 -->
> “Mr. Edmund Potter is so impressed with the exceptional and supreme importance of the cotton masters that, in order to preserve this class and perpetuate their profession, he would keep half a million of the labouring class confined in a great moral workhouse against their will. ‘Is the trade worth retaining?’ asks Mr. Potter. ‘Certainly by all honest means it is,’ we answer. ‘Is it worth while keeping the machinery in order?’ again asks Mr. Potter. Here we hesitate. By the ‘machinery’ Mr. Potter means the human machinery, for he goes on to protest that he does not mean to use them as an absolute property. We must confess that we do not think it ‘worth while,’ or even possible, to keep the human machinery in order – that is to shut it up and keep it oiled till it is wanted. Human machinery will rust under inaction, oil and rub it as you may. Moreover, the human machinery will, as we have just seen, get the steam up of its own accord, and burst or run amuck in our great towns. It might, as Mr. Potter says, require some time to reproduce the workers, but, having machinists and capitalists at hand, we could always find thrifty, hard, industrious men wherewith to improvise more master manufacturers than we can ever want. Mr. Potter talks of the trade reviving ‘in one, two, or three years,’ and he asks us not ‘to encourage or allow (!) the working power to emigrate.’[^16] He says that it is very natural the workers should wish to emigrate; but he thinks that in spite of their desire, the nation ought to keep this half million of workers with their 700,000 dependents, shut up in the cotton districts; and as a necessary consequence, he must of course think that the nation ought to keep down their discontent by force, and sustain them by alms – and upon the chance that the cotton masters may some day want them.... The time is come when the great public opinion of these islands must operate to save this ‘working power’ from those who would deal with it as they would deal with iron, and coal, and cotton.”

[^16]: Parliament did not vote a single farthing in aid of emigration. It simply passed some Acts that empowered the municipal corporations to keep the factory workers in a half-starved state, that is, to exploit them at less than the normal wages. On the other hand, when the cattle disease broke out 3 years later, Parliament broke wildly with its customs and voted, straight off, millions to compensate the millionaire landlords, whose farmers in any case came off without loss, thanks to the rise in the price of meat. The bull-like bellowing of the landed proprietors at the opening of Parliament in 1866 showed that a man can worship the cow Sabala without being a Hindu, and can turn himself into an ox without being a Jupiter.

<!-- ch23-p023 -->
*The Times'* article was merely an exercise in wit. In fact, the “great public opinion” shared Mr. Potter's opinion that the factory workers are part of the movable fittings of a factory. Their emigration was prevented. They were locked up in that “moral workhouse,” the cotton districts, and they form, as before, “the strength” of the cotton manufacturers of Lancashire.

<!-- ch23-p024 -->
Capitalist production, therefore, reproduces, by itself, the separation between labor-power and the means of labor. In doing so, it reproduces and perpetuates the condition for exploiting the worker. It constantly forces him to sell his labor-power in order to live, and it enables the capitalist to buy labor-power in order to enrich himself.[^17] It is no longer a mere accident that capitalist and worker face each other in the market as buyer and seller. It is the process itself that constantly hurls the worker back onto the market as a seller of his labor-power, and that constantly turns his own product into a means by which another man can buy him. In reality, the worker belongs to capital before he has sold himself to capital. His economic bondage[^18] is both brought about and concealed by the periodic sale of himself, by his change of masters, and by the ups and downs in the market price of labor-power.[^19]

[^17]: “L’ouvrier demandait de la subsistence pour vivre, le chef demandait du travail pour gagner.” [The worker asked for the means of subsistence in order to live; the boss asked for labor in order to make a profit] (Sismondi, cited above, p. 91.)

[^18]: A crudely clumsy form of this bondage exists in the county of Durham. This is one of the few counties where circumstances do not guarantee the farmer undisputed property rights over the farm laborer. The mining industry gives the laborer some choice. In this county, unlike elsewhere, the farmer rents only farms that have laborers' cottages on them. The rent of the cottage is part of the wages. These cottages are known as “hinds’ houses.” They are let to the laborers in return for certain feudal services, under a contract called “bondage.” Among other things, this contract binds the laborer, during the time he is employed elsewhere, to leave someone, say his daughter, etc., to take his place. The laborer himself is called a “bondsman.” This relationship also shows, from quite a new angle, how the worker's individual consumption becomes consumption on behalf of capital ‒ or productive consumption: “It is curious to observe that the very dung of the hind and bondsman is the perquisite [perk] of the calculating lord ... and the lord will allow no privy [outdoor toilet] but his own to exist in the neighbourhood, and will rather give a bit of manure here and there for a garden than bate [give up] any part of his seigneurial right.” (“Public Health, Report VII., 1864,” p. 188.)

[^19]: Let us not forget that, in the case of the labor of children, etc., even the formality of a voluntary sale disappears.

<!-- ch23-p025 -->
Capitalist production, therefore, seen as a continuous connected process, as a process of reproduction, produces not only commodities, not only surplus-value. It also produces and reproduces the capitalist relation: on the one side the capitalist, on the other the wage worker.[^20]

[^20]: “Capital pre-supposes wage labour, and wage labour pre-supposes capital. One is a necessary condition to the existence of the other; they mutually call each other into existence. Does an operative in a cotton-factory produce nothing but cotton goods? No, he produces capital. He produces values that give fresh command over his labour, and that, by means of such command, create fresh values.” (Karl Marx: “Lohnarbeit und Kapital,” in the *Neue Rheinische Zeitung*: No. 266, 7th April, 1849.) The articles published under that title in the *N. Rh. Z.* are parts of some lectures I gave on that subject in 1847, in the German “Arbeiter-Verein” [Workers' Association] at Brussels. Their publication was interrupted by the February revolution.
