### Section 4: Division of Labor in Manufacture, and Division of Labor in Society

<!-- ch14-p029 -->
We first looked at the origin of manufacture, then at its simple elements, then at the detail worker and his tools, and finally at the mechanism as a whole. We will now touch briefly on the relation between the division of labor in manufacture and the social division of labor, which forms the foundation of all production of commodities.

<!-- ch14-p030 -->
If we keep only labor in view, we may call the separation of social production into its main divisions or *genera* [kinds] – namely agriculture, industries, etc. – division of labor in general. We may call the splitting up of these families into species and sub-species division of labor in particular. And we may call the division of labor within the workshop division of labor in singular, or in detail.[^25]

[^25]: “Division of labour proceeds from the separation of professions the most widely different to that division, where several labourers divide between them the preparation of one and the same product, as in manufacture.” (Storch: “Cours d’Econ. Pol.,” Paris Edn. t. I., p. 173.) “Nous rencontrons chez les peuples parvenus à un certain degré de civilisation trois genres de divisions d’industrie: la première, que nous nommerons générale, amène la distinction des producteurs en agriculteurs, manufacturiers et commerçants, elle se rapporte aux trois principales branches d’industrie nationale; la seconde qu’on pourrait appeler spéciale, est la division de chaque genre d’industrie en espèces ... la troisième division d’industrie, celle enfin qu’on devrait qualifier de division de la besogne on de travail proprement dit, est celle qui s’établit dans les arts et les métiers séparés ... qui s’établit dans la plupart des manufactures et des ateliers.” [Among peoples who have reached a certain level of civilization, we find three kinds of division of labor. The first, which we will call general, divides the producers into farmers, manufacturers, and traders; it corresponds to the three main branches of the nation's labor. The second, which one could call particular, is the division of each branch of labor into species. ... The third division of labor, which one should call a division of tasks, or of labor in the strict sense, is the one that grows up in the individual crafts and trades ... which is established in most manufacturing establishments and workshops.] (Skarbek, cited above, pp. 84, 85.)

<!-- ch14-p031 -->
Division of labor in a society, and the tying down of individuals to a particular occupation that goes with it, develops from opposite starting-points, just as the division of labor in manufacture does. Within a family,[^26] and after further development within a tribe, a division of labor springs up naturally. It is caused by differences of sex and age, and so rests on a purely physiological foundation. This division widens its material as the community expands and the population grows, and above all through conflicts between different tribes and the conquest of one tribe by another. On the other hand, as I have remarked before, the exchange of products springs up at the points where different families, tribes, and communities come into contact. For at the beginning of civilization it is not private individuals but families, tribes, etc., that meet on an independent footing. Different communities find different means of production and different means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) in their natural surroundings. So their modes of production and of living, and their products, are different. It is this spontaneously developed difference that, when different communities come into contact, calls forth the mutual exchange of products, and so the gradual conversion of those products into commodities. Exchange does not create the differences between the spheres of production. It brings spheres that are already different into relation with each other, and so turns them into more or less interdependent branches of the collective production of an enlarged society. In this second case, the social division of labor arises from exchange between spheres of production that were originally distinct and independent of one another. In the first case, where the physiological division of labor is the starting-point, the particular organs of a compact whole grow loose and break off, mainly because of the exchange of commodities with foreign communities. They then become so isolated from one another that the only bond still connecting the various kinds of work is the exchange of the products as commodities. In the one case, what was independent before is made dependent; in the other, what was dependent before is made independent.

[^26]: *Note to the third edition.* Later, very searching study of the primitive condition of humanity led the author to the conclusion that it was not the family that originally developed into the tribe. On the contrary, the tribe was the primitive and spontaneously developed form of human association, based on blood relationship. The many and various forms of the family developed afterwards, out of the first beginnings of the loosening of tribal bonds. [F. E.]

The foundation of every division of labor that is well developed and brought about by the exchange of commodities is the separation between town and country.[^27] It may be said that the whole economic history of society is summed up in the movement of this opposition. For the present, however, we pass it over.

[^27]: Sir James Steuart is the economist who has handled this subject best. His book appeared ten years before the “Wealth of Nations.” How little it is known, even today, can be judged from this fact: the admirers of Malthus do not even know that the first edition of Malthus's work on population contains, apart from its purely declamatory part, very little but extracts from Steuart, and to a lesser degree from Wallace and Townsend.

A certain number of workers employed at the same time are the material precondition for division of labor in manufacture. In the same way, the number and density of the population, which here correspond to the crowding together in one workshop, are a necessary condition for the division of labor in society.[^28] This density, however, is more or less relative. A relatively thinly populated country with well-developed means of communication has a denser population than a more numerously populated country with badly developed means of communication. In this sense the Northern States of the American Union, for instance, are more thickly populated than India.[^29]

[^28]: “There is a certain density of population which is convenient, both for social intercourse, and for that combination of powers by which the produce of labour is increased.” (James Mill, cited above, p. 50.) “As the number of labourers increases, the productive power of society augments in the compound ratio of that increase, multiplied by the effects of the division of labour.” (Th. Hodgskin, cited above, pp. 125, 126.)

[^29]: Because of the great demand for cotton after 1861, cotton production in some thickly populated districts of India was extended at the expense of rice growing. Local famines followed, because the poor means of communication did not allow a failure of rice in one district to be made up by bringing rice in from another.

The production and the circulation of commodities (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying) are the general preconditions of the capitalist mode of production. So division of labor in manufacture requires that division of labor in society at large should already have reached a certain degree of development. The other way round, division of labor in manufacture reacts on division of labor in society, and develops and multiplies it. As the instruments of labor become more differentiated, so, at the same time, do the industries that produce these instruments.[^30] Suppose the manufacturing system seizes on an industry that was previously carried on by one producer together with other industries, either as the chief or as a subordinate industry. These industries then immediately break their connection and become independent. If it seizes on a particular stage in the production of a commodity, the other stages of its production become so many independent industries. It has already been stated that where the finished article is merely a number of parts fitted together, the detail operations may re-establish themselves as genuine and separate handicrafts. To carry out the division of labor in manufacture more perfectly, a single branch of production is split up into numerous, and to some extent entirely new, branches of manufacture. The split follows the varieties of its raw material, or the various forms that one and the same raw material may take. So in France alone, in the first half of the 18th century, over 100 different kinds of silk fabrics were woven, and in Avignon it was law that “every apprentice should devote himself to only one sort of fabrication, and should not learn the preparation of several kinds of stuff at once.” The territorial division of labor, which confines special branches of production to special districts of a country, gets a fresh stimulus from the manufacturing system, which exploits every special advantage.[^31] The colonial system and the opening up of the world's markets, both of which belong to the general conditions of existence of the manufacturing period, supply rich material for developing the division of labor in society. This is not the place to go on to show how division of labor seizes not only the economic sphere but every other sphere of society. Everywhere it lays the foundation of that all-absorbing system of specializing and sorting people, that development in a person of one single ability at the expense of all the others, which made A. Ferguson, Adam Smith's teacher, exclaim: “We make a nation of Helots [state serfs of ancient Sparta], and have no free citizens.”[^32]

[^30]: So as early as the 17th century, the making of shuttles formed a special branch of industry in Holland.

[^32]: A. Ferguson: “History of Civil Society.” Edinburgh, 1767; Part iv, sect. ii., p. 285.

But in spite of the many analogies and links between them, division of labor inside a society and division of labor inside a workshop differ not only in degree but also in kind. The analogy seems most beyond dispute where an invisible bond unites the various branches of trade. For instance, the cattle-breeder produces hides, the tanner makes the hides into leather, and the shoemaker makes the leather into boots. Here what each of them produces is only a step toward the final form, which is the product of all their labors combined. Besides these, there are all the various industries that supply the cattle-breeder, the tanner, and the shoemaker with means of production. Now it is quite possible to imagine, with Adam Smith, that the difference between this social division of labor and the division in manufacture is merely subjective. On this view it exists only for the observer: in a manufacturing workshop he can see at one glance all the many operations being carried out in one place, while in the example above, the spreading of the work over great areas and the great number of people employed in each branch of labor hide the connection.[^33] But what forms the bond between the independent labors of the cattle-breeder, the tanner, and the shoemaker? The fact that their products are commodities. What, on the other hand, is characteristic of division of labor in manufacture? The fact that the detail worker produces no commodities.[^34] Only the common product of all the detail workers becomes a commodity.[^35] Division of labor in society comes about through the purchase and sale of the products of different branches of industry. The connection between the detail operations in a workshop, by contrast, comes from the sale of the labor-power of several workers to one capitalist, who applies it as combined labor-power. The division of labor in the workshop implies that the means of production are concentrated in the hands of one capitalist. The division of labor in society implies that they are dispersed among many independent producers of commodities. Inside the workshop, the iron law of proportionality subjects definite numbers of workers to definite functions. In the society outside the workshop, chance and whim have full play in distributing the producers and their means of production among the various branches of industry. It is true that the different spheres of production constantly tend toward an equilibrium. On the one hand, each producer of a commodity is bound to produce a use-value (the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties), to satisfy a particular social want; and while the extent of these wants differs in quantity, there is still an inner relation that settles their proportions into a regular system, and a system of spontaneous growth at that. On the other hand, the law of the value of commodities ultimately determines how much of its available working time society can spend on each particular class of commodities. But this constant tendency of the various spheres of production toward equilibrium works only as a reaction against the constant upsetting of that equilibrium. Within the workshop, the division of labor is carried out regularly according to a plan fixed in advance. Within society, this becomes a necessity imposed by nature after the fact, which controls the lawless whims of the producers and can be seen in the barometer-like fluctuations of market prices. Division of labor within the workshop implies the undisputed authority of the capitalist over people who are only parts of a mechanism that belongs to him. The division of labor within society brings into contact independent commodity producers who acknowledge no authority but that of competition, the coercion exerted by the pressure of their mutual interests – just as in the animal kingdom the *bellum omnium contra omnes* [war of all against all – Hobbes] more or less preserves the conditions of existence of every species. The same bourgeois mind (bourgeois means belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it) praises division of labor in the workshop, the life-long annexation of the worker to a partial operation, and his complete subjection to capital, as an organization of labor that increases its productivity. That same bourgeois mind denounces just as vigorously every conscious attempt to control and regulate the process of production socially, as an attack on such sacred things as the rights of property, freedom, and unrestricted play for the inclinations of the individual capitalist. It is very characteristic that the enthusiastic apologists of the factory system – production in which a system of machines, driven by a central power source, does the work and workers attend the machines – have nothing more damning to say against a general organization of the labor of society than that it would turn all society into one immense factory.

If, in a society with capitalist production, anarchy in the social division of labor and despotism in the division of labor in the workshop are each a condition of the other, we find the opposite in those earlier forms of society in which the separation of trades developed spontaneously, then hardened, and was finally made permanent by law. There we find, on the one hand, an example of the labor of society organized according to an approved and authoritative plan, and on the other, the complete exclusion of division of labor in the workshop, or at any rate only a stunted, or sporadic and accidental, development of it.[^36]

[^36]: “On peut... établir en règle générale, que moins l’autorité préside à la division du travail dans l’intérieur de la société, plus la division du travail se développe dans l’intérieur de l’atelier, et plus elle y est soumise à l’autorité d’un seul. Ainsi l’autorité dans l’atelier et celle dans la société, par rapport à la division du travail, sont en raison inverse l’une de l’autre.” [It can ... be laid down as a general rule that the less authority presides over the division of labor inside society, the more the division of labor develops inside the workshop, and the more it is subject there to the authority of a single person. So authority in the workshop and authority in society, in relation to the division of labor, are in inverse ratio to each other.] (Karl Marx, “Misère,” etc., pp. 130-131.)

Those small and extremely ancient Indian communities, some of which have lasted down to this day, are based on holding the land in common, on combining agriculture and handicrafts, and on an unalterable division of labor, which serves as a ready-made plan and scheme whenever a new community is started. Each one covers an area of from 100 up to several thousand acres and forms a compact whole that produces everything it needs. Most of the products are meant for direct use by the community itself and do not take the form of a commodity. So production here is independent of the division of labor that is brought about in Indian society as a whole by the exchange of commodities. Only the surplus becomes a commodity, and even a part of that only once it has reached the hands of the State. From time immemorial a certain quantity of these products has found its way into the State's hands as rent paid in kind. The constitution of these communities varies in different parts of India. In those of the simplest form, the land is farmed in common and the produce is divided among the members. At the same time, spinning and weaving are carried on in each family as side industries. Side by side with the masses all occupied with this same work, we find the “chief inhabitant,” who is judge, police, and tax-collector in one; the bookkeeper, who keeps the accounts of the farming and records everything connected with it; another official, who prosecutes criminals, protects strangers traveling through, and escorts them to the next village; the boundary man, who guards the boundaries against neighboring communities; the water-overseer, who distributes water for irrigation from the common tanks; the Brahmin, who conducts the religious services; the schoolmaster, who teaches the children reading and writing in the sand; the calendar-Brahmin, or astrologer, who announces the lucky or unlucky days for sowing and harvest and for every other kind of farm work; a smith and a carpenter, who make and repair all the farm implements; the potter, who makes all the pottery of the village; the barber; the washerman, who washes clothes; the silversmith; and here and there the poet, who in some communities replaces the silversmith and in others the schoolmaster. This dozen individuals is kept at the expense of the whole community. If the population increases, a new community is founded on unoccupied land, on the pattern of the old one. The whole mechanism shows a systematic division of labor. But a division like that in manufacture is impossible, since the smith, the carpenter, etc., find an unchanging market, and at most there are, depending on the size of the villages, two or three of each instead of one.[^37] The law that regulates the division of labor in the community acts with the irresistible authority of a law of Nature. At the same time, each individual craftsman, the smith, the carpenter, and so on, carries out all the operations of his handicraft in his workshop in the traditional way, but independently, and without recognizing any authority over him. Take the simplicity of the organization for production in these self-sufficient communities, which constantly reproduce themselves in the same form and, when accidentally destroyed, spring up again on the same spot and with the same name[^38] – this simplicity supplies the key to the secret of the unchangeableness of Asiatic societies. That unchangeableness stands in striking contrast with the constant breaking up and refounding of Asiatic States and the never-ending changes of dynasty. The structure of the economic elements of society remains untouched by the storm-clouds of the political sky.

[^37]: Lieut.-Col. Mark Wilks: “Historical Sketches of the South of India.” London, 1810-17, v. I., pp. 118-20. A good description of the various forms of the Indian communities can be found in George Campbell's “Modern India.” London, 1852.

[^38]: “Under this simple form ... the inhabitants of the country have lived from time immemorial. The boundaries of the villages have been but seldom altered; and though the villages themselves have been sometimes injured, and even desolated by war, famine, and disease, the same name, the same limits, the same interests, and even the same families, have continued for ages. The rules of the guilds, as I have said before, strictly limited the number of apprentices and journeymen that a single master could employ, and so prevented him from becoming a capitalist. Besides, he could not employ his journeymen in many handicrafts other than the one in which he was a master. The guilds eagerly fought off every encroachment by merchants' capital, the only form of free capital they came into contact with. A merchant could buy every kind of commodity, but he could not buy labor as a commodity. He was only tolerated, as a dealer in the products of the handicrafts. If circumstances called for a further division of labor, the existing guilds split into varieties, or founded new guilds alongside the old ones; but they did all this without concentrating various handicrafts in a single workshop. So the guild organization, however much it may have helped to create the material conditions for the existence of manufacture by separating, isolating, and perfecting the handicrafts, excluded division of labor in the workshop. On the whole, the worker and his means of production stayed closely united, like the snail and its shell. So the principal basis of manufacture was missing: the separation of the worker from his means of production, and the conversion of these means into capital.

<!-- ch14-p039 -->
Division of labor in society at large, whether or not it is brought about by the exchange of commodities, is common to the most diverse economic formations of society. But division of labor in the workshop, as practiced by manufacture, is a special creation of the capitalist mode of production alone.
