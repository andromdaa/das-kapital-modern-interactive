## Chapter 3: Money, Or the Circulation of Commodities

### Section 1: The Measure of Values

<!-- ch03-p001 -->
Throughout this work, for the sake of simplicity, I assume that gold is the money-commodity. (A commodity is a thing produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer's own use.)

<!-- ch03-p002 -->
The first main function of money is to supply commodities with the material for expressing their values. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time.) Or, put another way, its function is to represent their values as magnitudes of the same denomination: equal in quality and comparable in quantity. So money serves as a *universal measure of value*. And only through this function does gold, the equivalent commodity in the fullest sense, become money.

<!-- ch03-p003 -->
It is not money that makes commodities commensurable, that is, measurable by a common standard. Just the opposite. All commodities, as values, are realized human labor, and therefore commensurable. That is why their values can be measured by one and the same special commodity, and why this commodity can be turned into the common measure of their values, that is, into money. Money as a measure of value is the form of appearance that must necessarily be taken by the measure of value built into commodities: labor-time.[^1]

[^1]: The question, why does money not directly represent labor-time, so that a piece of paper might represent, for instance, x hours' labor, is at bottom the same as the question, why, given the production of commodities, must products take the form of commodities? This is clear, since their taking the form of commodities implies that they split into commodities and money. Or: why can private labor (labor for the account of private individuals) not be treated as its opposite, directly social labor? I have elsewhere examined thoroughly the Utopian idea of “labour-money” in a society founded on the production of commodities (cited above, p. 61, and following). On this point I will only add that Owen's “labour-money,” for instance, is no more “money” than a theater ticket is. Owen presupposes directly associated labor, a form of production that is entirely inconsistent with the production of commodities. The certificate of labor is merely evidence of the individual's share in the common labor, and of his right to a certain portion of the common product set aside for consumption. But it never enters Owen's head to presuppose the production of commodities and at the same time, by juggling with money, to try to dodge the necessary conditions of that production.

<!-- ch03-p004 -->
The expression of the value of a commodity in gold, *x* commodity A = *y* money-commodity, is its money form or price. A single equation, such as 1 ton of iron = 2 ounces of gold, is now enough to express the value of the iron in a socially valid way. This equation no longer needs to appear as one link in the chain of equations that express the values of all other commodities, because the equivalent commodity, gold, now has the character of money. The general form of relative value has gone back to its original shape of simple or isolated relative value. On the other hand, the expanded expression of relative value, the endless series of equations, has now become the form peculiar to the relative value of the money-commodity. The series itself, too, is now given, and is socially recognized in the prices of actual commodities. We have only to read the quotations of a price-list backwards to find the magnitude of the value of money expressed in all sorts of commodities. (The magnitude of value is how much value a commodity has, measured by the socially necessary labor-time needed to produce it.) But money itself has no price. To put it on an equal footing with all other commodities in this respect, we would have to equate it to itself as its own equivalent.

<!-- ch03-p005 -->
The price or money form of commodities is, like their form of value in general, a form quite distinct from their tangible bodily form. (The form of value is the way the value of one commodity is expressed in another commodity.) It is, therefore, a purely ideal or mental form. The value of iron, linen and grain is invisible, but it actually exists in these very articles. It is made perceptible, in an ideal way, by their equality with gold, a relation that, so to speak, exists only in their own heads. So their owner must lend them his tongue, or hang a ticket on them, before their prices can be communicated to the outside world.[^2] Since expressing the value of commodities in gold is a merely ideal act, we may use imaginary or ideal money for this purpose. Every trader knows that he is far from having turned his goods into money when he has expressed their value in a price, or in imaginary money. He also knows that it does not take the least bit of real gold to estimate millions of pounds' worth of goods in that metal. So when money serves as a measure of value, it is used only as imaginary or ideal money. This fact has given rise to the wildest theories.[^3] But although the money that performs the functions of a measure of value is only ideal money, price depends entirely on the actual substance that is money. The value of a ton of iron, in other words the quantity of human labor contained in it, is expressed in imagination by a quantity of the money-commodity that contains the same amount of labor as the iron. So, depending on whether the measure of value is gold, silver, or copper, the value of the ton of iron will be expressed by very different prices, or represented by very different quantities of those metals.

[^2]: Savages and half-civilized peoples use the tongue differently. Captain Parry says of the inhabitants of the west coast of Baffin's Bay: “In this case (he refers to barter) they licked it (the thing represented to them) twice to their tongues, after which they seemed to consider the bargain satisfactorily concluded.” In the same way, the Eastern Inuit licked the articles they received in exchange. If the tongue is used like this in the North as the organ of appropriation (of taking possession), no wonder that in the South the stomach serves as the organ of accumulated property, and that a Bantu-speaking southern African judges a man's wealth by the size of his belly. That these southern Africans know what they are about is shown by the following. At the same time as the official British Health Report of 1864 revealed the lack of fat-forming food among a large part of the working class, a certain Dr. Harvey (not, however, the famous discoverer of the circulation of the blood) did very well for himself by advertising recipes for reducing the excess fat of the bourgeoisie (the capitalist class) and the aristocracy.

[^3]: See Karl Marx: “Zur Kritik, &c.” “Theorien von der Masseinheit des Geldes,” p. 53, and following.

<!-- ch03-p006 -->
So if two different commodities, such as gold and silver, are measures of value at the same time, all commodities have two prices: one a gold-price, the other a silver-price. These exist quietly side by side as long as the ratio of the value of silver to that of gold stays unchanged, say at 15:1. Every change in that ratio disturbs the ratio between the gold-prices and the silver-prices of commodities. It thus proves, by the facts, that a double standard of value is inconsistent with the functions of a standard.[^4]

[^4]: “Wherever gold and silver have by law been made to perform the function of money or of a measure of value side by side, it has always been tried, but in vain, to treat them as one and the same material. To assume that there is an invariable ratio between the quantities of gold and silver in which a given quantity of labour-time is incorporated, is to assume in fact that gold and silver are of one and the same material, and that a given mass of the less valuable metal, silver, is a constant fraction of a given mass of gold. From the reign of Edward III. to the time of George II., the history of money in England consists of one long series of perturbations caused by the clashing of the legally fixed ratio between the values of gold and silver with the fluctuations in their real values. At one time gold was too high, at another, silver. The metal that for the time being was estimated below its value was withdrawn from circulation [the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying], melted and exported. The ratio between the two metals was then again altered by law, but the new nominal ratio soon came into conflict again with the real one. In our own times, the slight and transient fall in the value of gold compared with silver, which was a consequence of the Indo-Chinese demand for silver, produced on a far more extended scale in France the same phenomena: export of silver, and its expulsion from circulation by gold. During the years 1855, 1856 and 1857, the excess in France of gold-imports over gold-exports amounted to £41,580,000, while the excess of silver-exports over silver-imports was £14,704,000. In fact, in those countries in which both metals are legally measures of value, and therefore both legal tender, so that everyone has the option of paying in either metal, the metal that rises in value is at a premium, and, like every other commodity, measures its price in the over-estimated metal, which alone serves in reality as the standard of value. The result of all experience and history with regard to this equation is simply that, where two commodities perform by law the functions of a measure of value, in practice one alone maintains that position.” (Karl Marx, cited above, pp. 52, 53.)

<!-- ch03-p007 -->
Commodities with definite prices appear in this form: *a* commodity A = x gold; *b* commodity B = z gold; *c* commodity C = y gold, etc. Here *a*, *b*, *c* stand for definite quantities of the commodities A, B, C, and x, z, y for definite quantities of gold. So the values of these commodities are changed in imagination into so many different quantities of gold. Hence, despite the confusing variety of the commodities themselves, their values become magnitudes of the same denomination: gold-magnitudes. They can now be compared with each other and measured. And the need is felt, as a technical matter, to compare them with some fixed quantity of gold as a unit of measure. This unit, by being further divided into equal fractions, itself becomes the standard or scale. Even before they become money, gold, silver, and copper already have such standard measures in their standards of weight. For example, a pound weight serves as the unit; on the one hand it can be divided into ounces, and on the other it can be combined to make up hundredweights.[^5] This is why, in all metallic currencies, the names given to the standards of money or of price were originally taken from the already existing names of the standards of weight.

[^5]: The peculiar fact that in England the ounce of gold serves as the unit of the standard of money, while the pound sterling does not form an equal fraction of it, has been explained as follows: “Our coinage was originally adapted to the employment of silver only, hence, an ounce of silver can always be divided into a certain adequate number of pieces of coin, but as gold was introduced at a later period into a coinage adapted only to silver, an ounce of gold cannot be coined into an aliquot [evenly dividing] number of pieces.” Maclaren, “A Sketch of the History of the Currency.” London, 1858, p. 16.

<!-- ch03-p008 -->
As *measure of Value* and as *standard of price*, money has two entirely distinct functions to perform. It is the measure of value insofar as it is the socially recognized incarnation of human labor. It is the standard of price insofar as it is a fixed weight of metal. As the measure of value, it serves to turn the values of all the many different commodities into prices, into imaginary quantities of gold. As the standard of price, it measures those quantities of gold. The measure of values measures commodities considered as values. The standard of price, on the contrary, measures quantities of gold by a unit quantity of gold, not the value of one quantity of gold by the weight of another. To make gold a standard of price, a certain weight must be fixed on as the unit. In this case, as in all cases of measuring quantities of the same denomination, establishing an unvarying unit of measure is all-important. Hence the less the unit varies, the better the standard of price does its job. But gold can serve as a measure of value only insofar as it is itself a product of labor, and therefore potentially variable in value.[^6]

[^6]: Among English writers the confusion between measure of value and standard of price (standard of value) is indescribable. Their functions, as well as their names, are constantly mixed up.

<!-- ch03-p009 -->
In the first place, it is quite clear that a change in the value of gold does not in any way affect its function as a standard of price. No matter how this value varies, the proportions between the values of different quantities of the metal stay constant. However far its value falls, 12 ounces of gold still have 12 times the value of 1 ounce; and in prices, the only thing considered is the relation between different quantities of gold. On the other hand, since no rise or fall in the value of an ounce of gold can change its weight, the weight of its equal fractions cannot change either. So gold always does the same service as an unvarying standard of price, however much its value may vary.

<!-- ch03-p010 -->
In the second place, a change in the value of gold does not interfere with its functions as a measure of value. The change affects all commodities at the same time. So, other things being equal, it leaves their relative values among themselves unchanged, although those values are now expressed in higher or lower gold-prices.

<!-- ch03-p011 -->
We may estimate the value of any commodity by a definite quantity of the use-value of some other commodity. (Use-value is the usefulness of a thing: what it can be used for, which comes from its physical properties.) Just as when we do that, so when we estimate the first commodity's value in gold, we assume nothing more than that producing a given quantity of gold costs a given amount of labor at the given period. As for the fluctuations of prices in general, they are subject to the laws of elementary relative value examined in an earlier chapter.

<!-- ch03-p012 -->
A general rise in the prices of commodities can result only from one of two things: either from a rise in their values, while the value of money stays constant, or from a fall in the value of money, while the values of commodities stay constant. On the other hand, a general fall in prices can result only either from a fall in the values of commodities, while the value of money stays constant, or from a rise in the value of money, while the values of commodities stay constant. So it by no means follows that a rise in the value of money necessarily implies a proportional fall in the prices of commodities, or that a fall in the value of money implies a proportional rise in prices. Such a change of price holds good only for commodities whose value stays constant. With those, for example, whose value rises at the same time as, and in proportion to, that of money, there is no change in price. And if their value rises either more slowly or faster than that of money, the fall or rise in their prices will be determined by the difference between the change in their value and the change in that of money; and so on.

<!-- ch03-p013 -->
Let us now go back to examining the price form.

<!-- ch03-p014 -->
Gradually a gap arises between the current money-names of the various weights of the precious metal that serves as money, and the actual weights those names originally stood for. This gap is the result of historical causes, the chief of which are: (1) The import of foreign money into a community that is not fully developed. This happened in Rome in its early days, where gold and silver coins at first circulated as foreign commodities. The names of these foreign coins never match those of the native weights. (2) As wealth increases, the less precious metal is pushed out of its place as a measure of value by the more precious one: copper by silver, silver by gold, however much this order of sequence may contradict poetical chronology.[^7] The word pound, for instance, was the money-name given to an actual pound weight of silver. When gold replaced silver as a measure of value, the same name was applied, according to the ratio between the values of silver and gold, to perhaps 1-15th of a pound of gold. So the word pound, as a money-name, becomes distinct from the same word as a weight-name.[^8] (3) The debasing of money, carried on for centuries by kings and princes, to such an extent that nothing in fact remained of the original weights of the coins but the names.[^9]

[^7]: Moreover, it is not valid for history in general.

[^8]: This is how the pound sterling in English stands for less than one-third of its original weight; the pound Scot, before the union, only 1-36th; the French livre, 1-74th; the Spanish maravedi, less than 1-1,000th; and the Portuguese rei a still smaller fraction.

[^9]: “Le monete le quali oggi sono ideal, sono le piû antiche d’ogni nazione, e tutte furono un tempo real, e perche erano reali con esse si contava” [“The coins that are ideal today are the oldest coins of every nation, and all of them were once real, and because they were real, people counted with them”] (Galiani: Della moneta, cited above, p. 153.)

<!-- ch03-p015 -->
These historical causes turn the separation of the money-name from the weight-name into an established habit in the community. The standard of money is, on the one hand, purely conventional, and on the other hand it must be generally accepted. So in the end it is regulated by law. A given weight of one of the precious metals, an ounce of gold, for instance, is officially divided into equal fractions, which are given legal names such as pound, dollar, etc. These equal fractions, which from then on serve as units of money, are then subdivided into other equal fractions with legal names, such as shilling, penny, etc.[^10] But both before and after these divisions are made, a definite weight of metal is the standard of metallic money. The only change is in the subdivision and the naming.

[^10]: David Urquhart, in his “Familiar Words,” remarks on the monstrosity (!) that nowadays a pound (sterling), which is the unit of the English standard of money, equals about a quarter of an ounce of gold. “This is falsifying a measure, not establishing a standard.” In this “false denomination” of the weight of gold he sees, as in everything else, the falsifying hand of civilization.

<!-- ch03-p016 -->
So the prices, or quantities of gold, into which the values of commodities are ideally changed, are now expressed in the names of coins, or in the legally valid names of the subdivisions of the gold standard. Hence, instead of saying: A quarter of wheat is worth an ounce of gold, we say: it is worth £3 17s. 10 1/2d. In this way commodities express through their prices how much they are worth, and money serves as *money of account* whenever the value of an article has to be fixed in its money form.[^11]

[^11]: When Anacharsis was asked what the Greeks used money for, he replied, “For reckoning.” (Ashen. Deipn. 1. iv. 49 v. 2. ed. Schweighauser, 1802.)

<!-- ch03-p017 -->
The name of a thing is something distinct from the qualities of that thing. I know nothing about a man by knowing that his name is Jacob. In the same way, with money, every trace of a value-relation disappears in the names pound, dollar, franc, ducat, etc. Attributing a hidden meaning to these cabalistic (secret, magical) signs causes confusion. The confusion is all the greater because these money-names express both the values of commodities and, at the same time, equal fractions of the weight of the metal that is the standard of money.[^12] On the other hand, it is absolutely necessary that value, in order to be distinguished from the varied bodily forms of commodities, should take on this material and meaningless, but at the same time purely social, form.[^13]

[^12]: “Owing to the fact that money, when serving as the standard of price, appears under the same reckoning names as do the prices of commodities, and that therefore the sum of £3 17s. 10 1/2d. may signify on the one hand an ounce weight of gold, and on the other, the value of a ton of iron, this reckoning name of money has been called its mint-price. Hence there sprang up the extraordinary notion that the value of gold is estimated in its own material, and that, in contradistinction to all other commodities, its price is fixed by the State. It was erroneously thought that the giving of reckoning names to definite weights of gold is the same thing as fixing the value of those weights.” (Karl Marx, cited above, p. 52.)

[^13]: See “Theorien von der Masseinheit des Geldes” in “Zur Kritik der Pol Oekon. &c.,” p. 53, and following. There are fantastic notions about raising or lowering the mint-price of money by transferring the names already legally assigned to fixed weights of gold or silver to greater or smaller weights of those metals. Such notions, at least in those cases where they aim not at clumsy financial operations against creditors, both public and private, but at economic quack remedies, were treated so exhaustively by Wm. Petty in his “Quantulumcunque concerning money: To the Lord Marquis of Halifax, 1682,” that even his immediate followers, Sir Dudley North and John Locke, not to mention later ones, could only water him down. “If the wealth of a nation,” he remarks, “could be decupled [multiplied tenfold] by a proclamation, it were strange that such proclamations have not long since been made by our Governors.” (cited above, p. 36.)

<!-- ch03-p018 -->
Price is the money-name of the labor realized in a commodity. Hence to express the equivalence of a commodity with the sum of money that makes up its price is a tautology, a statement that says the same thing twice[^14], just as, in general, to express the relative value of a commodity is to state the equivalence of two commodities. Price, as the exponent (the indicator) of the magnitude of a commodity's value, is the exponent of its exchange-ratio with money. But although this is so, it does not follow that the exponent of this exchange-ratio is necessarily the exponent of the magnitude of the commodity's value. Suppose two equal quantities of socially necessary labor are represented by 1 quarter of wheat and £2 (nearly 1/2 oz. of gold) respectively. (Socially necessary labor-time is the time needed to produce an article under the normal conditions of production of the day, with the average skill and intensity of labor.) Then £2 is the expression in money of the magnitude of the value of the quarter of wheat, or its price. Now suppose circumstances allow this price to be raised to £3, or force it to be reduced to £1. Then £1 and £3 may be too small or too great to express the magnitude of the wheat's value properly; nevertheless they are its prices. For they are, in the first place, the form in which its value appears, that is, money; and in the second place, the exponents of its exchange-ratio with money. If the conditions of production, in other words the productive power of labor, stay constant, the same amount of social labor-time must be spent on reproducing a quarter of wheat both before and after the change in price. This fact depends neither on the will of the wheat producer nor on that of the owners of other commodities.

[^14]: “Ou bien, il faut consentir à dire qu’une valeur d’un million en argent vaut plus qu’une valeur égale en marchandises.” [“Or else we must agree to say that a value of a million in money is worth more than an equal value in commodities.”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 919), which amounts to saying “qu’une valeur vaut plus qu’une valeur égale.” [“that a value is worth more than an equal value.”]

<!-- ch03-p019 -->
Magnitude of value expresses a relation of social production: it expresses the connection that necessarily exists between a certain article and the portion of society's total labor-time required to produce it. As soon as magnitude of value is turned into price, this necessary relation takes the shape of a more or less accidental exchange-ratio between a single commodity and another, the money-commodity. But this exchange-ratio may express either the real magnitude of that commodity's value, or the quantity of gold, deviating from that value, for which, depending on circumstances, the commodity may be parted with. So the possibility of a quantitative mismatch between price and magnitude of value, that is, of price deviating from magnitude of value, is built into the price form itself. This is no defect. On the contrary, it makes the price form admirably suited to a mode of production whose built-in laws assert themselves only as the average of apparently lawless irregularities that cancel one another out.

<!-- ch03-p020 -->
The price form, however, is not only compatible with the possibility of a quantitative mismatch between magnitude of value and price, that is, between magnitude of value and its expression in money. It may also hide a qualitative inconsistency, so much so that price stops expressing value altogether, although money is nothing but the form of value of commodities. Things that in themselves are not commodities, such as conscience, honor, etc., can be offered for sale by their holders, and so acquire, through their price, the form of commodities. Hence a thing may have a price without having value. The price in that case is imaginary, like certain quantities in mathematics. On the other hand, the imaginary price form may sometimes hide either a direct or an indirect real value-relation; for instance, the price of uncultivated land, which is without value, because no human labor has been incorporated in it.

<!-- ch03-p021 -->
Price, like relative value in general, expresses the value of a commodity (for example, a ton of iron) by stating that a given quantity of the equivalent (for example, an ounce of gold) can be directly exchanged for iron. But it by no means states the reverse, that iron can be directly exchanged for gold. So, for a commodity to act effectively in practice as exchange-value, it must leave its bodily shape and transform itself from merely imaginary gold into real gold. (Exchange-value is the proportion in which one commodity trades for others, for example how many coats a quantity of linen trades for.) For the commodity, this transubstantiation (change of substance) may be harder than the transition from “necessity” to “freedom” is for the Hegelian “concept,” or than casting off its shell is for a lobster, or than putting off the old Adam was for Saint Jerome.[^15] A commodity may, alongside its actual form (iron, for instance), take the form of gold in our imagination, but it cannot actually be both iron and gold at one and the same time. To fix its price, it is enough to equate it to gold in imagination. But to enable it to do its owner the service of a universal equivalent (a single commodity in which all other commodities express their values), it must actually be replaced by gold. Suppose the owner of the iron went to the owner of some other commodity offered for exchange and pointed to the price of the iron as proof that it was already money. He would get the same answer that St. Peter gave Dante in heaven, when Dante recited the creed –

So a price implies both that a commodity can be exchanged for money and that it must be so exchanged. On the other hand, gold serves as an ideal measure of value only because it has already established itself as the money-commodity in the process of exchange. Under the ideal measure of values lurks hard cash.
