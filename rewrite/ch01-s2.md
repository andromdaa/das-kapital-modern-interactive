### Section 2: The Two-fold Character of the Labor Embodied in Commodities

<!-- ch01-p020 -->
At first sight a commodity presented itself to us as a complex of two things: use-value and exchange-value. Later on, we also saw that labor, too, has the same two-fold nature. For insofar as labor finds expression in value, it does not have the same characteristics that belong to it as a creator of use-values. I was the first to point out this two-fold nature of the labor contained in commodities and to examine it critically. This point is the pivot on which a clear understanding of political economy (the science of economics as it was then called) turns, so we must go into it in more detail.

<!-- ch01-p021 -->
Let us take two commodities, such as a coat and 10 yards of linen, and let the coat have double the value of the linen, so that, if 10 yards of linen = W, the coat = 2W.

<!-- ch01-p022 -->
The coat is a use-value that satisfies a particular want. It exists as the result of a special sort of productive activity, whose nature is determined by its aim, its way of operating, its object, its means, and its result. Labor whose usefulness is represented in this way by the use-value of its product, or which shows itself by making its product a use-value, we call useful labor. In this connection we consider only its useful effect.

<!-- ch01-p023 -->
Just as the coat and the linen are two use-values that differ in quality, so too are the two forms of labor that produce them, tailoring and weaving. If these two objects did not differ in quality, and were not produced by labor of different quality, they could not stand to each other in the relation of commodities. Coats are not exchanged for coats; one use-value is not exchanged for another of the same kind.

<!-- ch01-p024 -->
To all the different kinds of use-values there correspond as many different kinds of useful labor. These are classified according to the order, genus, species, and variety to which they belong in the social division of labor. This division of labor is a necessary condition for the production of commodities. But it does not follow, the other way round, that the production of commodities is a necessary condition for the division of labor. In the primitive Indian community there is a social division of labor, without production of commodities. Or, to take an example nearer home, in every factory the labor is divided according to a system, but this division is not brought about by the factory workers exchanging their individual products with one another. Only products that result from different kinds of labor, each kind carried on independently and on the account of private individuals, can become commodities in relation to each other.

<!-- ch01-p025 -->
To sum up, then: in the use-value of each commodity there is contained useful labor, that is, productive activity of a definite kind, carried out with a definite aim. Use-values cannot face each other as commodities unless the useful labor embodied in them differs in quality in each of them. Take a community whose produce in general takes the form of commodities, that is, a community of commodity producers. There, this difference in quality between the useful forms of labor, which individual producers carry on independently, each on their own account, develops into a complex system, a social division of labor.

<!-- ch01-p026 -->
In any case, whether the coat is worn by the tailor or by his customer, in either case it works as a use-value. Nor is the relation between the coat and the labor that produced it changed by the fact that tailoring may have become a special trade, an independent branch of the social division of labor. Wherever the need for clothing forced them to it, the human race made clothes for thousands of years without a single man becoming a tailor. But coats and linen, like every other element of material wealth that is not produced spontaneously by Nature, must always owe their existence to a special productive activity, carried out with a definite aim. This activity takes particular materials given by nature and makes them serve particular human wants. So insofar as labor is a creator of use-value, is useful labor, it is a necessary condition for the existence of the human race, independent of all forms of society. It is an eternal necessity imposed by nature, without which there can be no material exchanges between man and Nature, and therefore no life.

<!-- ch01-p027 -->
The use-values coat, linen, etc., that is, the bodies of commodities, are combinations of two elements: matter and labor. If we take away the useful labor spent on them, a material base is always left, which Nature supplies without the help of man. Man can work only as Nature does, that is, by changing the form of matter.[^13] Indeed, more than that: in this work of changing form he is constantly helped by natural forces. We see, then, that labor is not the only source of material wealth, of the use-values that labor produces. As William Petty puts it, labor is its father and the earth its mother.

[^13]: Tutti i fenomeni dell’universo, sieno essi prodotti della mano dell’uomo, ovvero delle universali leggi della fisica, non ci danno idea di attuale creazione, ma unicamente di una modificazione della materia. Accostare e separare sono gli unici elementi che l’ingegno umano ritrova analizzando l’idea della riproduzione: e tanto e riproduzione di valore (value in use, although Verri in this passage of his controversy with the Physiocrats is not himself quite certain of the kind of value he is speaking of) e di ricchezze se la terra, l’aria e l’acqua ne’ campi si trasmutino in grano, come se colla mano dell’uomo il glutine di un insetto si trasmuti in velluto ovvero alcuni pezzetti di metalio si organizzino a formare una ripetizione.” [“All the phenomena of the universe, whether produced by the hand of man or by the universal laws of physics, are not actual new creations, but merely a modification of matter. XV., p. 22.

<!-- ch01-p028 -->
Let us now pass from the commodity considered as a use-value to the value of commodities.

<!-- ch01-p029 -->
By our assumption, the coat is worth twice as much as the linen. But this is a merely quantitative difference, which does not concern us for the present. We bear in mind, however, that if the value of the coat is double that of 10 yds of linen, 20 yds of linen must have the same value as one coat. Insofar as they are values, the coat and the linen are things of a like substance, objective expressions of essentially identical labor. But tailoring and weaving are kinds of labor that differ in quality. There are, however, states of society in which one and the same man does tailoring and weaving by turns. In that case these two forms of labor are mere variations of the labor of the same individual, not special and fixed functions of different persons. In just the same way, the coat our tailor makes one day and the trousers he makes another day imply only a variation in the labor of one and the same individual. Moreover, we see at a glance that in our capitalist society a given portion of human labor is supplied, according to the varying demand, at one time in the form of tailoring, at another in the form of weaving. This change may possibly not take place without friction, but take place it must.

<!-- ch01-p030 -->
Productive activity, if we leave aside its special form, namely the useful character of the labor, is nothing but the spending of human labor-power. Tailoring and weaving, though they are productive activities that differ in quality, are each a productive spending of human brains, nerves, and muscles, and in this sense are human labor. They are merely two different ways of spending human labor-power. Of course, this labor-power, which stays the same through all its variations, must have reached a certain level of development before it can be spent in many different ways. But the value of a commodity represents abstract human labor, the spending of human labor in general. In society, a general or a banker plays a great part, while mere man, on the other hand, plays a very shabby part,[^14] and it is the same here with mere human labor. It is the spending of simple labor-power, that is, of the labor-power that, on average, exists in the body of every ordinary individual, apart from any special development. Simple average labor, it is true, varies in character in different countries and at different times, but in a particular society it is given. Skilled labor counts only as simple labor intensified, or rather as multiplied simple labor, so that a given quantity of skilled labor is considered equal to a greater quantity of simple labor. Experience shows that this reduction is constantly being made. A commodity may be the product of the most skilled labor, but its value equates it to the product of simple unskilled labor, and so represents a definite quantity of that simple labor alone.[^15] The different proportions in which different sorts of labor are reduced to unskilled labor as their standard are set by a social process that goes on behind the backs of the producers. Consequently, these proportions appear to be fixed by custom. For simplicity’s sake we shall from now on count every kind of labor as unskilled, simple labor. By doing this we do no more than save ourselves the trouble of making the reduction.

[^14]: Compare Hegel, “Philosophie des Rechts.” Berlin, 1840. p. 250.

[^15]: The reader must note that we are not speaking here of the wages or value that the worker gets for a given labor-time, but of the value of the commodity in which that labor-time is materialized. Wages is a category that does not yet exist at the present stage of our investigation.

<!-- ch01-p031 -->
So, just as we abstract from the different use-values of the coat and the linen when we view them as values, so it is with the labor represented by those values: we disregard the difference between its useful forms, weaving and tailoring. The use-values coat and linen are combinations of special productive activities with cloth and yarn. The values coat and linen, on the other hand, are mere uniform, congealed quantities of undifferentiated labor. In the same way, the labor embodied in these values does not count because of its productive relation to cloth and yarn, but only as being the spending of human labor-power. Tailoring and weaving are necessary factors in creating the use-values coat and linen precisely because these two kinds of labor differ in quality. But tailoring and weaving form the substance of the values of these same articles only insofar as we abstract from their special qualities, only insofar as both have the same quality of being human labor.

<!-- ch01-p032 -->
Coats and linen, however, are not merely values, but values of a definite magnitude, and according to our assumption, the coat is worth twice as much as the ten yards of linen. Where does this difference in their values come from? It comes from the fact that the linen contains only half as much labor as the coat. Consequently, in producing the coat, labor-power must have been spent for twice the time needed to produce the linen.

<!-- ch01-p033 -->
So, with reference to use-value, the labor contained in a commodity counts only by its quality, while with reference to value it counts only by its quantity, and must first be reduced to human labor pure and simple. In the first case, it is a question of How and What; in the second, of How much? How long a time? Since the magnitude of the value of a commodity represents only the quantity of labor embodied in it, it follows that all commodities, when taken in certain proportions, must be equal in value.

<!-- ch01-p034 -->
If the productive power of all the different sorts of useful labor needed to produce a coat stays unchanged, the sum of the values of the coats produced increases with their number. If one coat represents x days’ labor, two coats represent 2x days’ labor, and so on. But assume that the time of the labor needed to produce a coat is doubled or halved. In the first case one coat is worth as much as two coats were before. In the second case two coats are worth only as much as one was before, although in both cases one coat gives the same service as before, and the useful labor embodied in it remains of the same quality. But the quantity of labor spent on producing it has changed.

<!-- ch01-p035 -->
An increase in the quantity of use-values is an increase of material wealth. With two coats two men can be clothed, with one coat only one man. Nevertheless, an increased quantity of material wealth may go together with a simultaneous fall in the magnitude of its value. This antagonistic movement has its origin in the two-fold character of labor. Productive power, of course, refers only to labor of some useful concrete form: how effective any special productive activity is during a given time depends on its productivity. So useful labor becomes a more or less abundant source of products, in proportion to the rise or fall of its productivity. On the other hand, no change in this productivity affects the labor represented by value. Since productive power is a property of the concrete useful forms of labor, it of course can no longer have any bearing on that labor as soon as we abstract from those concrete useful forms. However much productive power may vary, then, the same labor, carried on for equal periods of time, always yields equal amounts of value. But during equal periods of time it will yield different quantities of use-values: more if the productive power rises, fewer if it falls. The same change in productive power that increases the fruitfulness of labor, and so the quantity of use-values that labor produces, will reduce the total value of this increased quantity of use-values, provided the change shortens the total labor-time needed to produce them; and the other way round.

<!-- ch01-p036 -->
On the one hand, all labor is, physiologically speaking, a spending of human labor-power, and in its character as identical abstract human labor it creates and forms the value of commodities. On the other hand, all labor is the spending of human labor-power in a special form and with a definite aim, and in this character, as concrete useful labor, it produces use-values.[^16]

[^16]: To prove that labor alone is that all-sufficient and real measure by which the value of all commodities can at all times be estimated and compared, Adam Smith says: “Equal quantities of labour must at all times and in all places have the same value for the labourer. In his normal state of health, strength, and activity, and with the average degree of skill that he may possess, he must always give up the same portion of his rest, his freedom, and his happiness.” (“Wealth of Nations,” b. I. ch. V.) On the one hand, Adam Smith here (but not everywhere) confuses two things: fixing value by the quantity of labor spent in producing commodities, and fixing the values of commodities by the value of labor. As a result he tries to prove that equal quantities of labor always have the same value. On the other hand, he has an inkling that labor, insofar as it shows itself in the value of commodities, counts only as the spending of labor-power. But he treats this spending as merely the sacrifice of rest, freedom, and happiness, not also as the normal activity of living beings. But then, he has the modern wage worker in view. Much more aptly, the anonymous predecessor of Adam Smith, quoted above in note 9 of this chapter, says: “one man has employed himself a week in providing this necessary of life ... and he that gives him some other in exchange cannot make a better estimate of what is a proper equivalent, than by computing what cost him just as much labour and time; which in effect is no more than exchanging one man’s labour in one thing for a time certain, for another man’s labour in another thing for the same time.” (cited above, p. 39.) [The English language has the advantage of having different words for the two aspects of labor considered here.
