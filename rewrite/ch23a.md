# Part 7: The Accumulation of Capital

<!-- pt7-p001 -->
The first step taken by the amount of value that is going to function as capital is the conversion of a sum of money into means of production and labor-power. (Value is what commodities have in common that lets them be exchanged as equals: the human labor spent on them, counted by labor-time. Capital is value that is used to make more value: money that is advanced in order to come back larger. Means of production are the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things. Labor-power is a person’s capacity to work: the mental and physical abilities a human being uses when producing anything. It is this capacity, not labor itself, that the worker sells to the capitalist.) This conversion takes place in the market, within the sphere of circulation (the exchange of commodities through money: selling and buying). The second step, the process of production, is complete as soon as the means of production have been turned into commodities (things produced in order to be exchanged, or sold, not for the producer’s own use) whose value is greater than that of their component parts. So their value contains the capital originally advanced, plus a surplus-value (the extra value that workers produce beyond the value of their own labor-power, which the capitalist keeps without paying for it). These commodities must then be thrown into circulation. They must be sold and their value realized in money, and this money must be turned into capital afresh, and so on, over and over again. This circular movement, in which the same phases are continually gone through one after another, forms the circulation of capital.

<!-- pt7-p002 -->
The first condition of accumulation (the accumulation of capital: turning surplus-value back into capital, so that capital grows) is that the capitalist must have managed to sell his commodities, and to turn the greater part of the money so received back into capital. In the following pages we shall assume that capital circulates in its normal way. The detailed analysis of the process will be found in Book II.

<!-- pt7-p003 -->
The capitalist who produces surplus-value, that is, who extracts unpaid labor directly from the workers and fixes it in commodities, is indeed the first to appropriate this surplus-value, but by no means its final owner. He has to share it with capitalists, with landowners, etc., who perform other functions in the complex whole of social production. Surplus-value, therefore, splits up into various parts. Its fragments fall to various categories of persons and take various forms, independent of one another, such as profit, interest, merchants’ profit, rent, etc. Only in Book III. can we take up these modified forms of surplus-value.

<!-- pt7-p004 -->
On the one hand, then, we assume that the capitalist sells the commodities he has produced at their value. We do not concern ourselves either with the new forms that capital takes on while in the sphere of circulation, or with the concrete conditions of reproduction hidden under these forms. On the other hand, we treat the capitalist producer as the owner of the entire surplus-value, or, better perhaps, as the representative of all who share the booty with him. So we first of all consider accumulation from an abstract point of view, that is, as a mere phase in the actual process of production.

<!-- pt7-p005 -->
To the extent that accumulation takes place, the capitalist must have succeeded in selling his commodities and in turning the money from the sale back into capital. Moreover, the breaking-up of surplus-value into fragments changes neither its nature nor the conditions under which it becomes an element of accumulation. Whatever share of surplus-value the industrial capitalist keeps for himself or gives up to others, he is the one who appropriates it in the first instance. So we assume no more than what actually takes place. On the other hand, the simple fundamental form of the process of accumulation is obscured by the incidental process of circulation that brings it about, and by the splitting-up of surplus-value. So an exact analysis of the process demands that we disregard, for a time, all phenomena that hide the play of its inner mechanism.

## Chapter 23: Simple Reproduction

<!-- ch23-p001 -->
Whatever the form of the process of production in a society, it must be a continuous process; it must keep going periodically through the same phases. A society can no more stop producing than it can stop consuming. So when it is viewed as a connected whole, and as flowing on with constant renewal, every social process of production is at the same time a process of reproduction.

<!-- ch23-p002 -->
The conditions of production are also those of reproduction. No society can go on producing, in other words no society can reproduce, unless it constantly turns a part of its products back into means of production, or elements of new products. All other circumstances remaining the same, the only way it can reproduce its wealth and keep it at one level is by replacing the means of production (that is, the instruments of labor, the raw material, and the auxiliary substances used up in the course of the year) with an equal quantity of articles of the same kind. These must be separated from the mass of the year’s products and thrown afresh into the process of production. So a definite portion of each year’s product belongs to the domain of production. This portion is destined for productive consumption from the very first, and for the most part it exists in the shape of articles totally unfit for individual consumption.

<!-- ch23-p003 -->
If production is capitalist in form, so too will reproduction be. Just as in capitalist production the labor process (the process of work itself, in which people use tools on materials to make useful things) figures only as a means toward the self-expansion of capital, so in capitalist reproduction it figures only as a means of reproducing the value advanced as capital, that is, as self-expanding value. It is only because his money constantly functions as capital that a man wears the economic guise of a capitalist. If, for instance, a sum of £100 has been turned into capital this year and has produced a surplus-value of £20, it must go on repeating the same operation next year and in the years after. As a periodic increase of the capital advanced, or periodic fruit of capital in process, surplus-value takes on the form of a revenue flowing out of capital.[^1]

[^1]: “Mais ces riches, qui consomment les produits du travail des autres, ne peuvent les obtenir que par des échanges [purchases of commodities]. S’ils donnent cependant leur richesse acquise et accumulée en retour contre ces produits nouveaux qui sont l’objet de leur fantaisie, ils semblent exposés à épuiser bientôt leur fonds de réserve; ils ne travaillent point, avons-nous dit, et ils ne peuvent même travailler; on croirait donc que chaque jour doit voir diminuer leurs vieilles richesses, et que lorsqu’il ne leur en restera plus, rien ne sera offert en échange aux ouvriers qui travaillent exclusivement pour eux.... Mais dans l’ordre social, la richesse a acquis la propriété de se reproduire par le travail d’autrui, et sans que son propriétaire y concoure. La richesse, comme le travail, et par le travail, donne un fruit annuel qui peut être détruit chaque année sans que le riche en devienne plus pauvre. Ce fruit est le *revenu* qui naît du *capital*.” [But these rich people, who consume the products of the labor of others, can obtain them only by exchanges. Yet if they give their acquired and accumulated wealth in return for these new products, which are the object of their fancy, they seem exposed to using up their reserve fund soon. They do not work at all, we have said, and they cannot even work. So one would think that each day must see their old wealth shrink, and that when none of it is left to them, nothing will be offered in exchange to the workers who work exclusively for them.... But in the social order, wealth has acquired the property of reproducing itself through the labor of others, and without its owner taking part. Wealth, like labor, and through labor, yields a yearly fruit that can be destroyed every year without the rich man becoming any poorer. This fruit is the *revenue* that arises from *capital*.] (Sismondi: “Nouv. Princ. d’Econ. Pol.” Paris, 1819, t. I, pp. 81-82.)

<!-- ch23-p004 -->
If this revenue serves the capitalist only as a fund for his consumption, and is spent as periodically as it is gained, then, other things being equal, simple reproduction will take place. And although this reproduction is a mere repetition of the process of production on the old scale, this mere repetition, or continuity, still gives the process a new character, or rather, makes some apparent characteristics disappear that it had as an isolated, discontinuous process.

<!-- ch23-p005 -->
The purchase of labor-power for a fixed period is the prelude to the process of production. This prelude is constantly repeated when the agreed term comes to an end, when a definite period of production, such as a week or a month, has passed. But the worker is not paid until after he has spent his labor-power and realized in commodities not only its value, but surplus-value. So he has produced not only surplus-value, which for the present we regard as a fund to meet the private consumption of the capitalist. He has also produced the fund out of which he himself is paid, the variable capital, before it flows back to him in the shape of wages (the price paid for labor-power, which appears, wrongly, as the price of labor itself). (Variable capital is the part of capital spent on labor-power. It reproduces its own value and also produces surplus-value, so its size varies.) And he stays employed only as long as he keeps reproducing this fund. Hence that formula of the economists, mentioned in Chapter XVIII, which represents wages as a share in the product itself.[^2] What flows back to the worker in the shape of wages is a portion of the product that he continuously reproduces. The capitalist, it is true, pays him in money, but this money is merely the changed form of the product of his labor. While he is turning a portion of the means of production into products, a portion of his earlier product is being turned into money. It is his labor of last week, or of last year, that pays for his labor-power this week or this year. The illusion created by money stepping in between vanishes at once if, instead of taking a single capitalist and a single worker, we take the class of capitalists and the class of workers as a whole. The capitalist class constantly gives the working class order-notes, in the form of money, on a portion of the commodities that the workers produced and the capitalists appropriated. The workers just as constantly give these order-notes back to the capitalist class, and in this way get their share of their own product. The transaction is veiled by the commodity form of the product and the money form of the commodity. (The money form is the form of value in which one commodity, gold, has become the universal equivalent.)

[^2]: “Wages as well as profits are to be considered, each of them, as really a portion of the finished product.” (Ramsay, cited above, p. 142.) “The share of the product which comes to the labourer in the form of wages.” (J. Mill, “Eléments, &c.” Translated by Parissot. Paris, 1823, p. 34.)

<!-- ch23-p006 -->
So variable capital is only a particular historical form of appearance of the fund for providing the necessaries of life, or the labor fund, which the worker needs to maintain himself and his family, and which, whatever the system of social production, he must himself produce and reproduce. If the labor fund constantly flows to him in the form of money that pays for his labor, it is because the product he has created constantly moves away from him in the form of capital. But all this does not change the fact that it is the worker’s own labor, realized in a product, that the capitalist advances to him.[^3] Let us take a peasant who is bound to do compulsory service for his lord. He works on his own land, with his own means of production, for, say, 3 days a week. On the 3 other days he does forced work on the lord’s estate. He constantly reproduces his own labor fund, which in his case never takes the form of a money payment for his labor, advanced by another person. But in return, his unpaid forced labor for the lord, for its part, never takes on the character of voluntary paid labor. If one fine morning the lord takes for himself the land, the cattle, the seed, in a word, the means of production of this peasant, the peasant will from then on be obliged to sell his labor-power to the lord. Other things being equal, he will labor 6 days a week as before, 3 for himself and 3 for his lord, who from then on becomes a wage-paying capitalist. As before, he will use up the means of production as means of production and transfer their value to the product. As before, a definite portion of the product will go to reproduction. But from the moment the forced labor is changed into wage labor, from that moment the labor fund, which the peasant himself goes on producing and reproducing as before, takes the form of a capital advanced by the lord in the form of wages. The bourgeois economist (bourgeois: belonging to the capitalist class, or to society and thinking shaped by it), whose narrow mind cannot separate the form of appearance from the thing that appears, shuts his eyes to the fact that even nowadays the labor fund crops up in the form of capital only here and there on the face of the earth.[^4]

[^3]: “When capital is employed in advancing to the workman his wages, it adds nothing to the funds for the maintenance of labour.” (Cazenove in note to his edition of Malthus’ “Definitions in Pol. Econ.” London, 1853, p. 22.)

[^4]: “The wages of labour are advanced by capitalists in the case of less than one fourth of the labourers of the earth.” (Rich. Jones: “Textbook of Lectures on the Pol. Econ. of Nations.” Hertford, 1852, p. 36.)

<!-- ch23-p007 -->
Variable capital, it is true, loses its character of a value advanced out of the capitalist’s funds[^5] only when we view the process of capitalist production in the flow of its constant renewal. But that process must have had a beginning of some kind. So from our present standpoint it seems likely that the capitalist, once upon a time, came into possession of money through some accumulation that took place independently of the unpaid labor of others, and that this, therefore, was how he was able to go to the market as a buyer of labor-power. However this may be, the mere continuity of the process, simple reproduction, brings about some other wonderful changes, which affect not only the variable capital but the total capital.

[^5]: “Though the manufacturer” (that is, the worker) “has his wages advanced to him by his master, he in reality costs him no expense, the value of these wages being generally reserved, together with a profit, in the improved value of the subject [the material worked on] upon which his labour is bestowed.” (A. Smith, cited above, Book II. ch. III, p. 311.)

<!-- ch23-p008 -->
If a capital of £1,000 produces a surplus-value of £200 a year, and if this surplus-value is consumed every year, then clearly at the end of 5 years the surplus-value consumed will amount to 5 × £200, or the £1,000 originally advanced. If only a part, say one half, were consumed, the same result would follow at the end of 10 years, since 10 × £100= £1,000. General Rule: The value of the capital advanced, divided by the surplus-value consumed each year, gives the number of years, or reproduction periods, at the end of which the capital originally advanced has been consumed by the capitalist and has disappeared. The capitalist thinks that he is consuming the product of the unpaid labor of others, that is, the surplus-value, and keeping his original capital intact; but what he thinks cannot change the facts. After a certain number of years, the capital value he then owns is equal to the sum total of the surplus-value he appropriated during those years, and the total value he has consumed is equal to that of his original capital. It is true that he has in hand a capital whose amount has not changed, and part of which, namely the buildings, machinery, etc., was already there when the work of his business began. But what we are dealing with here is not the material elements of that capital, but its value. When a person runs through all his property by taking on debts equal to the value of that property, clearly his property represents nothing but the sum total of his debts. And so it is with the capitalist. When he has consumed the equivalent of his original capital, the value of his present capital represents nothing but the total amount of the surplus-value he appropriated without payment. Not a single atom of the value of his old capital still exists.

<!-- ch23-p009 -->
Apart, then, from all accumulation, the mere continuity of the process of production, in other words simple reproduction, sooner or later and of necessity turns every capital into accumulated capital, or capitalized surplus-value. Even if that capital was originally acquired by the personal labor of the one who employs it, sooner or later it becomes value appropriated without an equivalent: the unpaid labor of others, materialized either in money or in some other object. We saw in Chapters IV.-VI. that turning money into capital requires something more than the production and circulation of commodities. We saw that on the one side the possessor of value or money, and on the other the possessor of the value-creating substance; on the one side the possessor of the means of production and subsistence, and on the other the possessor of nothing but labor-power, must face each other as buyer and seller. (Means of subsistence are the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live.) So the separation of labor from its product, of subjective labor-power from the objective conditions of labor, was the real foundation in fact, and the starting-point, of capitalist production.

<!-- ch23-p010 -->
But what at first was only a starting-point becomes, through the mere continuity of the process, through simple reproduction, the distinctive result of capitalist production, constantly renewed and made permanent. On the one hand, the process of production constantly turns material wealth into capital, into means of creating more wealth and means of enjoyment for the capitalist. On the other hand, the worker leaves the process as he entered it: a source of wealth, but without any means of making that wealth his own. Since his own labor, before he enters the process, has already been alienated from him by the sale of his labor-power, and has been appropriated by the capitalist and incorporated into capital, it must, during the process, be realized in a product that does not belong to him. Since the process of production is also the process by which the capitalist consumes labor-power, the worker’s product is constantly turned not only into commodities but into capital: into value that sucks up the value-creating power, into means of subsistence that buy the person of the worker, into means of production that command the producers.[^6] So the worker constantly produces material, objective wealth, but in the form of capital, of an alien power that dominates and exploits him. And the capitalist just as constantly produces labor-power, but in the form of a subjective source of wealth, separated from the objects in which and through which alone it can be realized. In short, he produces the worker, but as a wage worker.[^7] This constant reproduction, this perpetuation of the worker, is the indispensable condition of capitalist production.

[^6]: “This is a remarkably peculiar property of productive labour. Whatever is productively consumed is capital and it becomes capital by consumption.” (James Mill, cited above, p. 242.) James Mill, however, never got onto the track of this “remarkably peculiar property.”

[^7]: “It is true indeed, that the first introducing a manufacture employs many poor, but they cease not to be so, and the continuance of it makes many.” (“Reasons for a Limited Exportation of Wool.” London, 1677, p. 19.) “The farmer now absurdly asserts, that he keeps the poor. They are indeed kept in misery.” (“Reasons for the Late Increase of the Poor Rates: or a Comparative View of the Prices of Labour and Provisions.” London, 1777, p. 31.)

<!-- ch23-p011 -->
The worker consumes in two ways. While producing, he consumes the means of production by his labor and turns them into products with a higher value than that of the capital advanced. This is his productive consumption. It is at the same time the consumption of his labor-power by the capitalist who bought it. On the other hand, the worker turns the money paid to him for his labor-power into means of subsistence: this is his individual consumption. So the worker’s productive consumption and his individual consumption are totally distinct. In the first, he acts as the motive power of capital and belongs to the capitalist. In the second, he belongs to himself and performs his necessary vital functions outside the process of production. The result of the one is that the capitalist lives; of the other, that the worker lives.
