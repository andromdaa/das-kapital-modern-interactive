#### B. The Badly Paid Strata of the British Industrial Class

<!-- ch25-p106 -->
During the cotton famine of 1862, the Privy Council put Dr. Smith in charge of an inquiry into how well the distressed factory workers in Lancashire and Cheshire were fed. His observations over many earlier years had led him to conclude that “to avert starvation diseases,” the daily food of an average woman ought to contain at least 3,900 grains of carbon with 180 grains of nitrogen, and the daily food of an average man at least 4,300 grains of carbon with 200 grains of nitrogen. For women, by his reckoning, that is about the same quantity of nutritive elements as is contained in 2 lbs. of good wheaten bread, and for men 1/9 more. For the weekly average of adult men and women, his figure was at least 28,600 grains of carbon and 1,330 grains of nitrogen. His calculation was confirmed in practice in a surprising way: it agreed with the miserable quantity of food to which want had forced down the consumption of the cotton factory workers. In December, 1862, this was 29,211 grains of carbon and 1,295 grains of nitrogen weekly.

<!-- ch25-p107 -->
In the year 1863, the Privy Council ordered an inquiry into the state of distress of the worst-fed part of the English working class. Dr. Simon, medical officer to the Privy Council, chose the above-mentioned Dr. Smith for this work. On the one hand, his inquiry covers the agricultural workers. On the other, it covers silk-weavers, needlewomen, kid-glovers, stocking-weavers, glove-weavers, and shoemakers. Except for the stocking-weavers, the people in these last categories live exclusively in towns. The inquiry made it a rule to select, in each category, the healthiest families, and those comparatively best off.

<!-- ch25-p108 -->
The general result was that

<!-- ch25-p109 -->
> “in only one of the examined classes of in-door operatives did the average nitrogen supply just exceed, while in another it nearly reached, the estimated standard of bare sufficiency [that is, sufficient to avert starvation diseases], and that in two classes there was defect – in one, a very large defect – of both nitrogen and carbon. Moreover, as regards the examined families of the agricultural population, it appeared that more than a fifth were with less than the estimated sufficiency of carbonaceous food, that more than one-third were with less than the estimated sufficiency of nitrogenous food, and that in three counties (Berkshire, Oxfordshire, and Somersetshire), insufficiency of nitrogenous food was the average local diet.”[^46]

[^46]: “Public Health. Sixth Report, 1864,” p. 13.

<!-- ch25-p110 -->
Among the agricultural workers, those of England, the wealthiest part of the United Kingdom, were the worst fed.[^47] Among the agricultural workers, the shortage of food fell, as a rule, chiefly on the women and children, for “the man must eat to do his work.” Still greater want ravaged the town workers examined.

[^47]: Cited above, p. 17.

<!-- ch25-p111 -->
> “They are so ill fed that assuredly among them there must be many cases of severe and injurious privation.”[^48]

[^48]: Cited above, p. 13.

One half, or 60/125, of the categories of industrial workers investigated had absolutely no beer, and 28% had no milk. The weekly average of liquid foods in the families varied from seven ounces among the needle-women to 24¾ ounces among the stocking-makers. Most of those who got no milk were needle-women in London. The quantity of bread-stuffs eaten weekly varied from 7¾ lbs. for the needle-women to 11½ lbs. for the shoemakers, giving a total average of 9.9 lbs. per adult weekly. Sugar (treacle, etc.) varied from 4 ounces weekly for the kid-glovers to 11 ounces for the stocking-makers. The total average per week for all categories was 8 ounces per adult weekly. The total weekly average of butter (fat, etc.) was 5 ounces per adult. The weekly average of meat (bacon, etc.) varied from 7¼ ounces for the silk-weavers to 18¼ ounces for the kid-glovers; the total average for the different categories was 13.6 ounces. The weekly cost of food per adult gave the following average figures: silk-weavers 2s. 2½d., needle-women 2s. 7d., kid-glovers 2s. 9½d., shoemakers 2s. 7¾d., stocking-weavers 2s. 6¼d. For the silk-weavers of Macclesfield the average was only 1s. 8½d. The worst categories were the needle-women, silk-weavers and kid-glovers.[^50] Dr. Simon, in his General Health Report, says of these facts:

[^50]: Cited above, pp. 232, 233.

<!-- ch25-p116 -->
> “That cases are innumerable in which defective diet is the cause or the aggravator of disease, can be affirmed by any one who is conversant with poor law medical practice, or with the wards and out-patient rooms of hospitals.... Yet in this point of view, there is, in my opinion, a very important sanitary context to be added. It must be remembered that privation of food is very reluctantly borne, and that as a rule great poorness of diet will only come when other privations have preceded it. Long before insufficiency of diet is a matter of hygienic concern, long before the physiologist would think of counting the grains of nitrogen and carbon which intervene between life and starvation, the household will have been utterly destitute of material comfort; clothing and fuel will have been even scantier than food – against inclemencies of weather there will have been no adequate protection – dwelling space will have been stinted to the degree in which overcrowding produces or increases disease; of household utensils and furniture there will have been scarcely any ‒ even cleanliness will have been found costly or difficult, and if there still be self-respectful endeavours to maintain it, every such endeavour will represent additional pangs of hunger. The home, too, will be where shelter can be cheapest bought; in quarters where commonly there is least fruit of sanitary supervision, least drainage, least scavenging [street-cleaning], least suppression of public nuisances, least or worst water supply, and, if in town, least light and air. Such are the sanitary dangers to which poverty is almost certainly exposed, when it is poverty enough to imply scantiness of food. And while the sum of them is of terrible magnitude against life, the mere scantiness of food is in itself of very serious moment.... These are painful reflections, especially when it is remembered that the poverty to which they advert [refer] is not the deserved poverty of idleness. In all cases it is the poverty of working populations. Indeed, as regards the in-door operatives, the work which obtains the scanty pittance of food, is for the most part excessively prolonged. Yet evidently it is only in a qualified sense that the work can be deemed self-supporting.... And on a very large scale the nominal self-support can be only a circuit, longer or shorter, to pauperism.”[^51]

[^51]: Cited above, pp. 14, 15.

<!-- ch25-p117 -->
The close connection between the pangs of hunger of the most industrious layers of the working class and the extravagant consumption, coarse or refined, of the rich, for which capitalist accumulation is the basis, reveals itself only when the economic laws are known. It is different with the “housing of the poor.” Every unprejudiced observer sees that the greater the centralization of the means of production, the greater is the corresponding heaping together of the workers within a given space. He sees that therefore the faster capitalist accumulation goes, the more miserable are the dwellings of the working people. “Improvements” of towns go along with the increase of wealth: badly built quarters are demolished, palaces are put up for banks, warehouses, etc., and streets are widened for business traffic, for luxury carriages, and for bringing in tramways, etc. These “improvements” drive the poor away into even worse and more crowded hiding places. On the other hand, everyone knows that the dearness of dwellings is in inverse ratio to their quality, and that house speculators exploit the mines of misery with more profit or less cost than the silver mines of Potosi were ever exploited. The antagonistic character of capitalist accumulation, and therefore of capitalist property relations generally,[^52] is so evident here that even the official English reports on this subject are full of unorthodox attacks on “property and its rights.” With the development of industry, with the accumulation of capital, with the growth and “improvement” of towns, the evil makes such progress that the mere fear of contagious diseases, which do not spare even “respectability,” brought into existence no less than 10 Acts of Parliament on sanitation from 1847 to 1864. In some towns, such as Liverpool, Glasgow, etc., the frightened bourgeois took strenuous measures through their municipal governments. Nevertheless Dr. Simon, in his report of 1865, says:

[^52]: “In no particular have the rights of *persons* been so avowedly and shamefully sacrificed to the rights of *property* as in regard to the lodging of the labouring class. Every large town may be looked upon as a place of human sacrifice, a shrine where thousands pass yearly through the fire as offerings to the moloch [the idol fed with human sacrifices] of avarice,” S. Laing, cited above, p. 150.

<!-- ch25-p118 -->
> “Speaking generally, it may be said that the evils are uncontrolled in England.”

<!-- ch25-p119 -->
By order of the Privy Council, an inquiry was made in 1864 into the housing conditions of the agricultural workers, and in 1865 into those of the poorer classes in the towns. The results of Dr. Julian Hunter’s admirable work are found in the seventh (1865) and eighth (1866) reports on “Public Health.” I shall come to the agricultural workers later. On the condition of town dwellings, I first quote a general remark of Dr. Simon’s.

<!-- ch25-p120 -->
> “Although my official point of view,” he says, “is one exclusively physical, common humanity requires that the other aspect of this evil should not be ignored .... In its higher degrees it [that is, over-crowding] almost necessarily involves such negation of all delicacy, such unclean confusion of bodies and bodily functions, such exposure of animal and sexual nakedness, as is rather bestial than human. To be subject to these influences is a degradation which must become deeper and deeper for those on whom it continues to work. To children who are born under its curse, it must often be a very baptism into infamy. And beyond all measure hopeless is the wish that persons thus circumstanced should ever in other respects aspire to that atmosphere of civilisation which has its essence in physical and moral cleanliness.”[^53]

[^53]: “Public Health, Eighth Report. 1866.” p. 14, note.

London takes first place for over-crowded homes that are absolutely unfit for human beings.

<!-- ch25-p122 -->
> “He feels clear,” says Dr. Hunter, “on two points; first, that there are about 20 large colonies in London, of about 10,000 persons each, whose miserable condition exceeds almost anything he has seen elsewhere in England, and is almost entirely the result of their bad house accommodation; and second, that the crowded and dilapidated condition of the houses of these colonies is much worse than was the case 20 years ago.”[^54] “It is not too much to say that life in parts of London and Newcastle is infernal.”[^55]

[^54]: Cited above, p. 89. About the children in these colonies, Dr. Hunter says: “People are not now alive to tell us how children were brought up before this age of dense agglomerations of poor began, and he would be a rash prophet who should tell us what future behaviour is to be expected from the present growth of children, who, under circumstances probably never before paralleled in this country, are now completing their education for future practice, as ’dangerous classes’ by sitting up half the night with persons of every age, half naked, drunken, obscene, and quarrelsome.” (Cited above, p. 56.)

Further, in London the better-off part of the working class, together with the small shopkeepers and other elements of the lower middle class, falls more and more under the curse of these vile housing conditions. This happens in proportion as “improvements” advance, and with them the demolition of old streets and houses; as factories and the inflow of human beings grow in the metropolis; and finally as house rents rise with the ground-rents.

<!-- ch25-p124 -->
> “Rents have become so heavy that few labouring men can afford more than one room.”[^56]

[^56]: “Report of the Officer of Health of St. Martins-in-the-Fields, 1865.”

<!-- ch25-p125 -->
There is almost no house property in London that is not weighed down by a number of middlemen. For the price of land in London is always very high compared with its yearly revenue. So every buyer speculates on getting rid of it again at a jury price (the expropriation valuation fixed by jurymen), or on pocketing an extraordinary increase of value arising from having some large establishment in the neighborhood. As a consequence, there is a regular trade in buying up “fag-ends of leases” (the last few years left to run on leases).

<!-- ch25-p126 -->
> “Gentlemen in this business may be fairly expected to do as they do – get all they can from the tenants while they have them, and leave as little as they can for their successors.”[^57]

[^57]: “Public Health, Eighth Report, 1866,” p. 91.

The rents are weekly, and these gentlemen run no risk. As a result of railroads being built in the City,

<!-- ch25-p128 -->

The workhouses are already over-crowded, and the “improvements” already approved by Parliament have only just begun. If workers are driven away by the demolition of their old houses, they do not leave their old parish, or at most they settle down on its borders, as near to it as they can get.

<!-- ch25-p130 -->

Half the workmen ... of the Strand ... walked two miles to their work.”[^59]

[^59]: Cited above, p. 88.

<!-- ch25-p131 -->
This same Strand, a main street that gives strangers an imposing idea of the wealth of London, may serve as an example of how human beings are packed together in that town. In one of its parishes, the Officer of Health counted 581 persons per acre, even though half the width of the Thames was included in the reckoning. It goes without saying that every sanitary measure that drives the workers out of one quarter by demolishing uninhabitable houses, as has been the case in London until now, serves only to crowd them together even more closely in another.

<!-- ch25-p132 -->
> “Either,” says Dr. Hunter, “the whole proceeding will of necessity stop as an absurdity, or the public compassion (!) be effectually aroused to the obligation which may now be without exaggeration called national, of supplying cover to those who by reason of their having no capital, cannot provide it for themselves, though they can by periodical payments reward those who will provide it for them.”[^60]

[^60]: Cited above, p. 89.

<!-- ch25-p133 -->
Admire this capitalist justice! When the owner of land or of houses, or the businessman, is expropriated by “improvements” such as railroads, the building of new streets, etc., he not only receives full compensation. According to law, human and divine, he must also be comforted for his enforced “abstinence” by a thumping profit on top. The worker, with his wife and child and belongings, is thrown out into the street. And if he crowds in too large numbers into quarters of the town where the vestries (the parish councils) insist on decency, he is prosecuted in the name of sanitation!

<!-- ch25-p134 -->
At the beginning of the 19th century, apart from London, there was not a single town in England of 100,000 inhabitants. Only five had more than 50,000. Now there are 28 towns with more than 50,000 inhabitants.

<!-- ch25-p135 -->
> “The result of this change is not only that the class of town people is enormously increased, but the old close-packed little towns are now centres, built round on every side, open nowhere to air, and being no longer agreeable to the rich are abandoned by them for the pleasanter outskirts. The successors of these rich are occupying the larger houses at the rate of a family to each room [... and find accommodation for two or three lodgers ...] and a population, for which the houses were not intended and quite unfit, has been created, whose surroundings are truly degrading to the adults and ruinous to the children.”[^61]

The faster capital accumulates in an industrial or commercial town, the faster flows the stream of exploitable human material, and the more miserable are the makeshift dwellings of the workers.

Newcastle-on-Tyne, as the center of a coal and iron district of growing productivity, takes the next place after London in the housing inferno. No fewer than 34,000 persons live there in single rooms. Because they were an absolute danger to the community, great numbers of houses have lately been destroyed by the authorities in Newcastle and Gateshead. The building of new houses goes on very slowly, business very quickly. So in 1865 the town was fuller than ever. There was scarcely a room to let. Dr. Embleton, of the Newcastle Fever Hospital, says:

<!-- ch25-p138 -->

As capital and labor ebb and flow, the state of the dwellings in an industrial town may be bearable today and hideous tomorrow. Or the town’s aedileship (its city authorities) may have pulled itself together to remove the most shocking abuses. Tomorrow, like a swarm of locusts, masses of ragged Irishmen or broken-down English agricultural workers come crowding in. But now comes that revival of trade over which the mildly liberal Mr. Forster, the Black man’s friend, recently crowed with so much grace. #### C. The Nomad Population

<!-- ch25-p145 -->
We turn now to a class of people whose origin is agricultural, but whose occupation is largely industrial. They are the light infantry of capital, thrown by it, according to its needs, now to this point, now to that. When they are not on the march, they “camp.” Nomad labor is used for various operations of building and draining, brick-making, lime-burning, railway-making, etc. It is a flying column of pestilence: it carries smallpox, typhus, cholera, scarlet fever, etc., into the places near which it pitches its camp.[^67] In undertakings that involve much capital outlay, such as railways, etc., the contractor himself generally provides his army with wooden huts and the like. He thus improvises villages without any sanitary provisions, outside the control of the local boards, and very profitable to the contractor, who exploits the workers in two ways – as soldiers of industry and as tenants. Depending on whether the wooden hut contains 1, 2, or 3 holes, its inhabitant, navvy or whatever he may be, has to pay 1, 3, or 4 shillings weekly.[^68] One example will be enough. In September, 1864, Dr. Simon reports that the Chairman of the Nuisances Removal Committee of the parish of Sevenoaks sent the following denunciation to Sir George Grey, Home Secretary: –

[^67]: “Public Health. Seventh Report. 1865,” p. 18.

[^68]: Cited above, p. 165.

<!-- ch25-p146 -->
> “Small-pox cases were rarely heard of in this parish until about twelve months ago. Shortly before that time, the works for a railway from Lewisham to Tunbridge were commenced here, and, in addition to the principal works being in the immediate neighbourhood of this town, here was also established the depôt for the whole of the works, so that a large number of persons was of necessity employed here. As cottage accommodation could not be obtained for them all, huts were built in several places along the line of the works by the contractor, Mr. Jay, for their especial occupation. These huts possessed no ventilation nor drainage, and, besides, were necessarily over-crowded, because each occupant had to accommodate lodgers, whatever the number in his own family might be, although there were only two rooms to each tenement. The consequences were, according to the medical report we received, that in the night-time these poor people were compelled to endure all the horror of suffocation to avoid the pestiferous [disease-breeding] smells arising from the filthy, stagnant water, and the privies [outdoor toilets] close under their windows. Complaints were at length made to the Nuisances Removal Committee by a medical gentleman who had occasion to visit these huts, and he spoke of their condition as dwellings in the most severe terms, and he expressed his fears that some very serious consequences might ensue, unless some sanitary measures were adopted. About a year ago, Mr. Jay promised to appropriate a hut, to which persons in his employ, who were suffering from contagious diseases, might at once be removed. He repeated that promise on the 23rd July last, but although since the date of the last Promise there have been several cases of small-pox in his huts, and two deaths from the same disease, yet he has taken no steps whatever to carry out his promise. On the 9th September instant [of this month], Mr. Kelson, surgeon, reported to me further cases of small-pox in the same huts, and he described their condition as most disgraceful. I should add, for your (the Home Secretary’s) information that an isolated house, called the Pest-house, which is set apart for parishioners who might be suffering from infectious diseases, has been continually occupied by such patients for many months past, and is also now occupied; that in one family five children died from small-pox and fever; that from the 1st April to the 1st September this year, a period of five months, there have been no fewer than ten deaths from small-pox in the parish, four of them being in the huts already referred to; that it is impossible to ascertain the exact number of persons who have suffered from that disease although they are known to be many, from the fact of the families keeping it as private as possible.”[^69]

[^69]: Cited above, p. 18, Note. — The Relieving Officer of the Chapel-en-le-Frith Union reported to the Registrar-General as follows: — “At Doveholes, a number of small excavations have been made into a large hillock of lime ashes (the refuse of lime-kilns), and which are used as dwellings, and occupied by labourers and others employed in the construction of a railway now in course of construction through that neighbourhood. The excavations are small and damp, and have no drains or privies [outdoor toilets] about them, and not the slightest means of ventilation except up a hole pulled through the top, and used for a chimney. In consequence of this defect, small-pox has been raging for some time, and some deaths [among the troglodytes, the cave-dwellers] have been caused by it.” (Cited above, note 2.)

<!-- ch25-p147 -->
The workers in coal and other mines belong to the best paid categories of the British proletariat. The price at which they buy their wages was shown on an earlier page.[^70] Here I merely cast a hurried glance over their housing conditions. As a rule, the exploiter of a mine, whether its owner or his tenant, builds a number of cottages for his hands. They receive cottages and coal for heating “for nothing” – that is, these form part of their wages, paid in kind. Those who are not housed in this way receive £4 per year in compensation. The mining districts quickly attract a large population, made up of the miners themselves and the craftsmen, shopkeepers, etc., who group themselves around them. The ground-rents are high, as they generally are where population is dense. So the employer tries to run up, in the smallest space possible at the mouth of the pit, just as many cottages as are needed to pack together his hands and their families. If new mines are opened in the neighborhood, or old ones are set working again, the pressure increases. In building the cottages, only one point of view matters: the “abstinence” of the capitalist from all spending that is not absolutely unavoidable.

[^70]: The details given at the end of Part IV. refer especially to the workers in coal mines. On the still worse conditions in metal mines, see the very conscientious Report of the Royal Commission of 1864.

> “The lodging which is obtained by the pitman [miner] and other labourers connected with the collieries [coal mines] of Northumberland and Durham,” says Dr. Julian Hunter, “is perhaps, on the whole, the worst and the dearest of which any large specimens can be found in England, the similar parishes of Monmouthshire excepted.... The extreme badness is in the high number of men found in one room, in the smallness of the ground-plot on which a great number of houses are thrust, the want of water, the absence of privies [outdoor toilets], and the frequent placing of one house on the top of another, or distribution into flats, ... the lessee acts as if the whole colony were encamped, not resident.”[^71]

[^71]: Cited above, pp. 180, 182.

<!-- ch25-p149 -->
> “In pursuance of my instructions,” says Dr. Stevens, “I visited most of the large colliery villages in the Durham Union.... With very few exceptions, the general statement that no means are taken to secure the health of the inhabitants would be true of all of them.... All colliers are bound ['bound,’ a term which, like bondage, dates from the age of serfdom] to the colliery lessee or owner for twelve months.... If the colliers express discontent, or in any way annoy the ‘viewer’ [the colliery manager], a mark of memorandum is made against their names, and, at the annual ‘binding,’ such men are turned off [dismissed]... It appears to me that no part of the ‘truck system’ could be worse than what obtains [prevails] in these densely-populated districts. The collier is bound to take as part of his hiring a house surrounded with pestiferous [disease-breeding] influences; he cannot help himself, and it appears doubtful whether anyone else can help him except his proprietor (he is, to all intents and purposes, a serf), and his proprietor first consults his balance-sheet, and the result is tolerably certain. The collier is also often supplied with water by the proprietor, which, whether it be good or bad, he has to pay for, or rather he suffers a deduction for from his wages.”[^72]

[^72]: Cited above, pp. 515, 517.

When it comes into conflict with “public opinion,” or even with the Officers of Health, capital has no difficulty “justifying” the conditions, partly dangerous, partly degrading, to which it confines the worker’s working and home life. It says they are necessary for profit. It is the same thing when capital “abstains” from protective measures against dangerous machinery in the factory, from devices for ventilation and for safety in mines, etc. It is the same here with the housing of the miners. Dr. Simon, medical officer of the Privy Council, says in his official Report:

<!-- ch25-p151 -->
> “In apology for the wretched household accommodation ... it is alleged that miners are commonly worked on lease; that the duration of the lessee’s interest (which in collieries is commonly for 21 years), is not so long that he should deem it worth his while to create good accommodation for his labourers, and for the tradespeople and others whom the work attracts; that even if he were disposed to act liberally in the matter, this disposition would commonly be defeated by his landlord’s tendency to fix on him, as ground-rent, an exorbitant additional charge for the privilege of having on the surface of the ground the decent and comfortable village which the labourers of the subterranean property ought to inhabit, and that prohibitory price (if not actual prohibition) equally excludes others who might desire to build. It would be foreign to the purpose of this report to enter upon any discussion of the merits of the above apology. Nor here is it even needful to consider where it would be that, if decent accommodation were provided, the cost ... would eventually fall – whether on landlord, or lessee, or labourer, or public. But in presence of such shameful facts as are vouched for in the annexed reports [those of Dr. Hunter, Dr. Stevens, etc.] a remedy may well be claimed.... Claims of landlordship are being so used as to do great public wrong. The landlord in his capacity of mine-owner invites an industrial colony to labour on his estate, and then in his capacity of surface-owner makes it impossible that the labourers whom he collects, should find proper lodging where they must live. The lessee [the capitalist exploiter] meanwhile has no pecuniary [financial] motive for resisting that division of the bargain; well knowing that if its latter conditions be exorbitant, the consequences fall, not on him, that his labourers on whom they fall have not education enough to know the value of their sanitary rights, that neither obscenest lodging nor foulest drinking water will be appreciable inducements towards a ‘strike.’”[^73]
