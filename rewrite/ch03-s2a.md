### Section 2: The Medium of Circulation

##### A. The Metamorphosis of Commodities

<!-- ch03-p024 -->
We saw in an earlier chapter that the exchange of commodities involves conditions that contradict and exclude one another. The splitting of commodities into commodities and money does not sweep these inconsistencies away. Instead, it develops a *modus vivendi*, a form in which they can exist side by side. This is generally the way in which real contradictions are reconciled. For instance, it is a contradiction to depict one body as constantly falling towards another and, at the same time, as constantly flying away from it. The ellipse is a form of motion which lets this contradiction go on and at the same time reconciles it.

<!-- ch03-p025 -->
Insofar as exchange is a process that transfers commodities from hands in which they are not use-values to hands in which they become use-values, it is a social circulation of matter. The product of one form of useful labor (labor of a particular kind, such as tailoring or weaving, that makes a particular useful thing) replaces the product of another. Once a commodity has found a resting place where it can serve as a use-value, it falls out of the sphere of exchange and into the sphere of consumption. But only the sphere of exchange interests us at present. So we now have to consider exchange from the point of view of its form. We have to investigate the change of form, or metamorphosis, of commodities which brings about the social circulation of matter.

<!-- ch03-p026 -->
As a rule, this change of form is very poorly understood. Apart from unclear ideas about value itself, the cause is this: every change of form in a commodity results from the exchange of two commodities, an ordinary one and the money-commodity. If we keep in view only the material fact that a commodity has been exchanged for gold, we overlook the very thing we ought to observe, namely what has happened to the form of the commodity. We overlook the facts that gold, when it is a mere commodity, is not money, and that when other commodities express their prices in gold, this gold is only the money form of those commodities themselves.

<!-- ch03-p027 -->
To begin with, commodities enter the process of exchange just as they are. The process then splits them into commodities and money. It thus produces an external opposition that corresponds to the internal opposition built into them, as being at once use-values and values. Commodities as use-values now stand opposed to money as exchange-value. On the other hand, both opposing sides are commodities, unities of use-value and value. But this unity of differences shows itself at two opposite poles, and at each pole in an opposite way. Being poles, they are as necessarily opposite as they are connected. On one side of the equation we have an ordinary commodity, which is in reality a use-value. Its value is expressed only ideally, in its price. Through its price it is equated to its opponent, the gold, as to the real embodiment of its value. On the other side, the gold, in its metallic reality, counts as the embodiment of value, as money. Gold, as gold, is exchange-value itself. As for its use-value, that exists only ideally. It is represented by the series of expressions of relative value in which gold stands face to face with all other commodities, and the sum of the uses of all these commodities makes up the sum of the various uses of gold. These antagonistic forms of commodities are the real forms in which the process of their exchange moves and takes place.

<!-- ch03-p028 -->
Let us now go with the owner of some commodity, say our old friend the linen weaver, to the scene of action, the market. His 20 yards of linen has a definite price, £2. He exchanges it for the £2. Then, being a man of the good old sort, he parts with the £2 for a family Bible of the same price. In his eyes the linen is a mere commodity, a container of value. He gives it up in exchange for gold, which is the linen’s form of value, and he in turn parts with this form for another commodity, the Bible. The Bible is destined to enter his house as an object of utility and of moral improvement for his household. The exchange is completed by two metamorphoses of opposite yet complementary character: the conversion of the commodity into money, and the reconversion of the money into a commodity.[^16] The two phases of this metamorphosis are both separate transactions of the weaver: selling, or the exchange of the commodity for money; buying, or the exchange of the money for a commodity; and the unity of the two acts, selling in order to buy.

Lassalle: “Die Philosophie Herakleitos des Dunkeln.” Berlin, 1858, Vol. I, p. 222.) In his note on this passage, p. 224, n. 3., Lassalle wrongly makes gold a mere symbol of value.

<!-- ch03-p029 -->
For the weaver, the result of the whole transaction is this: instead of the linen, he now has the Bible. Instead of his original commodity, he now owns another of the same value but of a different usefulness. In the same way he obtains his other means of subsistence (the food, clothing, housing, and other necessities a person needs to live) and means of production (the tools, machines, buildings, and raw materials used in producing things). From his point of view, the whole process does nothing more than exchange the product of his labor for the product of someone else’s, nothing more than an exchange of products.

<!-- ch03-p030 -->
The exchange of commodities is therefore accompanied by the following changes in their form.

<!-- ch03-p031 -->
Commodity – Money – Commodity.\
C–––––– M ––––––C.

<!-- ch03-p032 -->
As far as the objects themselves are concerned, the result of the whole process is C – C, the exchange of one commodity for another, the circulation of materialized social labor. When this result is reached, the process is at an end.

##### C – M. *First metamorphosis, or sale*

<!-- ch03-p033 -->
The leap that value takes from the body of the commodity into the body of the gold is, as I have called it elsewhere, the salto mortale [death-defying leap] of the commodity. If the leap falls short, the commodity itself is not harmed, but its owner decidedly is. The social division of labor makes his labor as one-sided as his wants are many-sided. This is exactly why the product of his labor serves him only as exchange-value. But it cannot take on the properties of a socially recognized universal equivalent except by being converted into money. That money, however, is in someone else’s pocket. To coax the money out of that pocket, our friend’s commodity must, above all, be a use-value to the owner of the money. For this, the labor spent on it must be of a socially useful kind, a kind that forms a branch of the social division of labor. But division of labor is a system of production which has grown up spontaneously and goes on growing behind the backs of the producers. The commodity to be exchanged may possibly be the product of some new kind of labor that claims to satisfy newly arisen needs, or even to create new needs itself. A particular operation may yesterday, perhaps, have been one of the many operations carried out by one producer in making a given commodity. Today it may separate itself from this connection, set itself up as an independent branch of labor, and send its unfinished product to market as an independent commodity. The circumstances may or may not be ripe for such a separation. Today the product satisfies a social want. Tomorrow some other suitable product may wholly or partly take the article’s place. Moreover, our weaver’s labor may be a recognized branch of the social division of labor, but that fact is by no means enough to guarantee the usefulness of his 20 yards of linen. The community’s want of linen has a limit, like every other want. If it is already saturated by the products of rival weavers, our friend’s product is superfluous, redundant, and therefore useless. People do not look a gift horse in the mouth, but our friend does not go to market to hand out presents. But suppose his product turns out to be a real use-value, and so attracts money? The question arises: how much will it attract? No doubt the answer is already anticipated in the price of the article, the expression of its magnitude of value. Here we leave out of account any accidental miscalculation of value by our friend, a mistake that the market soon corrects. We suppose that he has spent on his product only the amount of labor-time that is, on average, socially necessary. The price, then, is merely the money name of the quantity of social labor realized in his commodity. But without our weaver’s leave, and behind his back, the old-fashioned way of weaving changes. The labor-time that yesterday was without doubt socially necessary to produce a yard of linen is no longer so today. The owner of the money is only too eager to prove this fact from the prices quoted by our friend’s competitors. Unluckily for him, weavers are not few and far between. Lastly, suppose that every piece of linen on the market contains no more labor-time than is socially necessary. Even so, all these pieces taken as a whole may have had superfluous labor-time spent on them. If the market cannot stomach the whole quantity at the normal price of 2 shillings a yard, this proves that too large a part of the community’s total labor has been spent in the form of weaving. The effect is the same as if each individual weaver had spent more labor-time on his particular product than is socially necessary. Here we may say, with the German proverb: caught together, hung together. All the linen on the market counts only as one article of trade, of which each piece is only a fractional part. And as a matter of fact, the value of each single yard, too, is only the materialized form of the same definite and socially fixed quantity of uniform human labor.[^17]

[^17]: *Note by the Institute of Marxism-Leninism in the Russian edition*. — In his letter of November 28, 1878, to N. F. Danielson (Nikolai-on), Marx proposed that this sentence be corrected to read as follows: “And, as a matter of fact, the value of each single yard is but the materialised form of a part of the social labour expended on the whole number of yards.” A similar correction was made in a copy of the second German edition of the first volume of “Capital” that belonged to Marx; however, it is not in his handwriting.

<!-- ch03-p034 -->
So we see that commodities are in love with money, but “the course of true love never did run smooth.” The quantitative division of labor comes about in exactly the same spontaneous and accidental way as its qualitative division. The owners of commodities therefore find out that the same division of labor that turns them into independent private producers also frees the social process of production, and the relations of the individual producers to each other within that process, from all dependence on the will of those producers. They also find out that the seeming independence of the individuals from one another is supplemented by a system of general and mutual dependence through, or by means of, the products.

<!-- ch03-p035 -->
The division of labor turns the product of labor into a commodity, and so makes it necessary for that product to be further turned into money. At the same time, it also makes it quite a matter of chance whether this transubstantiation is accomplished. Here, however, we are concerned only with the phenomenon in its complete form, and so we assume that it proceeds normally. Moreover, if the conversion takes place at all, that is, if the commodity is not absolutely unsellable, its metamorphosis does take place, although the price realized may be abnormally above or below the value.

<!-- ch03-p036 -->
The seller has his commodity replaced by gold; the buyer has his gold replaced by a commodity. The fact that stares us in the face here is that a commodity and gold, 20 yards of linen and £2, have changed hands and places, in other words, that they have been exchanged. But for what is the commodity exchanged? For the shape taken by its own value, for the universal equivalent. And for what is the gold exchanged? For a particular form of its own use-value. Why does gold take the form of money when it faces the linen? Because the linen’s price of £2, its name in money, has already equated the linen to gold in its character as money. A commodity strips off its original commodity form at the moment it is given up, that is, at the instant its use-value actually attracts the gold that before existed only ideally in its price. So the realization of a commodity’s price, or of its ideal form of value, is at the same time the realization of the ideal use-value of money. The conversion of a commodity into money is at the same time the conversion of money into a commodity. The apparently single process is in reality a double one. From the pole of the commodity owner it is a sale; from the opposite pole of the money owner it is a purchase. In other words, a sale is a purchase, C–M is also M–C.[^18]

[^18]: “Toute vente est achat.” [“Every sale is a purchase.”] (Dr. Quesnay: “Dialogues sur le Commerce et les Travaux des Artisans.” Physiocrates ed. Daire I. Partie, Paris, 1846, p. 170), or, as Quesnay puts it in his “Maximes générales,” “Vendre est acheter.” [“To sell is to buy.”]

<!-- ch03-p037 -->
So far we have considered people in only one economic capacity, that of owners of commodities. In this capacity they take possession of the produce of other people’s labor by giving up the produce of their own. So for one commodity owner to meet another who has money, one of two things is necessary. Either the product of the latter person’s labor (the buyer’s) must itself be money, must be gold, the material money consists of; or his product must already have changed its skin and stripped off its original form of a useful object. To play the part of money, gold must of course enter the market at some point or other. This point is found at the source where the metal is produced. There gold is bartered, as the immediate product of labor, for some other product of equal value. From that moment on, it always represents the realized price of some commodity.[^19] Apart from its exchange for other commodities at the source of its production, gold, in whoever’s hands it may be, is the transformed shape of some commodity that its owner has given up; it is the product of a sale, or of the first metamorphosis C–M.[^20] Gold, as we saw, became ideal money, or a measure of values, because all commodities measured their values by it. They thus set it ideally against their natural shape as useful objects, and made it the shape of their value. It became real money through the general giving-up of commodities, by actually changing places with their natural forms as useful objects, and so becoming in reality the embodiment of their values. When commodities take on this money shape, they strip off every trace of their natural use-value and of the particular kind of labor that created them. They do so in order to turn themselves into the uniform, socially recognized incarnation of human labor that is all of one kind. We cannot tell from the mere look of a piece of money what particular commodity it has been exchanged for. In their money form all commodities look alike. So money may be dirt, although dirt is not money. Let us assume that the two gold pieces for which our weaver has parted with his linen are the metamorphosed shape of a quarter of wheat. The sale of the linen, C–M, is at the same time its purchase, M–C. But the sale is the first act of a process that ends with a transaction of the opposite kind, namely the purchase of a Bible. The purchase of the linen, on the other hand, ends a movement that began with a transaction of the opposite kind, namely the sale of the wheat. C–M (linen–money), which is the first phase of C–M–C (linen–money–Bible), is also M–C (money–linen), the last phase of another movement C–M–C (wheat–money–linen). So the first metamorphosis of one commodity, its change from a commodity into money, is also always the second metamorphosis of some other commodity, its change back from money into a commodity.[^21]

Daire II. Partie, p. 554.)

[^20]: “Pour avoir cet argent, il faut avoir vendu,” [“In order to have this money, one must have made a sale,”] cited above, p. 543.

[^21]: As remarked before, the actual producer of gold or silver is an exception. He exchanges his product directly for another commodity, without having first sold it.

##### M–C, *or purchase.* *The second and concluding metamorphosis of a commodity*

<!-- ch03-p038 -->
Because money is the metamorphosed shape of all other commodities, the result of their being given up in general, it can itself be given up without restriction or condition. It reads all prices backwards, and so, as it were, depicts itself in the bodies of all other commodities, which offer it the material for realizing its own use-value. At the same time the prices, wooing glances cast at money by commodities, set the limits of its convertibility by pointing to its quantity. Since every commodity disappears as a commodity on becoming money, it is impossible to tell from the money itself how it got into the hands of its owner, or what article has been changed into it. Non olet [It does not smell], from whatever source it may come. Representing on the one hand a sold commodity, it represents on the other a commodity to be bought.[^22]

[^22]: “Si l’argent représente, dans nos mains, les choses que nous pouvons désirer d’acheter, il y représente aussi les choses que nous avons vendues pour cet argent.” [“If money represents, in our hands, the things we may wish to buy, it also represents the things we have sold to get that money”] (Mercier de la Rivière, cited above, p. 586.)

<!-- ch03-p039 -->
M–C, a purchase, is at the same time C–M, a sale; the concluding metamorphosis of one commodity is the first metamorphosis of another. As far as our weaver is concerned, the life of his commodity ends with the Bible, into which he has turned his £2 back. But suppose the seller of the Bible turns the £2 set free by the weaver into brandy: then M–C, the concluding phase of C–M–C (linen–money–Bible), is also C–M, the first phase of C–M–C (Bible–money–brandy). The producer of a particular commodity has only that one article to offer. He very often sells it in large quantities, but his many and various wants force him to split up the price he has realized, the sum of money set free, into numerous purchases. So one sale leads to many purchases of various articles. The concluding metamorphosis of a commodity thus makes up a collection of first metamorphoses of various other commodities.

<!-- ch03-p040 -->
If we now consider the completed metamorphosis of a commodity as a whole, it appears, in the first place, that it is made up of two opposite and complementary movements, C–M and M–C. These two opposed transmutations of a commodity are brought about by two opposed social acts on the part of the owner, and these acts in turn stamp the character of the economic parts he plays. As the person who makes a sale, he is a seller; as the person who makes a purchase, he is a buyer. But in every such transmutation of a commodity, its two forms, commodity form and money form, exist at the same time but at opposite poles. In just the same way, every seller has a buyer facing him, and every buyer a seller. While one particular commodity goes through its two transmutations one after the other, from a commodity into money and from money into another commodity, the owner of the commodity changes his part in turn from that of seller to that of buyer. So these characters of seller and buyer are not permanent. They attach themselves in turn to the various persons involved in the circulation of commodities.

<!-- ch03-p041 -->
In its simplest form, the complete metamorphosis of a commodity involves four extremes and three characters. First, a commodity comes face to face with money. Money is the form taken by the commodity’s value, and it exists, in all its hard reality, in the buyer’s pocket. A commodity owner is thus brought into contact with an owner of money. Now, as soon as the commodity has been changed into money, the money becomes its passing equivalent form (in “20 yards of linen = 1 coat”, the coat serves as the material that expresses the linen’s value: it is in the equivalent form). The use-value of this equivalent form is to be found in the bodies of other commodities. Money, the final term of the first transmutation, is at the same time the starting point for the second. So the person who is a seller in the first transaction becomes a buyer in the second, in which a third commodity owner appears on the scene as a seller.[^23]

[^23]: “Il y a donc ... quatre termes et trois contractants, dont l’un intervient deux fois” [“So there are ... four terms and three contracting parties, one of whom intervenes twice”] (Le Trosne, cited above, p. 909.)

<!-- ch03-p042 -->
The two phases that make up the metamorphosis of a commodity, each the reverse of the other, together form a circular movement, a circuit: the commodity form, the stripping off of this form, and the return to the commodity form. No doubt the commodity appears here in two different aspects. At the starting point it is not a use-value to its owner; at the finishing point it is. In the same way, the money appears in the first phase as a solid crystal of value, a crystal into which the commodity eagerly solidifies. In the second phase it dissolves into the mere passing equivalent form, destined to be replaced by a use-value.

<!-- ch03-p043 -->
The two metamorphoses that make up the circuit are at the same time two reverse partial metamorphoses of two other commodities. One and the same commodity, the linen, opens the series of its own metamorphoses and completes the metamorphosis of another (the wheat). In the first phase, or sale, the linen plays these two parts in its own person. But then, changed into gold, it completes its own second and final metamorphosis, and at the same time helps to carry out the first metamorphosis of a third commodity. So the circuit made by one commodity in the course of its metamorphoses is inseparably tangled up with the circuits of other commodities. The total of all the different circuits makes up *the circulation of commodities*.

<!-- ch03-p044 -->
The circulation of commodities differs from the direct exchange of products (barter) not only in form but in substance. Just consider the course of events. The weaver has, as a matter of fact, exchanged his linen for a Bible, his own commodity for someone else’s. But this is true only as far as he himself is concerned. The seller of the Bible, who prefers something to warm his insides, no more thought of exchanging his Bible for linen than our weaver knew that wheat had been exchanged for his linen. B’s commodity replaces A’s, but A and B do not exchange those commodities with each other. It may, of course, happen that A and B buy from each other at the same time; but such exceptional transactions are by no means the necessary result of the general conditions of the circulation of commodities. Here we see, on the one hand, how the exchange of commodities breaks through all the local and personal limits that are inseparable from direct barter, and develops the circulation of the products of social labor. On the other hand, we see how it develops a whole network of social relations that grow up spontaneously and are entirely beyond the control of the actors. It is only because the farmer has sold his wheat that the weaver is able to sell his linen; only because the weaver has sold his linen that our Hotspur is able to sell his Bible; and only because Hotspur has sold the water of everlasting life that the distiller is able to sell his *eau-de-vie* [brandy; literally “water of life”], and so on.

<!-- ch03-p045 -->
So the process of circulation does not, like the direct barter of products, die out once the use-values have changed places and hands. The money does not vanish when it drops out of the circuit of the metamorphosis of a given commodity. It is constantly being deposited in new places in the arena of circulation that other commodities have left empty. In the complete metamorphosis of the linen, for example, linen – money – Bible, the linen first falls out of circulation, and money steps into its place. Then the Bible falls out of circulation, and again money takes its place. When one commodity replaces another, the money-commodity always sticks to the hands of some third person.[^24] Circulation sweats money from every pore.

[^24]: Self-evident as this may be, political economists (the writers on political economy, the science of economics as it was then called) for the most part fail to notice it, and the “Free-trader Vulgaris” especially.

<!-- ch03-p046 -->
Nothing can be more childish than the dogma that, because every sale is a purchase and every purchase a sale, the circulation of commodities necessarily implies a balance of sales and purchases. If this means that the number of actual sales equals the number of purchases, it is mere tautology. But its real purpose is to prove that every seller brings his buyer to market with him. Nothing of the kind. The sale and the purchase are one identical act, an exchange between a commodity owner and an owner of money, between two persons as opposed to each other as the two poles of a magnet. When they are performed by one single person, they form two distinct acts, of polar and opposite character. So the identity of sale and purchase implies that the commodity is useless if, once thrown into the alchemist’s retort of circulation, it does not come out again in the shape of money; in other words, if its owner cannot sell it, and the owner of the money therefore cannot buy it. That identity further implies that the exchange, if it does take place, forms a period of rest, an interval, long or short, in the life *of* the commodity. Since the first metamorphosis of a commodity is at once a sale and a purchase, it is also an independent process in itself. The purchaser has the commodity, the seller has the money, that is, a commodity ready to go into circulation at any time. No one can sell unless someone else buys. But no one is immediately bound to buy just because he has just sold. Circulation bursts through all the limits of time, place, and individuals imposed by direct barter. It does this by splitting into the opposition of a sale and a purchase the direct identity that does exist in barter between giving up one’s own product and acquiring someone else’s. To say that these two independent and opposed acts have an inner unity, that they are essentially one, is the same as saying that this inner oneness expresses itself in an external opposition. If the interval in time between the two complementary phases of the complete metamorphosis of a commodity becomes too great, if the split between the sale and the purchase becomes too pronounced, the close connection between them, their oneness, asserts itself by producing – a crisis. The opposition of use-value and value; the contradictions that private labor has to show itself as direct social labor, and that a particularized concrete kind of labor has to count as abstract human labor (labor counted simply as the spending of human effort, of brain, muscle, and nerve, whatever particular kind it is); the contradiction between the personification of objects and the representation of persons by things: all these oppositions and contradictions, which are built into commodities, assert themselves and develop their modes of motion in the opposed phases of the metamorphosis of a commodity. So these modes imply the possibility, and no more than the possibility, of crises. The conversion of this mere possibility into a reality is the result of a long series of relations that, from our present standpoint of simple circulation, do not yet exist.[^25]

[^25]: See my remarks on James Mill in “Zur Kritik, &c.,” pp. 74-76. On this subject, we may note two methods typical of apologetic economics (the economics of the defenders of the system). The first is to identify the circulation of commodities with the direct barter of products, simply by leaving out the points in which they differ. The second is the attempt to explain away the contradictions of capitalist production by reducing the relations between the persons engaged in that mode of production to the simple relations that arise out of the circulation of commodities. But the production and circulation of commodities are phenomena that occur, to a greater or lesser extent, in the most diverse modes of production. If we know nothing but the abstract categories of circulation, which are common to all these modes of production, we cannot possibly know anything of the specific points in which those modes differ, nor pass any judgment on them. In no science is such a big fuss made over commonplace truisms as in political economy. For instance, J. B. Say sets himself up as a judge of crises because, if you please, he knows that a commodity is a product.
