### Section 3: Separation of Surplus-Value into Capital and Revenue. The Abstinence Theory

<!-- ch24-p046 -->
In the last chapter, we treated surplus-value (or the surplus-product) only as a fund for supplying the capitalist's individual consumption. In this chapter we have so far treated it only as a fund for accumulation. But it is neither the one nor the other alone: it is both together. The capitalist consumes one portion as revenue;[^19] the other is used as capital, that is, it is accumulated.

[^19]: The reader will notice that the word revenue is used in a double sense. First, it means surplus-value insofar as it is the fruit that capital yields periodically. Second, it means the part of that fruit which the capitalist periodically consumes, or adds to the fund that supplies his private consumption. I have kept this double meaning because it fits the language of the English and French economists.

<!-- ch24-p047 -->
So, given the mass of surplus-value, the larger one of these parts is, the smaller the other is. Other things being equal, the ratio between these parts determines the size of the accumulation. But it is the owner of the surplus-value, the capitalist alone, who makes the division. It is his deliberate act. The part of the tribute he extracts which he accumulates is said to be saved by him, because he does not eat it, that is, because he performs the function of a capitalist and enriches himself.

<!-- ch24-p048 -->
Except as personified capital, the capitalist has no historical value, and no right to that historical existence which, to use an expression of the witty Lichnowsky, “hasn’t got no date.” And only to that extent is the necessity of his own passing existence contained in the passing necessity of the capitalist mode of production. But insofar as he is personified capital, what drives him to act is not use-values and the enjoyment of them, but exchange-value and its increase. He is fanatically bent on making value expand itself, so he ruthlessly forces the human race to produce for production’s sake. In this way he forces the development of the productive powers of society. He creates those material conditions which alone can form the real basis of a higher form of society, a society whose ruling principle is the full and free development of every individual. Only as personified capital is the capitalist respectable. As such, he shares with the miser the passion for wealth as wealth. But what in the miser is a mere personal quirk is, in the capitalist, the effect of the social mechanism, of which he is only one of the wheels. Moreover, the development of capitalist production makes it constantly necessary to keep increasing the amount of capital laid out in a given industrial undertaking. And competition makes each individual capitalist feel the built-in laws of capitalist production as external laws that coerce him. It compels him to keep extending his capital constantly in order to preserve it, but he cannot extend it except by progressive accumulation.

<!-- ch24-p049 -->
So, insofar as his actions are a mere function of capital – capital which, in his person, is endowed with consciousness and a will – his own private consumption is a robbery committed against accumulation. In the same way, in double-entry book-keeping, the capitalist’s private spending is placed on the debit side of his account, against his capital. To accumulate is to conquer the world of social wealth, to increase the mass of human beings he exploits, and so to extend both the direct and the indirect rule of the capitalist.[^20]

[^20]: Taking as his text the usurer, that old-fashioned but ever-renewed specimen of the capitalist, Luther shows very aptly that the love of power is an element in the desire to get rich. “The heathen were able, by the light of reason, to conclude that a usurer is a double-dyed thief and murderer. We Christians, however, hold them in such honour, that we fairly worship them for the sake of their money.... Whoever eats up, robs, and steals the nourishment of another, that man commits as great a murder (so far as in him lies) as he who starves a man or utterly undoes him. Such does a usurer, and sits the while safe on his stool, when he ought rather to be hanging on the gallows, and be eaten by as many ravens as he has stolen guilders, if only there were so much flesh on him, that so many ravens could stick their beaks in and share it. Meanwhile, we hang the small thieves.... Little thieves are put in the stocks, great thieves go flaunting in gold and silk.... Therefore is there, on this earth, no greater enemy of man (after the devil) than a gripe-money [miser], and usurer, for he wants to be God over all men. Turks, soldiers, and tyrants are also bad men, yet must they let the people live, and confess that they are bad, and enemies, and do, nay [indeed], must, now and then show pity to some. But a usurer and money-glutton, such a one would have the whole world perish of hunger and thirst, misery and want, so far as in him lies, so that he may have all to himself, and every one may receive from him as from a God, and be his serf for ever. To wear fine cloaks, golden chains, rings, to wipe his mouth, to be deemed and taken for a worthy, pious man .... Usury is a great huge monster, like a werewolf, who lays waste all, more than any Cacus, Gerion or Antus. And yet decks himself out, and would be thought pious, so that people may not see where the oxen have gone, that he drags backwards into his den. But Hercules shall hear the cry of the oxen and of his prisoners, and shall seek Cacus even in cliffs and among rocks, and shall set the oxen loose again from the villain. For Cacus means the villain that is a pious usurer, and steals, robs, eats everything. And will not own that he has done it, and thinks no one will find him out, because the oxen, drawn backwards into his den, make it seem, from their foot-prints, that they have been let out. So the usurer would deceive the world, as though he were of use and gave the world oxen, which he, however, rends, and eats all alone... And since we break on the wheel, and behead highwaymen, murderers and housebreakers, how much more ought we to break on the wheel and kill.... hunt down, curse and behead all usurers.” (Martin Luther, cited above.)

<!-- ch24-p050 -->
But original sin is at work everywhere. As capitalist production, accumulation, and wealth develop, the capitalist stops being the mere incarnation of capital. He has a fellow-feeling for his own Adam, and his education gradually lets him smile at the passion for self-denial as a mere prejudice of the old-fashioned miser. The capitalist of the classical type condemns individual consumption as a sin against his function, as “abstinence” from accumulating. The modernized capitalist, by contrast, is capable of seeing accumulation as “abstinence” from pleasure.

<!-- ch24-p051 -->
> “Two souls, alas, do dwell within his breast;\
> The one is ever parting from the other.”[^21]

[^21]: See Goethe’s “Faust.”

<!-- ch24-p052 -->
At the historical dawn of capitalist production – and every capitalist upstart has to go through this historical stage personally – greed and the desire to get rich are the ruling passions. But the progress of capitalist production does not only create a world of delights. In speculation and the credit system, it also opens up a thousand sources of sudden enrichment. Once a certain stage of development has been reached, a conventional degree of extravagance, which is also a display of wealth and so a source of credit, becomes a business necessity for the “unfortunate” capitalist. Luxury becomes part of capital’s costs of putting on a show. Moreover, the capitalist does not get rich as the miser does, in proportion to his own personal labor and restricted consumption. He gets rich at the same rate as he squeezes out the labor-power of others and forces on the worker abstinence from all of life’s enjoyments. So the capitalist’s extravagance never has the genuine character of the open-handed feudal lord’s extravagance. On the contrary, behind it there always lurk the most sordid greed and the most anxious calculation. Even so, his spending grows with his accumulation, without the one necessarily restricting the other. But along with this growth, there develops at the same time in his breast a Faustian conflict between the passion for accumulation and the desire for enjoyment.

<!-- ch24-p053 -->
Dr. Aikin says in a work published in 1795:

<!-- ch24-p054 -->
> “The trade of Manchester may be divided into four periods. First, when manufacturers were obliged to work hard for their livelihood.”

<!-- ch24-p055 -->
They enriched themselves chiefly by robbing the parents whose children were bound to them as apprentices. The parents paid a high fee, while the apprentices were starved. On the other hand, average profits were low, and accumulating required extreme thrift. They lived like misers and were far from consuming even the interest on their capital.

<!-- ch24-p056 -->
> “The second period, when they had begun to acquire little fortunes, but worked as hard as before,” – for direct exploitation of labor costs labor, as every slave-driver knows – “and lived in as plain a manner as before.... The third, when luxury began, and the trade was pushed by sending out riders [traveling salesmen] for orders into every market town in the Kingdom.... It is probable that few or no capitals of £3,000 to £4,000 acquired by trade existed here before 1690. However, about that time, or a little later, the traders had got money beforehand [had money saved up], and began to build modern brick houses, instead of those of wood and plaster.”

<!-- ch24-p057 -->
Even in the early part of the 18th century, a Manchester manufacturer who set a pint of foreign wine before his guests exposed himself to the remarks and head-shaking of all his neighbors. Before the rise of machinery, a manufacturer’s evening spending at the pub where they all met never went beyond sixpence for a glass of punch and a penny for a twist of tobacco. Not until 1758, and this marks an epoch, was a person actually engaged in business seen with a carriage of his own.

<!-- ch24-p058 -->
> “The fourth period,” the last 30 years of the 18th century, “is that in which expense and luxury have made great progress, and was supported by a trade extended by means of riders and factors [agents] through every part of Europe.”[^22]

[^22]: Dr. Aikin: “Description of the Country from 30 to 40 miles round Manchester.” London, 1795, p. 182, and following.

<!-- ch24-p059 -->
What would the good Dr. Aikin say if he could rise from his grave and see the Manchester of today?

<!-- ch24-p060 -->
Accumulate, accumulate! That is Moses and the prophets! “Industry furnishes the material which saving accumulates.”[^23] Therefore save, save, that is, turn as much as possible of the surplus-value, or surplus-product, back into capital! Accumulation for accumulation’s sake, production for production’s sake: in this formula classical economics expressed the historical mission of the bourgeoisie, and it did not for a single instant fool itself about the birth-pangs of wealth.[^24] But what is the use of lamenting in the face of historical necessity? If, to classical economics, the proletarian – a member of the proletariat, the class of wage workers, who own nothing they can live by except their labor-power – is only a machine for producing surplus-value, then, on the other hand, the capitalist too is in its eyes only a machine for turning this surplus-value into additional capital. Political economy takes the historical function of the capitalist in bitter earnest. Malthus wanted to charm out of the capitalist’s breast the awful conflict between the desire for enjoyment and the chase after riches. So, around the year 1820, he argued for a division of labor that assigns the business of accumulating to the capitalist actually engaged in production, and the business of spending to the other sharers in surplus-value: to the landlords, the holders of government posts, the clergy with church incomes, etc. It is of the highest importance, he says,

[^24]: Even J. B. Say says: “Les épargnes des riches se font aux dépens des pauvres.” [the savings of the rich are made at the expense of the poor] “The Roman proletarian lived almost entirely at the expense of society.... It can almost be said that modern society lives at the expense of the proletarians, on what it keeps out of the remuneration of labour.” (Sismondi: “études, &c.,” t. i., p. 24.)

<!-- ch24-p061 -->
> “to keep separate the passion for expenditure and the passion for accumulation.”[^25]

[^25]: Malthus, cited above, pp. 319, 320.

The capitalists had long since become men of the world who liked to live well, and they raised a loud outcry. One of their spokesmen, a follower of Ricardo, cried out: What! Mr. Malthus preaches high rents, heavy taxes, etc., so that unproductive consumers can keep the spur constantly pressed into the side of the industrious! By all means production, production on an ever-growing scale, so goes the slogan; but

<!-- ch24-p063 -->
> “production will, by such a process, be held back far more than it is spurred on. Nor is it quite fair to keep a number of persons idle in this way, only to pinch others, who are likely, from their characters, if you can force them to work, to work with success.”[^26]

[^26]: “An Inquiry into those Principles Respecting the Nature of Demand, &c.,” p. 67.

Unfair as he finds it to spur on the industrial capitalist by taking the butter off his bread, he still thinks it necessary to cut the worker’s wages to a minimum “to keep him industrious.” Nor does he hide for a moment the fact that the appropriation of unpaid labor is the secret of surplus-value.

<!-- ch24-p065 -->
> “Increased demand on the part of the labourers means nothing more than their willingness to take less of their own product for themselves, and leave a greater part of it to their employers; and if it be said, that this begets glut [oversupply], by lessening consumption” (on the part of the workers), “I can only reply that glut is synonymous with large profits.”[^27]

[^27]: Cited above, p. 59.

<!-- ch24-p066 -->
The learned debate over how the booty pumped out of the worker might be divided between the industrial capitalist and the rich idler, with the most advantage to accumulation, fell silent in the face of the revolution of July (the 1830 revolution in France). Shortly afterwards, the town proletariat at Lyons rang the alarm bell of revolution, and the country proletariat in England began to set fire to farmyards and grain-stacks. On this side of the Channel Owenism began to spread; on the other side, St. Simonism and Fourierism. The hour of vulgar economics had struck. Vulgar economics means economists who only describe and defend the surface appearances of the system, without investigating its real relations. Exactly a year before Nassau W. Senior discovered at Manchester that the profit (including interest) of capital is the product of the last hour of the twelve, he had announced another discovery to the world.

<!-- ch24-p067 -->
> “I substitute,” he proudly says, “for the word capital, considered as an instrument of production, the word abstinence.”

<!-- ch24-p068 -->
Here is a matchless sample of the discoveries of vulgar economics! It puts a fawning phrase in the place of an economic category – *voilà tout* [that’s all].

<!-- ch24-p069 -->
> “When the savage,” says Senior, “makes bows, he exercises an industry, but he does not practise abstinence.”[^28]

[^28]: (Senior, “Principes fondamentaux del’Écon. Pol.” trad. Arrivabene. Paris, 1836, p. 308.) This was rather too much for the followers of the old classical school. “Mr. Senior has substituted for it” (the expression, labor and profit) “the expression labour and Abstinence. He who converts his revenue abstains from the enjoyment which its expenditure would afford him. It is not the capital, but the use of the capital productively, which is the cause of profits.” (John Cazenove, cited above, p. 130, Note.) John St. Mill, on the contrary, accepts Ricardo’s theory of profit on the one hand, and on the other hand takes over Senior’s “remuneration of abstinence.” He is as much at home in absurd contradictions as he feels at sea in the Hegelian contradiction, the source of all dialectic. It has never occurred to the vulgar economist to make the simple reflection that every human action may be viewed as “abstinence” from its opposite. Eating is abstinence from fasting, walking is abstinence from standing still, working is abstinence from idling, idling is abstinence from working, etc. These gentlemen would do well to ponder, once in a while, Spinoza’s “Determinatio est Negatio” [Determination is negation].

<!-- ch24-p070 -->
This explains how and why, in the earlier states of society, the implements of labor were made without abstinence on the part of the capitalist.

<!-- ch24-p071 -->
> “The more society progresses, the more abstinence is demanded,”[^29]

[^29]: Senior, cited above, p. 342.

<!-- ch24-p072 -->
Namely, from those who carry on the industry of appropriating the fruits of other people’s industry. All the conditions for carrying on the labor process are suddenly turned into so many acts of abstinence on the part of the capitalist. If the grain is not all eaten, but part of it is also sown – abstinence of the capitalist. If the wine is given time to mature – abstinence of the capitalist.[^30] The capitalist robs his own self whenever he “lends (!) the instruments of production to the labourer.” That is, he robs himself whenever he incorporates labor-power with them and uses them to extract surplus-value out of that labor-power, instead of eating them up, steam-engines, cotton, railways, manure, horses, and all; or, as the vulgar economist childishly puts it, instead of squandering “their value” on luxuries and other articles of consumption.[^31] How the capitalists as a class are to perform that feat is a secret that vulgar economics has so far stubbornly refused to reveal. Enough that the world still jogs on, solely through the self-punishment of this modern penitent of Vishnu, the capitalist. Not only accumulation, but the simple “conservation of a capital requires a constant effort to resist the temptation of consuming it.”[^32] So the simple dictates of humanity plainly require that the capitalist be released from this martyrdom and temptation, in the same way that the Georgian slave-owner was lately delivered, by the abolition of slavery, from his painful dilemma: whether to squander the surplus-product lashed out of his niggers entirely on champagne, or to turn part of it back into more niggers and more land.

Econ.,” edit. by A. Potter, New York, 1841, pp. 133-134.)

[^31]: “La privation que s’impose le capitalisté, en prêtant [The deprivation the capitalist imposes on himself by lending ...] (this euphemism is used in order to identify, by the approved method of vulgar economics, the worker who is exploited with the industrial capitalist who exploits, and to whom other capitalists lend money) ses instruments de production au travailleur, au lieu d’en consacrer la valeur à son propre usage, en la transforment en objets d’utilité ou d’agrément.” [his instruments of production to the worker, instead of devoting their value to his own use by turning it into useful or pleasant objects] (G. de Molinari, cited above, p. 36.)

[^32]: “La conservation d’un capital exige ... un effort constant pour résister a la tentation de le consommer.” [The conservation of a capital requires ... a constant effort to resist the temptation to consume it.] (Courcelle-Seneuil, cited above, p. 57.)

In economic forms of society of the most varied kinds, there is not only simple reproduction but also, in varying degrees, reproduction on an extended scale. Gradually more is produced and more is consumed, and so more products have to be turned into means of production. But this process does not appear as accumulation of capital, or as the function of a capitalist, as long as the worker’s means of production, and with them his product and means of subsistence, do not confront him in the shape of capital.[^33] Richard Jones, who died a few years ago and succeeded Malthus in the chair of political economy at Haileybury College, discusses this point well in the light of two important facts. Since the great mass of the Hindu population are peasants who cultivate their land themselves, their products, their instruments of labor and their means of subsistence never take “the shape of a fund saved from revenue, which fund has, therefore, gone through a previous process of accumulation.”[^34] On the other hand, in those provinces where English rule has least disturbed the old system, the non-agricultural workers are employed directly by the magnates, to whom a portion of the agricultural surplus-product is handed over as tribute or rent. The magnates consume one portion of this product in kind. The workers turn another portion, for the magnates’ use, into luxury articles and similar things. The rest forms the wages of the workers, who own their implements of labor. Here, production and reproduction on an extended scale go on their way without any intervention from that odd saint, that knight of the woeful countenance, the capitalist “abstainer.”

[^33]: “The particular classes of income which yield the most abundantly to the progress of national capital, change at different stages of their progress, and are, therefore, entirely different in nations occupying different positions in that progress.... Profits ... unimportant source of accumulation, compared with wages and rents, in the earlier stages of society.... When a considerable advance in the powers of national industry has actually taken place, profits rise into comparative importance as a source of accumulation.” (Richard Jones, “Textbook, &c.,” pp. 16, 21.)

[^34]: Cited above, p. 36, and following.
