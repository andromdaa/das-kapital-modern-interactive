### Section 5: The Strife Between Workman and Machine

<!-- ch15-p109 -->
The struggle between the capitalist and the wage worker goes back to the very origin of capital. It raged on throughout the whole manufacturing period.[^112] But only since machinery was introduced has the worker fought against the instrument of labor itself, the material embodiment of capital. He revolts against this particular form of the means of production, seeing it as the material basis of the capitalist mode of production.

[^112]: See, among others, John Houghton: “Husbandry and Trade Improved.” London, 1727. “The Advantages of the East India Trade, 1720.” John Bellers, cited above. “The masters and their workmen are, unhappily, in a perpetual war with each other. The invariable object of the former is to get their work done as cheaply as possible; and they do not fail to employ every artifice to this purpose, whilst the latter are equally attentive to every occasion of distressing their masters into a compliance with higher demands.” (“An Enquiry into the Causes of the Present High Price of Provisions,” pp. 61-62. The author, the Rev. Nathaniel Forster, is entirely on the side of the workers.)

<!-- ch15-p110 -->
In the 17th century, workers in nearly all of Europe revolted against the ribbon-loom, a machine for weaving ribbons and trimmings, called in Germany Bandmühle [ribbon mill], Schnurmühle [cord mill], and Mühlenstuhl [mill loom]. These machines were invented in Germany. Abbé Lancellotti, in a work that appeared in Venice in 1636 but was written in 1579, says the following:

<!-- ch15-p111 -->
> “Anthony Müller of Danzig saw about 50 years ago in that town, a very ingenious machine, which weaves 4 to 6 pieces at once. But the Mayor being apprehensive that this invention might throw a large number of workmen on the streets, caused the inventor to be secretly strangled or drowned.”

<!-- ch15-p112 -->
In Leyden this machine was not used until 1629. There the riots of the ribbon-weavers finally forced the Town Council to ban it.

<!-- ch15-p113 -->
> “In hac urbe,” says Boxhorn (Inst. Pol., 1663), referring to the introduction of this machine into Leyden, “ante hos viginti circiter annos instrumentum quidam invenerunt textorium, quo solus plus panni et facilius conficere poterat, quan plures aequali tempore. Hinc turbae ortae et querulae textorum, tandemque usus hujus instrumenti a magistratu prohibitus est.” [In this town, about twenty years ago, some people invented a weaving instrument with which one person alone could make more cloth, and more easily, than several people in the same time. From this arose disturbances and complaints by the weavers, until at last the Town Council banned the use of this instrument.]

<!-- ch15-p114 -->
In 1632, 1639, etc., the States General of Holland issued various decrees that more or less banned this loom. Finally, by the decree of the 15th December, 1661, they allowed it to be used under certain conditions. It was also banned in Cologne in 1676, at the same time that its introduction into England was causing disturbances among the workers. An imperial Edict of 19th Feb., 1685, forbade its use throughout all Germany. In Hamburg it was burnt in public by order of the Senate. The Emperor Charles VI., on 9th Feb., 1719, renewed the edict of 1685, and not until 1765 was its use openly allowed in the Electorate of Saxony. This machine, which shook Europe to its foundations, was in fact the forerunner of the mule and the power-loom, and of the industrial revolution of the 18th century. It let a totally inexperienced boy set the whole loom, with all its shuttles, in motion simply by moving a rod backwards and forwards. In its improved form it produced from 40 to 50 pieces at once.

<!-- ch15-p115 -->
About 1630, a wind-driven sawmill put up near London by a Dutchman fell victim to the violence of the crowd. Even as late as the beginning of the 18th century, sawmills driven by water overcame the opposition of the people, which Parliament supported, only with great difficulty. In 1758 Everet put up the first wool-shearing machine driven by water-power; no sooner was it built than it was set on fire by 100,000 people who had been thrown out of work. Fifty thousand workers who had until then lived by carding wool petitioned Parliament against Arkwright’s scribbling mills and carding engines. During the first 15 years of this century, the English manufacturing districts saw an enormous destruction of machinery, caused chiefly by the use of the power-loom and known as the Luddite movement. It gave the anti-Jacobin governments of a Sidmouth, a Castlereagh, and the like, a pretext for the most reactionary and forcible measures. It took both time and experience before the workers learned to distinguish between machinery and its use by capital, and to direct their attacks not against the material instruments of production, but against the way in which they are used.[^113]

[^113]: In old-fashioned branches of manufacture, the revolts of the workers against machinery, even to this day, sometimes take a savage form, as in the case of the Sheffield file cutters in 1865.

<!-- ch15-p116 -->
The struggles about wages in manufacture presuppose manufacture, and are in no sense directed against its existence. The opposition to setting up new manufacturing workshops comes from the guilds and the privileged towns, not from the workers. So the writers of the manufacturing period treat the division of labor mainly as a means of making up, in effect, for a shortage of workers, and not as a means of actually displacing those in work. This distinction is self-evident. Suppose someone says that 100 millions of people would be needed in England to spin, with the old spinning-wheel, the cotton that is now spun with mules by 500,000 people. This does not mean that the mules took the place of those millions, who never existed. It means only that many millions of workers would be needed to replace the spinning machinery. If, on the other hand, we say that in England the power-loom threw 800,000 weavers onto the streets, we do not mean existing machinery that would have to be replaced by a definite number of workers. We mean a number of weavers who existed and were actually replaced or displaced by the looms. During the manufacturing period, handicraft labor, though altered by the division of labor, was still the basis. The demands of the new colonial markets could not be met, because the town workers handed down from the middle ages were relatively few. And manufacture proper opened up new fields of production to the rural population, driven from the land by the break-up of the feudal system. At that time, therefore, the division of labor and cooperation in the workshops were seen more from their positive side: that they made the workers more productive.[^114] Long before the period of modern industry, cooperation and the concentration of the instruments of labor in the hands of a few gave rise to great, sudden and forcible revolutions in the modes of production, and as a result in the conditions of existence and the means of employment of the rural populations. This happened in numerous countries where these methods were applied in agriculture. But this struggle at first takes place more between the large and the small landowners than between capital and wage labor. On the other hand, when the workers are displaced by instruments of labor, by sheep, horses, etc., force is in this case used directly, in the first instance, as the prelude to the industrial revolution. First the workers are driven from the land, and then come the sheep. Land grabbing on a great scale, such as was carried out in England, is the first step in creating a field for setting up agriculture on a great scale.[^115] So this overturning of agriculture looks at first more like a political revolution.

[^114]: Sir James Steuart also understands machinery entirely in this sense. “Je considère donc les machines comme des moyens d’augmenter (virtuellement) le nombre des gens industrieux qu’on n’est pas obligé de nourrir.... En quoi l’effet d’une machine diffère-t-il de celui de nouveaux habitants?” [So I consider machines as means of increasing (virtually) the number of industrious people whom one is not obliged to feed.... In what way does the effect of a machine differ from that of new inhabitants?] (French trans. t. I., l. I., ch. XIX.) Petty is more naïve: he says it replaces “Polygamy.” The point of view above can be accepted, at the most, only for some parts of the United States. On the other hand: “machinery can seldom be used with success to abridge [shorten] the labour of an individual; more time would be lost in its construction than could be saved by its application. It is only really useful when it acts on great masses, when a single machine can assist the work of thousands. It is accordingly in the most populous countries, where there are most idle men, that it is most abundant.... It is not called into use by a scarcity of men, but by the facility with which they can be brought to work in masses.” (Piercy Ravenstone: “Thoughts on the Funding System and its Effects.” London, 1824, p. 45.)

Wherever large-scale agriculture exists in our country, and so particularly in the East, it has become possible only as a result of the clearing of the estates (“Bauernlegen”), a practice which became widespread in the 16th century, and especially so after 1648. — *F. The instrument of labor, once it takes the form of a machine, immediately becomes a competitor of the worker himself.[^116] From then on, the self-expansion of capital by means of machinery is directly proportional to the number of workers whose means of livelihood that machinery has destroyed. The whole system of capitalist production rests on the fact that the worker sells his labor-power as a commodity. Division of labor specializes this labor-power by reducing it to skill in handling a particular tool. As soon as handling this tool becomes the work of a machine, the use-value of the worker’s labor-power vanishes, and its exchange-value with it. The worker becomes unsaleable, like paper money taken out of currency by law. Machinery in this way makes part of the working class superfluous, that is, no longer immediately necessary for the self-expansion of capital. This part either goes under in the unequal contest of the old handicrafts and branches of manufacture with machinery, or else floods all the more easily entered branches of industry, swamps the labor-market, and pushes the price of labor-power below its value. The workers are told, as a great consolation, first, that their sufferings are only temporary (“a temporary inconvenience”), and secondly, that machinery takes over the whole of a given field of production only by degrees, so that the extent and intensity of its destructive effect is lessened. The first consolation cancels out the second. When machinery takes over an industry by degrees, it produces chronic misery among the factory workers who compete with it. Where the change is rapid, the effect is acute and felt by great masses. History shows no tragedy more horrible than the gradual dying out of the English hand-loom weavers, a dying out that stretched over several decades and was finally sealed in 1838. Many of them died of starvation; many, with families, barely survived for a long time on 2½ d. a day.[^117] On the other hand, the English cotton machinery had an acute effect in India. The Governor General reported in 1834-35:

> “The misery hardly finds a parallel in the history of commerce. The bones of the cotton-weavers are bleaching the plains of India.”

<!-- ch15-p119 -->
No doubt, in turning them out of this “temporal” world, the machinery caused them no more than “a temporary inconvenience.” For the rest, since machinery is constantly seizing on new fields of production, its temporary effect is really permanent. The capitalist mode of production as a whole gives the instruments of labor and the product a character of independence and estrangement in relation to the worker. So, by means of machinery, this character is developed into a thorough antagonism.[^118] That is why it is with the arrival of machinery that the worker for the first time revolts brutally against the instruments of labor.

[^118]: “The same cause which may increase the revenue of the country” (that is, as Ricardo explains in the same passage, the revenues of landlords and capitalists, whose wealth, from the economic point of view, forms the Wealth of the Nation), “may at the same time render the population redundant and deteriorate the condition of the labourer.” (Ricardo, cited above, p. 469.) “The constant aim and the tendency of every improvement in machinery is, in fact, to do away entirely with the labour of man, or to lessen its price by substituting the labour of women and children for that of grown-up men, or of unskilled for that of skilled workmen.” (Ure, cited above, t. I., p. 35.)

<!-- ch15-p120 -->
The instrument of labor strikes down the worker. This direct antagonism between the two shows most strongly wherever newly introduced machinery competes with handicrafts or branches of manufacture handed down from former times. But even in modern industry, the constant improvement of machinery and the development of the automatic system have a similar effect.

<!-- ch15-p121 -->
> “The object of improved machinery is to diminish manual labour, to provide for the performance of a process or the completion of a link in a manufacture by the aid of an iron instead of the human apparatus.”[^119] “The adaptation of power to machinery heretofore [until now] moved by hand, is almost of daily occurrence ... the minor improvements in machinery having for their object economy of power, the production of better work, the turning off more work in the same time, or in supplying the place of a child, a female, or a man, are constant, and although sometimes apparently of no great moment, have somewhat important results.”[^120] “Whenever a process requires peculiar dexterity and steadiness of hand, it is withdrawn, as soon as possible, from the cunning workman, who is prone to irregularities of many kinds, and it is placed in charge of a peculiar mechanism, so self-regulating that a child can superintend it.”[^121] “On the automatic plan skilled labour gets progressively superseded.”[^122] “The effect of improvements in machinery, not merely in superseding the necessity for the employment of the same quantity of adult labour as before, in order to produce a given result, but in substituting one description of human labour for another, the less skilled for the more skilled, juvenile for adult, female for male, causes a fresh disturbance in the rate of wages.”[^123] “The effect of substituting the self-acting mule for the common mule, is to discharge the greater part of the men spinners, and to retain adolescents and children.”[^124]

The factory system has an extraordinary power of expansion, owing to accumulated practical experience, to the mechanical means at hand, and to constant technical progress. We saw this proved by the giant strides the system made under the pressure of a shortened working day. But who, in 1860, the peak year of the English cotton industry, would have dreamt of the galloping improvements in machinery, and the matching displacement of working people, that the American Civil War spurred on during the following 3 years? A couple of examples from the Reports of the Inspectors of Factories will be enough on this point. A Manchester manufacturer states:

<!-- ch15-p123 -->
> “We formerly had 75 carding engines, now we have 12, doing the same quantity of work.... We are doing with fewer hands by 14, at a saving in wages of £10 a-week. Our estimated saving in waste is about 10% in the quantity of cotton consumed.” “In another fine-spinning mill in Manchester, I was informed that through increased speed and the adoption of some self-acting processes, a reduction had been made, in number, of a fourth in one department, and of above half in another, and that the introduction of the combing machine in place of the second carding, had considerably reduced, the number of hands formerly employed in the carding-room.”

<!-- ch15-p124 -->
Another spinning mill is estimated to make a saving of labor of 10%. The Messrs. Gilmour, spinners at Manchester, state: “In our blowing-room department we consider our expense with new machinery is fully one-third less in wages and hands ... in the jack-frame and drawing-frame room, about one-third less in expense, and likewise one-third less in hands; in the spinning room about one-third less in expenses. But this is not all; when our yarn goes to the manufacturers, it is so much better by the application of our new machinery, that they will produce a greater quantity of cloth, and cheaper than from the yarn produced by old machinery.”[^125] Mr. Redgrave adds in the same Report:

[^125]: “Rep. Insp. Fact., 31st Oct., 1863,” pp. 108,109.

<!-- ch15-p125 -->
> “The reduction of hands against increased production is, in fact, constantly taking place, in woollen mills the reduction commenced some time since, and is continuing; a few days since, the master of a school in the neighbourhood of Rochdale said to me, that the great falling off in the girls’ school is not only caused by the distress, but by the changes of machinery in the woollen mills, in consequence of which a reduction of 70 short-timers had taken place.”[^126]

[^126]: Cited above, p. 109. During the crisis, machinery improved rapidly. This allowed the English manufacturers, immediately after the end of the American Civil War, and almost in no time, to glut the markets of the world again. During the last six months of 1866, cloth was almost unsaleable. Then began the shipping of goods on consignment to India and China, which naturally made the glut worse. At the beginning of 1867 the manufacturers turned to their usual way out of the difficulty, namely, cutting wages by 5 percent. The workers resisted, and said that the only remedy was to work short time, 4 days a week; and their theory was the correct one. After holding out for some time, the self-elected captains of industry had to make up their minds to short time, with reduced wages in some places, and without in others.

<!-- ch15-p128 -->
So between 1861 and 1868, 338 cotton factories disappeared. In other words, more productive machinery on a larger scale was concentrated in the hands of a smaller number of capitalists. The number of power-looms fell by 20,663; but since their product grew in the same period, an improved loom must have produced more than an old one. Lastly, the number of spindles rose by 1,612,541, while the number of factory workers fell by 50,505. The “temporary” misery that the cotton crisis inflicted on the workers was made worse, and turned from temporary into permanent, by the rapid and persistent progress of machinery.

<!-- ch15-p129 -->
But machinery does not only act as a competitor who beats the worker and is constantly on the point of making him superfluous. It is also a power hostile to him, and capital proclaims it as such from the rooftops and uses it as such. It is the most powerful weapon for putting down strikes, those periodic revolts of the working class against the autocracy of capital.[^127] According to Gaskell, the steam-engine was from the very first an antagonist of human power. It was an antagonist that let the capitalist trample on the growing demands of the workers, who threatened the newly born factory system with a crisis.[^128] One could write a whole history of the inventions made since 1830 for the sole purpose of supplying capital with weapons against the revolts of the working class. First among these in importance stands the self-acting mule, because it opened a new epoch in the automatic system.[^129]

Ure’s work appeared 30 years ago, when the factory system was still relatively little developed. Even so, it still expresses the spirit of the factory perfectly, not only through its open cynicism, but also through the naïveté with which it blurts out the stupid contradictions of the capitalist brain. For instance, after setting out the “doctrine” stated above, that capital, with the help of science taken into its pay, always reduces the refractory hand of labor to docility, he grows indignant because

<!-- ch15-p139 -->
> “it (physico-mechanical science) has been accused of lending itself to the rich capitalist as an instrument for harassing the poor.”

<!-- ch15-p140 -->
After preaching a long sermon to show how good the rapid development of machinery is for the working classes, he warns them that by their obstinacy and their strikes they speed up that development.

<!-- ch15-p141 -->
> “Violent revulsions of this nature,” he says, “display short-sighted man in the contemptible character of a self-tormentor.”

<!-- ch15-p142 -->
A few pages earlier he says the opposite.

<!-- ch15-p143 -->
> “Had it not been for the violent collisions and interruptions resulting from erroneous views among the factory operatives, the factory system would have been developed still more rapidly and beneficially for all concerned.” Then he exclaims again: “Fortunately for the state of society in the cotton districts of Great Britain, the improvements in machinery are gradual.” “It” (improvement in machinery) “is said to lower the rate of earnings of adults by displacing a portion of them, and thus rendering their number superabundant as compared with the demand for their labour. It certainly augments the demand for the labour of children and increases the rate of *their* wages.”

On the other hand, this same dispenser of consolation defends the low wages of children on the ground that they keep parents from sending their children into the factory at too early an age. His whole book is a defense of a working day of unlimited length. That Parliament should forbid children of 13 years to be exhausted by working 12 hours a day reminds his liberal soul of the darkest days of the Middle Ages. This does not stop him from calling on the factory workers to thank Providence, who by means of machinery has given them the leisure to think of their “immortal interests.”[^131]
