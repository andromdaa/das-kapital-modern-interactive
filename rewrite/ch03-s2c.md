#### C. Coin and symbols of value

<!-- ch03-p063 -->
Money takes the shape of coin because of its function as the medium of circulation. The prices or money names of commodities represent a weight of gold in imagination. Within circulation, that gold must face those commodities in the shape of coins, or pieces of gold of a given denomination. Coining, like establishing a standard of price, is the business of the State. As coins, gold and silver wear different national uniforms at home, and take them off again in the world market. This shows the separation between the internal or national spheres of the circulation of commodities and their universal sphere.

<!-- ch03-p064 -->
So the only difference between coin and bullion (uncoined gold or silver) is one of shape, and gold can pass from one form to the other at any time.[^34] But no sooner does a coin leave the mint than it finds itself on the high road to the melting pot. During their currency, coins wear away, some more, others less. Name and substance, nominal weight and real weight, begin to separate. Coins of the same denomination come to differ in value, because they differ in weight. The weight of gold fixed as the standard of price drifts away from the weight that serves as the medium of circulation. So the gold that circulates stops being a real equivalent of the commodities whose prices it realizes. The history of coinage in the middle ages and down into the 18th century records the confusion that this cause produced again and again. Circulation has a natural tendency to turn coins into a mere semblance of what they claim to be, into a symbol of the weight of metal they are officially supposed to contain. Modern legislation recognizes this tendency: it fixes how much weight a gold coin must lose to be demonetized, or to stop being legal tender.

[^34]: It lies, of course, entirely outside my purpose to consider such details as seigniorage (the charge for coining) on minting. However, for the benefit of the romantic sycophant Adam Muller, who admires the “generous liberality” with which the English Government coins free of charge, I will cite the following opinion of Sir Dudley North: “Silver and gold, like other commodities, have their ebbings and flowings. Upon the arrival of quantities from Spain ... it is carried into the Tower, and coined. Not long after there will come a demand for bullion to be exported again. If there is none, but all happens to be in coin, what then? Melt it down again; there’s no loss in it, for the coining costs the owner nothing. Thus the nation has been abused, and made to pay for the twisting of straw for asses to eat. If the merchant were made to pay the price of the coinage, he would not have sent his silver to the Tower without consideration, and coined money would always keep a value above uncoined silver.” (North, cited above, p. 18.) North was himself one of the leading merchants in the reign of Charles II.

The currency of coins itself separates their nominal weight from their real weight. It creates a distinction between them as mere pieces of metal on the one hand, and as coins with a definite function on the other. This fact implies the hidden possibility of replacing metal coins by tokens of some other material, by symbols that serve the same purposes as coins. There are practical difficulties in coining extremely tiny quantities of gold or silver. Also, at first the less precious metal is used as a measure of value instead of the more precious one, copper instead of silver, silver instead of gold, and the less precious metal circulates as money until the more precious one dethrones it. All these facts explain the parts that silver and copper tokens have played in history as substitutes for gold coins. Silver and copper tokens take the place of gold in those regions of circulation where coins pass from hand to hand most rapidly and suffer the most wear and tear. This happens where very small sales and purchases are going on all the time. To prevent these satellites from settling permanently in the place of gold, laws expressly lay down how far people must accept them as payment instead of gold. The particular tracks followed by the different kinds of coin in currency naturally run into each other. The tokens keep company with gold, to pay fractional parts of the smallest gold coin. Gold, on the one hand, is constantly pouring into retail circulation, and on the other hand is just as constantly being thrown out again by being changed into tokens.[^35]

[^35]: “If silver never exceed what is wanted for the smaller payments it cannot be collected in sufficient quantities for the larger payments ... the use of gold in the main payments necessarily implies also its use in the retail trade: those who have gold coin offering them for small purchases, and receiving with the commodity purchased a balance of silver in return; by which means the surplus of silver that would otherwise encumber the retail dealer, is drawn off and dispersed into general circulation. But if there is as much silver as will transact the small payments independent of gold, the retail trader must then receive silver for small purchases; and it must of necessity accumulate in his hands.” (David Buchanan; “Inquiry into the Taxation and Commercial Policy of Great Britain.” Edinburgh, 1844, pp. 248, 249.)

The law fixes the weight of metal in silver and copper tokens arbitrarily. In currency they wear away even faster than gold coins. Hence their functions are totally independent of their weight, and therefore of all value. The function of gold as coin becomes completely independent of the metallic value of that gold. So things that are relatively without value, such as paper notes, can serve as coins in its place. In metal tokens this purely symbolic character is hidden to a certain extent. In paper money it stands out plainly. In fact, ce n’est que le premier pas qui coûte [it is only the first step that costs].

<!-- ch03-p067 -->
We are speaking here only of inconvertible paper money (paper money that cannot be exchanged for gold) which the State issues and gives compulsory circulation. It arises directly out of the metal currency. Money based on credit, on the other hand, presupposes conditions that are still totally unknown to us from our standpoint of the simple circulation of commodities. But we may say this much: just as true paper money arises from the function of money as the medium of circulation, so money based on credit takes root spontaneously in the function of money as the means of payment (money used to settle a debt after goods have changed hands on credit).[^36]

[^36]: The mandarin Wan-mao-in, the Chinese Chancellor of the Exchequer, one day took it into his head to put before the Son of Heaven a proposal whose secret aim was to turn the *assignats* (paper money) of the empire into convertible bank-notes. In its report of April, 1854, the assignats Committee gave him a severe snubbing. Whether he also received the traditional beating with bamboo canes is not stated. The report ends as follows: — “The Committee has carefully examined his proposal and finds that it is entirely in favour of the merchants, and that no advantage will result to the crown.” (“Arbeiten der Kaiserlich Russischen Gesandtschaft zu Peking über China.” Aus dem Russischen von Dr. K. Abel und F. A. Mecklenburg. Erster Band. Berlin, 1858, p. 47 and following.) In his evidence before the Committee of the House of Lords on the Bank Acts, a governor of the Bank of England says this about the wearing down of gold coins during currency: “Every year a fresh class of sovereigns becomes too light. The class which one year passes with full weight, loses enough by wear and tear to draw the scales next year against it.” (House of Lords’ Committee, 1848, n. 429.)

The State puts into circulation bits of paper with their various denominations printed on them, say £1, £5, etc. Insofar as they actually take the place of gold to the same amount, their movement is subject to the laws that govern the currency of money itself. A law peculiar to the circulation of paper money can arise only from the proportion in which that paper money represents gold. Such a law exists. Stated simply, it is this: the issue of paper money must not exceed the amount of gold (or silver, as the case may be) that would actually circulate if it were not replaced by symbols. Now, the quantity of gold that circulation can absorb constantly fluctuates around a given level. Still, the mass of the medium of circulation in a given country never sinks below a certain minimum, which is easily found by actual experience. This minimum mass is constantly changing in its component parts: the pieces of gold that make it up are constantly being replaced by fresh ones. But this of course causes no change either in its amount or in the continuity of its circulation. It can therefore be replaced by paper symbols. If, on the other hand, all the channels of circulation were filled today with paper money to the full extent of their capacity to absorb money, they might be overflowing tomorrow because of a fluctuation in the circulation of commodities. There would no longer be any standard. The proper limit of paper money is the amount of gold coins of the same denomination that can actually circulate. If the paper money exceeds that limit, then, apart from the danger of falling into general disrepute, it would represent only that quantity of gold which the laws of the circulation of commodities require, and which alone can be represented by paper. If the quantity of paper money issued is double what it ought to be, then, as a matter of fact, £1 would be the money name not of 1/4 of an ounce, but of 1/8 of an ounce of gold. The effect would be the same as if a change had taken place in the function of gold as a standard of price. Values that were previously expressed by the price of £1 would now be expressed by the price of £2.

Paper money is a token that represents gold or money. Its relation to the values of commodities is this: those values are ideally expressed in the same quantities of gold that the paper represents symbolically. Paper money is a symbol of value only insofar as it represents gold, which, like all other commodities, has value.[^37]

[^37]: The following passage from Fullarton shows how unclear even the best writers on money are in their understanding of its various functions: “That, as far as concerns our domestic exchanges, all the monetary functions which are usually performed by gold and silver coins, may be performed as effectually by a circulation of inconvertible notes paying no value but that factitious [artificial] and conventional value they derive from the law is a fact which admits, I conceive, of no denial. Value of this description may be made to answer all the purposes of intrinsic value, and supersede even the necessity for a standard, provided only the quantity of issues be kept under due limitation.” (Fullerton: “Regulation of Currencies,” London, 1845, p. 21.) Because the commodity that serves as money can be replaced in circulation by mere symbols of value, therefore its functions as a measure of value and a standard of price are declared to be superfluous!

Finally, someone may ask: why can gold be replaced by tokens that have no value? But, as we have already seen, it can be replaced in this way only insofar as it functions exclusively as coin, or as the medium of circulation, and as nothing else. Now, money has other functions besides this one. The isolated function of serving as the mere medium of circulation is not necessarily the only one attached to gold coin, although it is the only one for those worn coins that continue to circulate. Each piece of money is a mere coin, or medium of circulation, only so long as it actually circulates. But this is exactly the case with that minimum mass of gold which can be replaced by paper money. That mass stays constantly within the sphere of circulation, functions continually as a medium of circulation, and exists only for that purpose. So its movement represents nothing but the continued alternation of the opposite phases of the metamorphosis C–M–C. In these phases commodities face their forms of value, only to disappear again immediately. Here the independent existence of a commodity's exchange-value is a passing apparition, by means of which the commodity is immediately replaced by another commodity. Hence, in this process that keeps making money pass from hand to hand, the mere symbolic existence of money is enough. Its functional existence absorbs, so to speak, its material existence. As a passing and objective reflection of the prices of commodities, it serves only as a symbol of itself, and can therefore be replaced by a token.[^38] One thing, however, is needed: this token must have an objective social validity of its own, and the paper symbol gets this by its forced currency. This compulsory action of the State can take effect only within the inner sphere of circulation whose boundaries coincide with the territories of the community. But it is also only within that sphere that money fully performs its function as the medium of circulation, or becomes coin.

[^38]: Nicholas Barbon starts from the fact that gold and silver, so far as they are coins, or serve exclusively as the medium of circulation, become mere tokens of themselves. From this he deduces the right of Governments “to raise money,” that is, to give the weight of silver that is called a shilling the name of a greater weight, such as a crown, and so to pay creditors shillings instead of crowns. “Money does wear and grow lighter by often telling over [counting]... It is the denomination and currency of the money that men regard in bargaining, and not the quantity of silver...’Tis the public authority upon the metal that makes it money.” (N. Barbon, cited above, pp. 29, 30, 25.)
